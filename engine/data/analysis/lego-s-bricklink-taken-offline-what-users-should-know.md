LEGO's BrickLink marketplace went offline late on 5 November 2026 and stayed dark for roughly a week, returning on 12 November. The first public statements, on the BrickLink homepage and repeated to the BBC on 11 November, described only unusual activity on the platform and pointed to containing the issue, investigating how it happened and checking for related issues. LEGO did not confirm a breach or say whether customer data was affected. BrickLink's own update after restoration is narrower than the week of speculation: usernames and IP addresses for some members were accessed, along with email addresses for a very small number, while passwords, postal addresses and financial information were not. No CVE, no exploit and no named actor attach to this incident, and LEGO has not said how access was obtained or when it began.

The exposed fields sound thin until they are placed next to the size of the platform. Brick-Tracker counted about 18,750 shops in BrickLink's last public store directory, including roughly 1,800 in Germany, and LEGO claims more than a million users. A username plus an IP address is enough to build targeted credential-stuffing lists and to send convincing order or payment messages to a known buyer, and November is the busiest month of the year for parts sellers: one seller, Simon Webster of DSLS Bricks, told Brick Fanatics his revenue was down 52% against the same four days a year earlier while his BrickOwl sales rose 79%. That points at the real story, which is not the record count but the recurrence. BrickLink went dark from 3 to 8 November 2023 after a threat and ransom demand, an incident later attributed to credential stuffing and closed with forced password resets for every user. Two November takedowns in three years, with the same silence in the first week, is a pattern in how this platform is operated rather than a one-off.

The availability lesson travels further than the marketplace. Brickset, a separate set database, hotlinks minifigure images from BrickLink servers and pulls prices and availability from its API, so its own listings lost images and price data while the outage ran. Any service that treats a single commerce backend as an image or pricing dependency inherits that backend's downtime. For individuals, there is no patch to apply: reset BrickLink and LEGO account passwords if they are reused anywhere else, turn on two-factor authentication where the account offers it, and treat any payment request arriving outside BrickLink checkout as hostile. The BBC reported a collector who received several payment links for a limited edition release she had already paid for, and LEGO's position was that the payment was not taken, so payment messages that reference real BrickLink orders deserve particular suspicion. Sellers need no action on existing listings, should expect November and December seller fees to be waived, and should watch buyer messages for payment redirection. Defenders running dependent services should cache BrickLink-sourced images and prices locally and keep a fallback feed, and should review logs for credential-stuffing traffic against LEGO authentication endpoints in the days after restoration.

Watch two things. First, whether LEGO issues direct notifications that enumerate categories and a start date, or files with a regulator, since the current account rests on a support-page statement and an email to affected members that most users will never see. Second, whether the Studio Forum stays offline and whether a forced password reset follows, as it did in 2023, because that would confirm the credentials, not just usernames and IPs, were in scope.
