LEGO took the BrickLink marketplace offline after detecting unusual activity, and the outage ran roughly a week. The first reports on 11 November 2026 (BBC, later summarised on malware.news) said only that the site was down and that LEGO had not confirmed a hack or whether customer data was affected. BrickLink then confirmed a data breach on 12 November 2026, attributing it to unauthorised access, and the marketplace was restored to service around 13 November 2026, with Slashdot covering the return under the framing of a week-long outage caused by a "cyber incident."

The confirmed-breach wording matters more than the outage itself: a company that first says "unusual activity" and then concedes unauthorised access is admitting that data, not just availability, was in scope. LEGO has not published what categories of data were exposed, how many accounts are affected, or when the access began, so the notification clock and the exposed-record count are both open questions. Buyer and seller account details, saved payment instruments and order history are the obvious candidates for a marketplace of this kind, but nothing in the reporting so far enumerates them.

Watch three things. First, whether LEGO issues direct breach notifications to affected users, which would settle the data categories and the timeline; the current reporting rests on the company's own statements and does not include a regulator filing or a leaked-record sample. Second, whether credentials taken from BrickLink turn up in infostealer logs or credential-stuffing waves against LEGO accounts, which is the usual second act for a marketplace breach. Third, whether the downtime itself was used for fraud: the early guidance to watch for suspicious payment requests and to stop reusing passwords still stands, and any payment request arriving via BrickLink messaging rather than the platform's own checkout should be treated as hostile until the platform confirms otherwise.
