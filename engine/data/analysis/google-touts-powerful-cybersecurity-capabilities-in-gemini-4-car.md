Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, a frontier AI model the company says outperforms rivals on benchmarks for detecting, exploiting, and patching vulnerabilities. Carbon scored 88.2 percent on SEC-bench Pro, which tests flaw discovery in real-world software, 54.8 percent on the ExploitGym exploitation benchmark, and 71.0 percent on Collinear's CWE-bench patching benchmark, topping OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra and Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 and Claude Fable 5.1 in Google's comparison. Those results were obtained with no guardrails in place. The model is not broadly available: this week Google is opening access to a select group of cyber defenders, then expanding in stages to approved partners through its Fairwind Program, which offers governments, critical infrastructure operators, and major technology platforms early access to advanced models for cyber defense and counts more than 650 participating organizations. Carbon follows Gemini 4 Argon, launched two weeks earlier, which some Fairwind partners already access.

The defensive framing is the notable element. Google describes exploitation capability as a defensive step that helps teams decide what to fix, and Carbon follows Argon in a sequence of models Google is positioning for vulnerability work. The benchmark scores, however, are company-reported and unverified by independent evaluation, and the no-guardrails condition means the results may not reflect how the model behaves under the safety controls that would apply in production.

What to watch: whether independent researchers reproduce the benchmark claims and whether Fairwind access terms and safety controls are published. Organizations should treat the announcement as a capability signal rather than a procurement-ready product, and track how defenders and attackers each adopt these models.
