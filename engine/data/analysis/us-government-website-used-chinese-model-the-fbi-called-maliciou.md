The Federal Register website, run by the National Archives, briefly integrated Alibaba's Qwen3 0.6B open-weight model as a search tool for public comments on proposed regulations. Social media users noticed the deployment on September 15, and the model was removed by September 17 after media coverage. The irony is sharp: the FBI earlier in September named Alibaba among six Chinese firms accused of "industrial-scale distillation" of US frontier AI models, calling the practice a national security concern. A US government agency was literally running Chinese AI on its public-facing site while federal law enforcement was denouncing the same vendor's models as "malicious."

The actual risk was limited. The Qwen3 0.6B model is open-source, runs locally without sending data to Alibaba, and the Federal Register's content is already public. Security experts told Reuters the deployment posed no substantial data risk. But the policy optics are terrible. Representative John Moolenaar, chair of the House China Committee, said "no federal government entity should use a Chinese AI model" and called it contrary to national interest. The incident exposes a gap between the administration's hard line on Chinese AI and the reality that open-weight Chinese models have become a default for cheap, controllable AI search tools, even inside the government.

Watch for whether this triggers executive action restricting Chinese model use across federal agencies, and whether the open-source AI community pushes back. The underlying tension is that small, specialized, deployable Chinese models are filling a gap the US has not yet addressed with its own open ecosystem.
