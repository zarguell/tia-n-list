1Password disclosed on November 25, 2026, that an attacker took over an employee's corporate GitHub account, used it to read private repositories and push malicious code into backend services, and made those services download administrative customer records. The investigation started November 19 after signs of the account compromise. Exposed fields included names, email addresses, account and user identifiers, creation and last sign-in times, public keys, SRP parameters, non-secret internal metadata, and tokens embedded in emailed invitation, provisioning, and recovery links. There is no CVE identifier or affected product version in the disclosure: this is a code-push and identity incident, not a patchable software flaw. The company says the attacker was contained, the GitHub account was disabled, the stolen token was revoked, and no customer vaults or vault keys were reached.

The material risk sits at the seam between source control, build infrastructure, and account administration, not in the encrypted vault. One developer identity reached private source, CI/CD with infrastructure secrets, and a backend path that could pull customer records on demand; that is the boundary password manager users assume is isolated from their secrets. Two populations need to be kept separate: a subset of customers had account records copied, and a smaller number in invitation, provisioning, or recovery state had emailed tokens stolen. 1Password published no count and says affected users were notified individually. It also says a token alone cannot open an account and that it found no internal evidence of takeover using those tokens, yet its emergency change from six numeric digits to ten alphanumeric characters is the clearest signal that the remaining second factor was not considered strong enough to leave in place. Names, emails, sign-in times, and account recovery lures make phishing the likely second act.

There is no hotfix version to install, so defender work is identity and telemetry work. Rotate infrastructure credentials that could have been present in CI/CD, disable the affected GitHub identity, revoke the stolen token, remove attacker-created access, and tighten GitHub, CI/CD, and endpoint controls with detection for malicious pushes into backend services. Hunt through GitHub audit history for pushes by that employee identity and changes to backend service repositories around November 19, through CI/CD secret-access records for infrastructure credential use, and through mail logs for provisioning invitations and recovery links issued in that window. Treat unsolicited 1Password invitation or recovery messages as phishing and verify them in the app or by typing the address. The advisory names no IoCs, hashes, or log paths, so anchor the hunt to the identity, the repositories, and those message flows rather than to invented indicators.

Reassess upward if 1Password confirms that CI/CD secrets were used outside its environment or that a stolen provisioning token was combined with a second factor to take over an account, since that would turn a metadata disclosure into account compromise. Reassess downward if the company publishes the affected count, confirms the token change was in place before any stolen link could be used, and shows the ten-character codes were deployed across all invitation and recovery flows.
