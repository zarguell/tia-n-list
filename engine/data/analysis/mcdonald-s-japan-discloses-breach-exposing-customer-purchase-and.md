McDonald's Japan disclosed on November 20, 2026 that an unauthorized third party accessed part of the database behind its official mobile app. The exposed fields are product purchase histories, internal customer-management IDs, IP addresses and location from the devices used when customers received service. Names, email addresses, phone numbers, home addresses and card data were not in scope, and the company says the internal IDs cannot identify a person on their own. No CVE, actor, intrusion date or victim count has been published; the authentication credentials used in the access are being invalidated.

The credential trail is the real injury, not the absence of a name field. Purchase timing paired with device location and an IP address can support re-identification when joined with other data, so the company's caveat applies to one identifier rather than to the combined records. With no count and no exfiltration finding, the practical exposed population is the app's user base until a scope figure appears, and there is no patch or consumer-side fix to close it. That unresolved credential compromise is why this notice merits desk space ahead of the day's patched CVEs: The Record carried it on November 24, four days after the company notice, and two more outlets followed.

Revoke and rotate the app-side authentication credentials tied to the intrusion, then review access to the affected database using the four published fields: customer-management ID, IP address, purchase history and device-location records. Ask the vendor for the intrusion window and any affected-account list before sizing notifications; no log paths, ports, IoCs or actor names were published, so a credential sweep is the only hunt available today. User-facing guidance should cover app location permission, checking order history for unfamiliar orders, avoiding links in messages that claim to be McDonald's and keeping app credentials distinct from other services.

Watch for a published victim count or an intrusion timeline, and for any account misuse detected after the credential invalidation. Either would convert an unscoped access notice into a measurable incident with a definable response. Continued silence on exfiltration should keep the assessment at potential exposure rather than confirmed theft.
