The State Department's Rewards for Justice program is offering up to $10 million for information on Zhang Yu, the Chinese national charged in a nine-count indictment, unsealed in July 2025, with stealing American COVID-19 research and taking part in the Hafnium campaign against Microsoft Exchange servers. RFJ names Zhang a director of Shanghai Firetech Information Science and Technology working for the Shanghai State Security Bureau under the Ministry of State Security. His codefendant Xu Zewei, then general manager at Shanghai Powerock Network, was arrested in Milan on July 3, 2025, extradited in April 2026 and has appeared in federal court in Houston; Zhang remains at large. The indictment spans February 2020 through June 2021, from university vaccine research to the Exchange intrusions Microsoft disclosed in March 2021 and now tracks as Silk Typhoon.

The reward adds no new intrusion; it converts a fifteen-month-old indictment into an active manhunt and signals the government still lacks location intelligence on Zhang. The part the event list understates is structural: both indicted men ran commercial firms, Firetech and Powerock, so Hafnium was contractor labor purchased by SSSB rather than a uniformed unit, and SentinelLabs' July 2025 review of Firetech's patent filings showed that payroll extending into surveillance tooling with no Hafnium connection. That framing matters for exposure math. Thousands of computers worldwide were compromised, victims included a US university that lost vaccine research and a US law firm, and a contractor model means the same hired hands can be re-tasked against health research or legal targets without a new state directive ever becoming visible.

Defenders should confirm every remaining Exchange server carries the patch cycle Microsoft shipped in March 2021 for Hafnium, since unpatched survivors of that window are exactly the population this indictment describes. Sweep 2020 to 2021 era email and perimeter telemetry for activity inside the indicted February 2020 to June 2021 window, and treat Shanghai Firetech and Shanghai Powerock as attributed contractor entities worth tagging in threat-intel platforms and watching for in supply and procurement chains. Organizations doing health research or legal work fit the documented victim profile and should weight those hunts accordingly. Anyone with actionable information on Zhang's location or associates can route it through Rewards for Justice, which is the entire point of the bounty.

Two developments would change the assessment. Treasury sanctions or further designations against Shanghai Firetech and affiliated contractors would add names and infrastructure to a map currently drawn from two indictments and a patent review. Xu Zewei's Houston proceedings matter just as much: trial evidence could publish tradecraft not yet public, and a conviction would set the prosecutorial template for pursuing Zhang. Beijing's reaction is the political tell, since the Foreign Ministry already criticized Italy over the April extradition, and prior RFJ bounties on MSS and PLA personnel have drawn reciprocal measures.
