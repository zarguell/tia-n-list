Maileva, the dematerialized-mail subsidiary of Docaposte inside the La Poste group, confirmed on 14 November 2026 that an intrusion in mid-September exposed the first names, last names and postal addresses of 22 million people. The exposed population is not a random user base: Maileva prints and mails payslips, invoices and registered letters for more than 70,000 organizations and handled 180 million documents in 2024, including work for social landlords and hospitals. Hopital Foch told patients that reminders mailed through Kiwee and printed by Maileva between 9 and 23 September exposed invoice numbers, care dates and amounts due, revealing that the recipient had been treated. No actor has been named and no exploit has been published, and Maileva has said only that it corrected a flaw.

The 22 million figure understates the problem because Maileva is a chokepoint, not a database. A single compromise of the print-and-mail layer hands an attacker the ability to send a letter that looks like it came from a hospital, a landlord or an employer, over a channel recipients already trust. That is why the exposed fields matter more than their number: an invoice number, a care date and an amount due are enough to build a credible payment demand, and the Hopital Foch notice already warns of exactly that. The incident was also visible long before the headline. Docaposte pulled the platform for six days from 23 to 29 September after unauthorized access attempts aimed at sending fraudulent mail, a fact reported on 30 September and only reframed as a 22 million person breach seven weeks later. The gap between the September outage and the November confirmation is the real story: victims spent those weeks unwarned while the letter channel stayed compromised enough to matter.

Defenders should treat Maileva and Kiwee as a supply-chain exposure, not an email problem. Any organization that uses Maileva for print and mail should audit outbound and inbound registered letters from 9 to 23 September 2026, check Maileva and Docaposte portal and API accounts for unauthorized changes, and require out-of-band verification of any bank-detail or payment change that arrived by post. Maileva has patched the flaw and notified the CNIL, and the Paris prosecutor's cybercrime unit has opened an investigation for unauthorized access to an automated data processing system. Individuals named in the breach should verify payment demands referencing Hopital Foch, their bailleur or their mutuelle through official channels, and cybermalveillance.gouv.fr is the state referral point.

Two developments would move the assessment: a CNIL finding on the exact data categories and the true victim count, and any attribution or exploit detail that explains how the flaw was reached. A ransom claim or a named crew would also change the picture, as would confirmation that other Docaposte clients or La Poste group entities were touched.
