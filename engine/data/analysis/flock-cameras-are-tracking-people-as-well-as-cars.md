A joint investigation by 404 Media and WIRED, based on data recovered from a physically removed Flock Safety camera, found that the device's on-device software detects people in addition to vehicles, bicycles, and license plates. A group of hackers reportedly removed a camera from a roadway, copied its storage, and recovered an encryption key stored on the device, unlocking videos of thousands of detections despite Flock's claim of on-device encryption protection.

The recovered camera files contained software models for person detection, contradicting Flock's public positioning as an automated license plate reader network built solely for vehicle tracking. Separately, Washington DC's police union disclosed that the Metropolitan Police Department used Flock data to track officers under Internal Affairs investigation without their knowledge.

This matters because Flock Safety's cameras are deployed across hundreds of US law enforcement agencies. The technical finding that person-detection models ship on devices marketed as vehicle-only surveillance tools raises questions about scope creep in public camera networks. The physical extraction of data also demonstrates that on-device encryption claims do not necessarily protect against local access attacks. The privacy implications extend beyond law enforcement use cases to any deployment where Flock cameras operate near public spaces.
