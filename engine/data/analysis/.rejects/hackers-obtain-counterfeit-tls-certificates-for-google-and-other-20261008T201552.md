Attackers compromised the registry operators behind three country-code top-level domains, Ghana's .gh, Sierra Leone's .sl and American Samoa's .as, and modified authoritative DNS records. With control of DNS they could pass the domain-verification checks certificate authorities rely on, typically proving control through a TXT record, and walk away with valid TLS certificates for domains they did not own. Google disclosed that unauthorized certificates were obtained for several of its own domains and for other organizations' domains, with CT log review continuing.

The certificates enable convincing impersonation: an attacker holding a trusted cert for a domain can serve arbitrary content as that brand and, combined with DNS redirection, intercept traffic meant for the real service. Google stresses the incident did not involve a compromise of Google's systems and says there is no indication the issuing CAs acted improperly. It blocked the unauthorized certificates for its properties in Chrome via CRLSets and worked with the CAs to revoke them.

Two aspects make this broader than a Google problem. First, ccTLDs are used by sites worldwide, not only in the home country, so exposure extends to any user of domains under those registries. Second, the attack targets the registry layer itself, above individual registrars, which no amount of customer-side hygiene fixes.

Watch for additional ccTLD registry compromises disclosed by other vendors, whether the revoked certificates were observed being used in the wild, and what remediation the affected registry operators apply, since DNS-level control is the credential that matters most here.
