Denmark's official account of the Central Person Register breach, published by the CPR administration on 5 October 2026, says unauthorized parties abused a Danish company's lawful search access to the system and reached names, addresses, and CPR numbers for about 8.8 million registered citizens, roughly 80 percent of the 11 million records BleepingComputer counts in the register. The ministry release puts first detection on the evening of Friday 2 October 2026, three days before the public advisory, by which point the company's access had been revoked, the Datatilsynet had been notified, and a police investigation was underway. The 10 October follow-up via Ritzau named the firm as Pays, a Funen-based IT company, and supplied the operational detail that matters most: at least three Pays accounts, one of them an administrator account, were protected by the password `123456`. There is no CVE here because there is nothing to patch, only a credential every password-guessing list tries first, sitting on population-scale read access.

The lesson generalizes past one sloppy supplier. The state register itself was not breached; a commercial entitlement to query it was, which means every company holding CPR lookup rights is a standing doorway to the entire registered population, and the controls that matter are credential hygiene and query scoping on those integrations. Administrator-level access implies bulk-query capability rather than record-by-record browsing, consistent with the 8.8 million scale, and CPR numbers are lifetime identifiers that Denmark has historically resisted rotating, so victims cannot reissue their way out of this loss. As the malware.news assessment of 6 October argues, the set enriches and validates data from other leaks, feeding targeted phishing, impersonation, and identity fraud for years. The story also exposes a disclosure gap: the password detail reached the public through Ritzau reporting five days after the official advisory, not through the official account of the incident.

There is no hotfix, so the defender work is entitlement governance and hunting. Organizations with CPR lookup integrations should inventory every account on them, purge weak and shared passwords, enforce MFA, and challenge whether administrative roles need population-wide query scopes at all. The concrete hunt uses the dates the ministry release provides: pull CPR-system access and query logs covering 2 to 5 October 2026 and look for off-hours sessions, administrator logins from unfamiliar machines, and bulk-search patterns rather than single-record lookups. The advisory's one carve-out helps with scoping: individuals registered with name and address protection, navne- og adressebeskyttelse, were not part of the exposed set. Danish-facing support and fraud teams should treat callers who can recite a name, address, and CPR number as lower-trust than before, since that combination is now abundant.

Two developments would move this assessment. A Datatilsynet decision on Pays would determine whether CPR lookup entitlements face audited credential requirements and could reopen the number-rotation debate Danish authorities have long resisted. And independent evidence that the 8.8 million records actually moved, whether a criminal market listing or a confirmed fraud uptick, would convert an access incident into confirmed wholesale exfiltration, which the 10 October headlines assert but no authority has yet confirmed.
