Denmark's Central Person Register (CPR) breach keeps getting worse on disclosure. Ritzau reporting via DataBreaches.net says at least three user accounts at Pays, the Funen-based IT company at the center of the incident, used the password "123456" when hackers gained access, including an administrator account. Other reporting puts the haul at records on roughly 8 million citizens, including names, addresses, and additional personal data, taken from the government database that registers virtually everyone living in Denmark.

Why it matters: the attack path was not an exotic exploit but basic credential hygiene, and administrator-level access means the intruders likely had broad read reach into the register. CPR numbers are the backbone of Danish identity verification, so a mass dataset of this kind feeds identity fraud, targeted phishing, and follow-on crimes for years. The disclosure pattern also matters: headline facts such as the trivial password emerged through media reporting rather than a complete official account.

Watch for the Danish Data Protection Authority and the responsible ministry to publish findings on how weak default credentials persisted in a state-adjacent supplier, for a confirmed record count replacing the current estimates, and for any enforcement against Pays. Any observed fraud uptick tied to CPR numbers would raise pressure for a national response on number rotation, which Danish authorities have historically resisted.
