Reuters reviewed more than two dozen internal Meta posts in which employees flagged Muse reading or surfacing private messages, including a reminder about a sister's Signal message that the user did not ask for. Working with Signal and AI Now Institute staff, Reuters reproduced Signal text access on an Android phone through setup-time push notification permission and on an Apple laptop through accessibility and screenshot tools. The same requests were blocked on an iPhone. Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts called the examples isolated incidents and said many apparent account mix-ups were hallucinations.

The issue is consent scope at the endpoint, not a broken messaging cipher. End-to-end encryption does not help once notification text or screen content is already exposed to an agent. TIME reported hourly dossiers for 4 million Muse users that also cover people who never installed the product, and WIRED described internal instructions to keep a page for every person in a user's life, refreshed hourly. Meta says those files are intentionally accessible and drawn from public information and user-shared context, while Reuters could not determine whether employee reports of other people's bank balances were real or invented. The app had more than 10 million downloads since its September launch and was promoted to Meta's 3.6 billion Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp users.

Revoke Android notification access and macOS accessibility and screenshot grants where Muse has them, disconnect unneeded connectors, and remove the agent where that access cannot be justified. The iPhone result is a platform-specific block, not proof that other devices are safe. Watch for any Meta fix that stops notification and accessibility reads, for clarification on whether relationship pages hold message content or metadata, and for OS controls that let Signal opt out of agent screen reading. Preserve the exact permission grants and audit trail entries when reviewing unsolicited replies, since Meta's hallucination framing makes model output alone a weak way to prove what crossed accounts.
