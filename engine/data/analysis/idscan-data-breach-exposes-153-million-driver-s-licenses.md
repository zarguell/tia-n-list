This is not a software vulnerability with a patch, it is the theft of an identity verification vendor's archive while it was still growing. IDScan.net, the New Orleans company whose scanners read licenses at rental counters, dispensaries, hotels, banks and gun shops, confirmed in a September 4 notice that an unauthorized third party may have copied customer data from accounts on the IDScan.net cloud, naming full names and driver's license or government ID numbers. The exposure surfaced on August 31, when a service calling itself Nexus opened for business on the Exploit forum selling scans of more than 153 million US and Canadian driver's licenses, 10 million ID cards, 3 million travel documents and 579,000 medical cards, and KrebsOnSecurity traced records to IDScan through scan timestamps. Two numbers frame the damage: IDScan's follow-up reporting puts confirmed stolen licenses at 13 to 15 million, while the Nexus catalog advertised 153 million and was still adding about 400,000 records per day when first examined.

The mechanism matters more than the count. These are not database rows but authenticated document images: Nexus records carry six image files per license, including infrared and ultraviolet captures taken precisely to prove a document is genuine, which Ars Technica noted could let a clone pass hologram checks. Unlike passwords, identity documents cannot be rotated; a full name, license number and front-and-back images survive every credential reset and are enough to beat many onboarding and account-recovery checks. The blast radius is effectively anyone who has handed an ID to a scanning counter, since IDScan claims over 21 million verifications monthly at more than 20,000 locations, and Krebs matched victims' records to Hertz rentals and Planet13 dispensary visits down to the same seconds. Nexus went dark within hours of exposure, but that only closed the storefront; BleepingComputer reports multiple actors claiming to resell the same corpus, so takedown is not remediation.

There is nothing to patch, so the work is hygiene and hunting. Organizations that are IDScan or VeriScan customers should audit IDScan.net cloud tenant access logs for unauthorized reads back through August 2025, since the sellers claimed over a year of continuous exfiltration, and rotate tenant credentials and API keys. Onboarding and KYC teams should stop treating a clean license image as proof of identity and add face match, liveness and out-of-band checks for accounts opened with US or Canadian license data. If a resale dump surfaces, it is fingerprintable: six-image sets with GMT-timestamped filenames and source tags such as CDL and CAC mark this corpus. Individuals can use IDScan's free credit monitoring and identity protection offer, hotline 1-833-516-2980, or place their own credit freezes, and businesses whose counters fed scans into IDScan should assume their customers' images are included and prepare their own notification obligations.

Two developments would change the assessment. A verified resale of the full 153 million catalog, or a revision of the confirmed 13 to 15 million figure upward, would turn a serious vendor breach into standing input for identity fraud at national scale. The FBI's New Orleans investigation and the Louisiana lawsuits will also settle whether retention was authorized at all: the notice says full access to the exposed information required payment, while Caesars has said it stopped using VeriScan in February 2025 and never authorized IDScan to keep its data, and the answer determines how much of this corpus should never have existed.
