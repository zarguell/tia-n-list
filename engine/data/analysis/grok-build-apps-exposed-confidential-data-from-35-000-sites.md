Claroc Research published on 10 November 2026 what it calls GhostShares 2: apps built with SpaceXSI's Grok Build vibe-coding agent were reachable by anyone who knew the hostname. The team resolved 43,601 names under grok.me by subdomain discovery, opened the first page of 43,551 from the public internet while logged into nothing, found 37,571 still live and 35,578 of those, 94.7 percent, loading for anonymous visitors. Only 1,993 returned HTTP 401. The cause was a publish-panel default of "Anyone with the link" combined with an application-generated sign-in check that trusted the visitor, so a random unauthenticated Google account could read, edit and delete records in a test app.

The exposed material was not limited to games, landing pages and calculators, which made up most of the set. Roughly one in ten open apps carried internal or personal data: a confidential onboarding plan, a sales territory map of 594 strategic accounts, eight X advertising proposals including one to a U.S. federal agency marked confidential, four live advertiser account boards, plus medical exam results and cruise-line galley checklists reported via Ars Technica. Twenty-four apps were built by X and SpaceXSI staff themselves; SpaceXSI removed those after the report but left other users' apps alone. Identifiable internal data from 30 other organizations was present, 26 of them with more than 1,000 employees.

This is the second pass of the same research program. The earlier GhostShares report covered Meta's Muse assistant, whose share URLs were named after conversation titles and surfaced through passive DNS; Meta suspended that feature on 20 October 2026 after Ars Technica asked about it. Claroc's LinkDrift dataset made the same point for ordinary corporate share links: 326 organizations, about 800 accessible links at Fortune 500 companies, and one folder holding 8,496 files and 749.1 GB. An unguessable URL used as an access control keeps working for whoever obtains it, and these URLs leak through DNS indexes, malware logs and crawlers.

Watch whether SpaceXSI and xAI move beyond deleting the 24 staff apps to notifying the 30 affected organizations, and whether any regulator treats the medical and federal-agency material as a reportable disclosure. The generalizable risk is that agentic app builders publish to a real hostname on a shared domain, so the security boundary is one platform default that few customers audit.
