Grok Build, SpaceXSI's vibe-coding agent, published generated apps under grok.me subdomains that defaulted to "Anyone with the link" access. Claroc Research (Asaf Aprozper) enumerated 43,551 grok.me names, found 37,571 apps live, and opened 35,578 of them (94.7 percent) with no sign-in while only 1,993 returned HTTP 401. Roughly one in ten of the open apps held internal business or personal data, including 24 apps built by staff at X and SpaceXSI, a confidential onboarding plan, a sales territory map covering 594 accounts, medical exam results, cruise galley checklists, and X advertising proposals marked confidential for a U.S. federal agency.

The exposure came from a design choice rather than an exploit: Grok Build handed each project its own hostname and preselected link sharing, so passive DNS and subdomain discovery turned unguessable URLs into a public index. Claroc's GhostShares lineage (the earlier Meta Muse report) shows the same pattern recurring across AI assistants that publish shared content by URL. SpaceXSI removed its 24 staff apps after notification but left other users' apps online, leaving an estimated 650 to 800 apps with private data still exposed.

Defenders should treat AI app builders as shadow IT: enumerate employee-deployed grok.me and similar hostnames, revoke any embedded provider keys, payment keys, and database passwords, and assume anything published by default is public. Expect copycat enumeration against other platforms that use hostname-per-app publishing, since the technique requires no authentication and no vulnerability.
