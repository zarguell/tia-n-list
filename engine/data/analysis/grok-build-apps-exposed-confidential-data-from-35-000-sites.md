Claroc Research published GhostShares 2 on November 10, 2026: of 43,601 grok.me hostnames it discovered, 43,551 addresses were tried once from the public internet with no account, 37,571 were still online, and 35,578 of those (94.7 percent) opened for anyone. Only 1,993 returned HTTP 401. Among the open apps were 24 built by SpaceXSI and X staff, including a sales territory map covering 594 accounts, eight advertising proposals with budget tiers (one to a U.S. federal agency marked confidential), and a confidential onboarding plan listing internal system addresses and 19 chat channels. Beyond SpaceXSI, Claroc identified internal data from 30 organizations, 26 with more than 1,000 employees, six in the Fortune 500. Grok Build's publish panel offered Anyone with the link preselected; a team app generated sign-in logic that added the signing-in party to the team before checking membership, so an uninvited Google account could read, change and delete records.

The exposure matters beyond the 35,578 open pages. A published Grok Build app gets its own hostname, passive DNS collects the lookups, and the name is searchable by pattern; unguessable four-word names protect nothing once the dataset lists them. Claroc estimates roughly 650 to 800 of the apps held private data, including a hospital group's searchable roster of more than 3,500 employees, an ICU briefing with labs and ventilator status, a timesheet whose downloaded script carried 18 plaintext accounts, and a trucking firm's list of more than a thousand drivers with personal email addresses. Claroc sent four fixes to SpaceXSI, which removed the 24 staff apps after the report and said nothing about reaching other app owners, so Claroc began notifying organizations itself and published without the hostnames. The Grok Build post cites the same team's GhostShares finding on Meta's Muse from October 18, 2026, where passive DNS exposed shared chats until Meta suspended the feature after Ars Technica asked about it; Claroc's LinkDrift report of October 5, 2026 already showed about 800 accessible links across Fortune 500 organizations still opening material with no login. Treating a share link as access control is the pattern, and Grok Build made the permissive setting the one that needed no thought.

Defenders should enumerate grok.me hostnames in passive DNS and open each one in a signed-out session; apps holding anything internal should show Invited users or Only you and return an error to a stranger. Check the access setting, test sign-in from an external account, and search the script files an app loads for names, emails and passwords, since every visitor's browser downloads them. Unpublishing takes a grok.me app offline immediately. The same enumeration applies to Lovable, Replit, v0, Bolt, Base44 and Google AI Studio, none of which require an admin rollout or company account, and to any AI builder that publishes to a per-app hostname.

Watch whether SpaceXSI makes Only you the default for all accounts, not only employee accounts, adds an organization-domain option or moves shared apps to a path under one host so the link never becomes a DNS name. Watch also whether other owners of the 35,578 open apps take their apps down, and whether enterprises start inventorying AI-built apps the way they inventory SaaS: one enterprise Claroc spoke to counted 500 AI-built apps, each changed by the next prompt.
