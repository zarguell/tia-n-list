The Spring Ring campaign, active between January and April 2026, targeted more than 150 employees across at least 10 organizations by creating external Microsoft Teams accounts that impersonated corporate IT help desks. Attackers used names like "Help Desk," "IT Assistance," and "Network Support" and set up attacker-controlled .onmicrosoft.com tenants to make the accounts appear legitimate. After initiating a Teams chat, they called victims and posed as IT technicians, using live voice conversations to build trust and walk employees through attacker-directed actions.

In the more sophisticated variant, attackers convinced victims to launch legitimate remote-support software like Quick Assist or third-party RMM tools, then performed reconnaissance with commands including whoami /groups and net group /dom. They used PowerShell to download an obfuscated remote access Trojan from the domain san-sid[.]c. In a separate variant, attackers used NTLM relay techniques to capture credential hashes after gaining initial remote access, providing an additional path to persistence without deploying custom malware.

The campaign illustrates a shift from email-based phishing into collaboration platforms where users have a higher baseline of trust. Teams calls and chats bypass traditional email security gateways entirely. Organizations should audit external Teams communication policies and consider restricting or flagging external Teams messages from new contacts. The 10-to-15-minute call durations suggest attackers were patient and persuasive, making employee awareness training on voice-based social engineering a critical control. The use of legitimate RMM tools as the initial access mechanism means endpoint detection needs to account for authorized software being abused in unexpected contexts.
