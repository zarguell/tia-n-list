Phishing is social engineering, not code: it works by impersonating a trusted source to steal credentials or sensitive data, usually through email, text, voice, or QR codes. The 2026 Verizon DBIR counts phishing as the initial entry point for 16% of confirmed breaches, and roughly 62% of breaches involve some human element: a click, a credential entry, or an approved request.

Stolen credentials rarely stay contained. They are typically sold, traded, or leaked on dark web markets within days, which is how a single phishing email becomes a company-wide breach. That makes phishing less a training problem and more a credential-governance problem.

The practical posture is unchanged but worth restating: MFA on everything reachable, especially anything internet-facing, fast credential revocation, and detection tuned to impersonation patterns (lookalike domains, brand abuse, callback lures) rather than the payload. QR code and voice phishing are the growth areas to watch this year.
