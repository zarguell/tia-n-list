On October 29, 2026, Binance disclosed an attack affecting approximately $6.34 billion in crypto assets, which the FBI has attributed to the Democratic People's Republic of Korea under the activity name TraderTraitor, part of the Lazarus grouping. Attackers moved from compromised engineering workstations to the systems that sign wallet transactions, relying on legitimate credentials and permissions for lateral movement. As of November 3, about $2.85 billion had been frozen or intercepted.

Binance is not alone. Since September, at least three cryptocurrency companies have been hit with a similar pattern: BitGet lost $388 million after attackers exploited a vulnerability in a third-party security product, and a third company followed the same playbook with a different initial-access vector. In the Binance case, the malware arrived through a fake job interview, a known Lazarus trick: a candidate receives an interview-themed file that executes malicious code on the engineering workstation. The reporting also notes that 44 percent of compromised machines held more than ten secrets, so developer environments are the critical exposure and credential hygiene is what limits blast radius.

The recurring theme is trust abuse: valid credentials, signed update channels, and familiar pretexts such as job interviews. Defenders should assume engineer workstations are high-value targets, rotate and scope secrets used for production and signing systems, and watch for TraderTraitor-associated infrastructure. The Binance figures and FBI attribution are from the company's initial investigation findings published after October 29.
