Researchers at Technische Universität Berlin report that opening the case and reflashing the TPM chip resets the lockout that limits PIN guesses on AMD-based PCs and some Intel systems. With that counter gone, Windows Hello and BitLocker PINs become open to brute force. In lab tests they recovered a four-digit Hello PIN on an AMD laptop and a six-digit BitLocker PIN on an Intel laptop. A November 13 research paper, described in follow-up coverage published on November 19, reports a four-digit PIN recovered in 40 hours against a locked, encrypted system, versus a 69-day baseline for the tested scenario.

The attack requires physical access and the ability to open the case, so the events do not describe a remote path. What it undermines is the assumption that a short PIN is safe because the TPM will throttle guesses. Laptops left unattended, devices in shared offices, and systems in transit are the practical exposure. The events name no CVE, affected TPM firmware list, or vendor fix, and the demonstrated success is tied to AMD-based PCs and some Intel hardware.

Watch for vendor responses on lockout design and for guidance on PIN length where case access can clear the TPM counter. The concrete figures to track are the 40-hour recovery against the 69-day baseline and the recovered four-digit Hello and six-digit BitLocker PINs; they show how far the effective guessing budget shifts. Until vendors fix or qualify that behavior, short PINs on exposed AMD and some Intel laptops should be treated as a local attack surface rather than a hardware-backed guarantee.
