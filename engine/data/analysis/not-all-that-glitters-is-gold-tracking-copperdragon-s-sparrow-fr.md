Check Point Research's year-long investigation ties three espionage clusters to a single supplier of Chinese-nexus tooling it tracks as CopperDragon, a potential digital quartermaster behind the cross-platform Sparrow framework for Linux and Windows. SilentDelivery ran highly selective watering-hole attacks across Europe and Asia that replaced legitimate pages with a fake Chrome certificate error and delivered SquadC or a SparrowDoor variant through trojanized MSI installers named CertFixer.msi. OceanGate compromised internet-facing Linux servers in Asia and pivoted to Windows, deploying AnnobinDoor, SparrowShell, trojanized SSH components, and a SparrowDoor variant, alongside custom tools such as CopperAdu, CopperList, CopperLogger, and CopperLsa. DarkWire targeted telecommunications and government organizations across Latin America using CopperDoor, also known as SparroWocky, and CopperDNS, loaded by CopperLoader through DLL sideloading.

The exposure is broad: several dozens of high-profile organizations across Latin America, Asia, and Europe, spanning telecommunications providers, government and military bodies, research institutions, and critical infrastructure. The argument for defenders is that this is one supply-side problem rather than three separate incidents, because the same framework artifacts recur across operators and the tooling is redeployable under new hands. An organization that cleaned up a 2024 FamousSparrow intrusion may still be exposed to a 2026 SilentDelivery watering-hole or a DarkWire telecom intrusion, since the implants, loaders, and infrastructure patterns overlap rather than the operator identity. The Melofee connection extends the scope: CopperDragon is assessed to maintain and supply Melofee variants through shared Reptile rootkit code and certificate reuse, so Linux estates are in scope as well as Windows. The watering-hole chain's browser-presence check, which requires an open browser to run SquadC, also means a sample that detonates cleanly in an isolated sandbox can still succeed on a real user workstation.

Defenders should hunt for the framework rather than a single intrusion set. On Windows, review registry Run keys under WindowsUpdateAssist, unexpected local administrator accounts such as admin$ created by CopperAdu, inbound firewall rules named RasiUwpApp on TCP 445, LSASS dump artifacts renamed to look like 1.png, and tools staged under paths such as C:\Program Files\7-Zip\Lang, C:\Windows\debug, C:\ProgramData\Oracle\Java, and C:\ProgramData\Waves. On Linux, look for modified SSH client and SSHD binaries that log credentials, AnnobinDoor (internally named annobinagent) and SparrowShell loading plugins from /tmp, and modified Reptile rootkit components. Network detection should cover the SilentDelivery endpoints /image-check, /report-url, and /register-ip, CopperDNS DNS MX tunneling on high-numbered ports such as 6532 with the reused TLS certificate (SHA-256 ee4a8b81fe3864582efc27a77f806990e2602dcc36447ddda4b3a230a473d575), and CopperDoor C2 on TCP 443 using raw TCP inside TLS with certificates whose common names mimic victim domains. Because CopperLsa writes stolen credentials to an operator-chosen log file and CopperLogger captures keystrokes and clipboard data, credential rotation should follow any suspected compromise.

Watch for two developments that would change the assessment: whether CopperDoor or SquadC samples surface outside the three known clusters and regions, which would indicate a wider quartermaster customer base than the current reporting supports, and whether further vendor analysis confirms or refutes the Melofee and Salt Typhoon links, which bear on which operators can be held responsible for a given intrusion. Expansion of the SilentDelivery watering-hole sites beyond government, shipping, and IT and research sectors would also widen the exposed population beyond the selective targeting described so far.
