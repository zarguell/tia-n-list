Eight intermediate certificates issued by the Indian CA eMudhra had private keys that anyone can recover from the public certificate. The keys were P-384 elliptic curve values below 256, generated during a key generation ceremony on April 30, 2025, and one of them was the number 4. eMudhra blamed an error in a script used for that ceremony, which produced a second set of weak keys with labels similar to the correct ones, and those weak keys were mistakenly used for signing, with some of the resulting certificates also submitted to Sectigo for cross signing. Six of the eight are TLS intermediates and two sign code, so a holder could issue certificates trusted for any domain and could sign Windows binaries with a verified publisher name on systems that skip revocation checks.

The researcher credited as Wayne reported the weakness to the Chrome Root Program on October 17 at 18:05 UTC and demonstrated possession of each key by signing messages with it. Chrome and Firefox blocked the intermediates within hours, Mozilla through OneCRL and then Firefox 158.0.1 with NSS 3.131, Apple on October 19 and Microsoft on October 20, with Sectigo revoking and Cloudflare patching its own services. The Go project assigned CVE-2026-111943 and shipped go1.27.3 and go1.26.10 on October 19, adding a denylist in crypto/x509 so certificate verification fails when one of the compromised intermediates appears in a chain. Cloudflare became aware around 17:00 UTC on October 19 and CERT-In has issued an official statement on the incident.

The remaining exposure is in software that ships its own CA bundle or ignores revocation checks, and estimates put the number of certificates that can be reissued from the compromised keys at around 30,000. The eMudhra incident page now carries the practical question from defenders: macOS blocks the compromised intermediates, but the built-in curl, git and python3 on the platform can still validate against them, and there is no simple workaround short of updating the trust stores. Dennis Jackson has published a tool for checking whether a client still trusts the eight intermediates, alongside the certificates, public keys and signatures needed for independent verification.

Watch for the gap between revocation in root programs and actual trust stores in appliances, containers and private PKI deployments, for any fraudulent certificates actually issued under the compromised keys, and for follow-up on the key generation ceremony failure, since the same script error may have affected keys that have not yet surfaced.
