Federal agencies warned on November 5 that attackers are targeting electronic temperature controls at grocery stores and cold storage facilities worldwide, in a joint fact sheet from CISA, the FBI, HHS, USDA and DOT. The warning follows Nextgov/FCW reporting from October 29 that U.S. officials suspect Iran-linked hackers in freezer malfunctions at military bases beginning around late August, affecting roughly six commissaries. Recent failures have spoiled food and created fire hazards. No CVE and no specific product version is named in the reporting; the exposed surface is industrial refrigeration and cold chain monitoring systems rather than a single code flaw.

A successful attack means physical loss of temperature control: spoiled food, fire hazards, and, as seen in France on November 2, attempted temperature excursions at regional blood banks. Exposure runs across grocery stores, cold storage, military commissaries and healthcare cold chains, with the European supermarket wave hitting stores in eight countries. This story ranks above the other thirty because it pairs a named-state suspicion with physical safety incidents on U.S. bases, shifting the burden from IT forensics to facility operations and food-safety response.

Defenders should reduce public internet exposure of temperature-control systems and strengthen access control as the CISA fact sheet directs, since no patch or fixed firmware version is named. Hunt for unauthorized setpoint changes, freezer alarm spikes and remote access to temperature-control human-machine interfaces using temperature log histories and alarm records from cold-chain management platforms. Isolate refrigeration control networks from corporate and guest Wi-Fi, require multi-factor authentication on any remote management path, and confirm backup monitoring so a spoilage event triggers an alert before stock is lost.

Watch for confirmation of Iranian attribution, expansion beyond the roughly six commissaries, and any CISA KEV listing or vendor advisory naming specific refrigeration controllers. A shift to ransomware-style disruption of cold chains, or exploitation telemetry against exposed temperature-control panels, would raise the assessment from targeted suspicion to a broad active campaign.
