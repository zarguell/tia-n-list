IDC Frontier, the SoftBank Group subsidiary operating the IDCF Cloud IaaS platform, disclosed a third-party ransomware attack that began around 03:40 JST on October 7 and forced the shutdown of East Japan Region 1. The vendor's own advisory chain, published October 7 and 8 on idcf.jp, shows the case hardening in steps: the first notice reported unauthorized access, the second confirmed ransomware and set the blast radius at 495 companies and municipalities, and the third named the affected capacity zones, tesla, henry, pascal, and joule, where virtual servers stopped and would not restart. IDC Frontier has isolated the region, disabled management consoles across all regions pending security verification, engaged an external security firm, and reported the incident to its supervising ministry and the Metropolitan Police. The intrusion route is still unidentified and no group has claimed the attack.

This is a supply-side failure, not a single-victim breach: one operator's compromise took hundreds of organizations offline simultaneously, including local governments that outsourced their infrastructure to a cloud brand. The decisive detail sits in the third advisory, where IDC Frontier tells affected customers that data in the tesla, henry, pascal, and joule zones is expected to be difficult to extract or restore, and that recovery is only possible from backups the customers hold themselves. That inverts the durability assumption behind managed cloud: recovery burden shifts to the victim, and any of the 495 customers without independent, offline copies faces permanent data loss rather than downtime. With exfiltration unconfirmed and no actor claim yet, the same data the provider says it cannot restore remains a live double-extortion target. Among today's stories this one matters because it prices ransomware risk at an IaaS concentration point, where one encryption event cascades across a customer base the attacker chose once.

Customers with workloads in the named zones should follow the vendor's guidance: rebuild in a freshly prepared environment rather than restoring in place, and rehydrate only from backups held outside the affected region, checking those backups for integrity and time gaps before reconnecting them to production. Customers in the other zones, radian and newton in Region 1 plus Regions 2 and 3 and West Japan Region 1, should treat the vendor's call to take their own backups now as an instruction, since the intrusion route is unknown and no other region has been cleared as safe in a final sense. Management consoles are down provider-wide and IDC Frontier is performing virtual server start and stop operations manually on customer request, so any out-of-band contact claiming to be vendor support during this window warrants verification through a separate channel before credentials are shared. Japanese public-sector clients should preserve their own console and API logs now, while the ministries and the Metropolitan Police already notified define what follows.

Two developments would change the assessment. A leak-site claim naming IDC Frontier or carrying sample data from affected customers would convert an availability incident into a disclosure event for all 495, and the eventual disclosure of the intrusion route will show whether other regions and peer Japanese providers share the same exposure. The console restoration timeline is the practical signal to watch, since it marks when the provider itself considers its control plane trustworthy again.
