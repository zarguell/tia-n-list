ActiveCloud, a Belarusian cloud and hosting provider, reported a massive cyberattack on November 10, 2026 after its infrastructure was hit at 03:30, and confirmed that ransomware encrypted parts of its cloud infrastructure. Customers lost access to key services for days: secure cloud hosting in Belarus, email in Belarus and Russia, the corporate website, and support channels including chats, telephony, and the ticketing system inside client accounts. As of November 14 no CVE, no named group, no confirmed data exfiltration, and no ransom demand had been published, so the technical picture rests on company statements alone.

This is not an isolated outage. Beget, a Russian host serving more than 200,000 customers, was hit the night of November 1 by a wiper that reached the management layer of its entire architecture and cost every binary VPS backup. ESTT was hit by file-encrypting malware on November 8. ActiveCloud is the third regional hosting provider knocked out in nine days, which reads as a sustained push against shared infrastructure rather than coincidence, and the blast radius is the business model: a tenant's own defenses do not matter when the provider's management layer is the breach point. Beget's November 6 assessment that only about half of VPS users would recover file backups is the realistic ceiling for what ActiveCloud tenants should expect, especially since ActiveCloud's support channels went dark with everything else.

Providers should segment the management plane from tenant workloads and keep offline or immutable backups, because Beget needed roughly 1,000 servers manually checked for vulnerabilities and hidden scripts before any restore. Tenants on regional hosts should treat provider email and ticketing as untrustworthy during an incident, keep out-of-band contact paths, and hold their own exports, since server restoration is not data recovery: ESTT had 90 percent of virtual servers back by the Monday after its November 8 hit while account dashboards stayed down. With no hashes or IoCs published, hunt on behavior: mass encryption or zeroing on shared infrastructure, scripts run from a management layer, and sudden backup deletion.

Two developments would move the assessment. Confirmation of exfiltration or a ransom demand would flip this from destruction toward extortion; Beget's investigators found no evidence data was downloaded or modified, and ActiveCloud has not said either way. So would named attribution or evidence tying the three providers through shared upstream infrastructure, which would redraw the exposure map for every tenant in Belarus and Russia.
