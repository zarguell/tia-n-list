Since July 26, attackers have been reconfiguring operational technology at US water utilities, starting with more than 30 Minnesota community water systems and expanding to at least 12 states. The newest confirmations are Clayton County Water Authority in Georgia, which reported a temporary disruption and reduced water pressure restored within hours, plus Michigan, Rapid City South Dakota's wastewater lift station, Cape May and Woodbine in New Jersey, and Alabama utilities. The FBI's alert describes the tradecraft precisely: internet-exposed Rockwell MicroLogix 1100/1400 PLCs get their IP addresses and passwords changed to cause loss of view and, in some cases, function, and at least one victim found modified PLC project files with ladder-logic discrepancies across sites. CISA advisory AA26-097A, expanded on July 22, ties the profile to Iranian-affiliated actors CyberAv3ngers and Handala; the government has not formally attributed, and the underlying controller flaw is CVE-2021-22681, an authentication bypass CISA added to the KEV catalog on March 5, 2026.

The decisive fact is that the enabling defect has no patch. Rockwell's own position in ICSA-21-056-03, published February 25, 2021, is that CVE-2021-22681 cannot be mitigated with a patch; the controls it names are architectural, namely network segmentation, blocking TCP 44818 from outside the control-system zone, and CIP Security. That reframes the campaign: Censys counted 4,100+ exposed Rockwell/Allen-Bradley hosts, 4,100 Siemens, and 2,000+ Schneider hosts, and nearly half of the Rockwell devices are reachable over carrier networks including Verizon, AT&T, T-Mobile, Comcast, Charter, and Starlink. Those cellular-connected units match Plymouth's report that impact was limited to cellular-connected equipment and the Rapid City lift station pattern, which means the perimeter that matters is the carrier's, not the utility's, and enterprise-side attack-surface scans will not see the beachhead. This is why the story outranks routine CVE churn: a CVSS 9.8 flaw with no vendor fix, exploited by an actor profile with 2020 Israeli water-sector precedent, produces a permanent exposure condition that only changes when the network architecture does.

Defenders should do four things in order. Remove internet-exposed PLCs from the internet, or force all remote access through a VPN gateway with IP allowlists and changed default credentials. Audit for undocumented cellular modems installed by operators, vendors, or integrators, since CISA flagged these as the likely Minnesota entry vector and they evade conventional asset discovery. Enforce the vendor mitigations from ICSA-21-056-03: block TCP 44818 at the ICS zone boundary, deploy CIP Security on supported Logix devices (1756-EN4TR modules where controllers cannot do it natively), set controllers to Run mode, and keep known-clean MicroLogix 1400 image backups because password lockout has no in-place recovery. For hunting, enable the Controller Log feature on v17+ and Change Detection in Logix Designer v20+, review controller change logs and FactoryTalk AssetCentre for unexpected edits, diff ladder logic across sites for the cross-site discrepancies the FBI documented, and review AA26-097A indicators against firewall and VPN logs.

Two developments would change the assessment. Formal US attribution to Iran would convert a sector incident into a geopolitical one, with sanctions, possible retaliation, and a second wave of opportunistic attacks on the same exposed fleet. And any verified drinking-water contamination, which CISA says has not occurred despite the pressure-loss-and-flooding effects the FBI documented, would move this from disruption with manual workarounds to a public-health event, and would likely force emergency federal mandates on the roughly 10,000 exposed PLCs Censys quantified.
