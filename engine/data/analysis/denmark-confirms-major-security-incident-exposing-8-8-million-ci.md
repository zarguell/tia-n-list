Denmark's Central Person Register (CPR) suffered unauthorized access through a Danish firm's legitimate search access, exposing names, residential addresses, and CPR numbers for 8.8 million registered individuals. The official cpr.dk advisory, published at 07:37 UTC on 5 October 2026, confirms attackers abused legal search privileges rather than exploiting a software vulnerability, and the scope exceeds Denmark's current population because the register retains historical records for deceased and emigrated citizens. The Ministry press release (ufm.dk) notes the CPR administration became aware of irregular behavior on the evening of Friday 2 October 2026, meaning the exposure window spans at least the month of September.

This is not a routine data leak. CPR numbers function as Denmark's national identity keys, linking citizens to health, tax, banking, and government services, so their exposure creates a persistent identity-theft surface rather than a one-time credential reset. The 8.8 million figure, carried by both GBHackers and securityonline.info, covers more individuals than Denmark's present population, indicating that attackers accessed a deep historical archive rather than an active operational slice. The absence of assigned CVEs and the abuse of legitimate access mean defenders must treat this as an insider-threat and access-control failure, not a patchable software bug.

Defenders at affected organizations should audit any service that relies on CPR-based identity verification, assume the 8.8 million number represents real exposure rather than an upper-bound estimate, and review access logs for irregular search patterns matching the firm's abuse profile described by cpr.dk. The Ministry's reference to September irregular behavior suggests the compromise was sustained, not a brief intrusion, so log retention covering late September through 2 October 2026 is the relevant hunt window. Network restrictions on CPR-query endpoints and verification of third-party firm access contracts should be treated as immediate remediation rather than long-term hardening.

Watch for the Ministry's follow-up disclosure of which firm's access was misused and whether the irregular September activity correlates with specific query-volume spikes. A change in the exposure count or identification of additional data classes beyond names, addresses, and CPR numbers would alter the risk assessment, as would any confirmation that deceased-citizen records were actively queried rather than incidentally included.
