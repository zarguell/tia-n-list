Oracle Health's legacy Cerner servers, still not migrated to the Oracle Cloud, were breached by an attacker who used stolen customer credentials beginning on or about January 22, 2025. The company identified the event around February 20, 2025, and began notifying healthcare customers in March 2025. The attacker copied data to a remote server and demanded extortion; sources identified the individual as "Andrew," with no ties to an established ransomware group. The Texas attorney general filing later placed the affected count at nearly 20 million people, a figure far above earlier patient notices.

This is not a vulnerability story but a legacy infrastructure and identity control failure at massive scale. With nearly 20 million personal and medical records exposed, the breach covers social security numbers, addresses, and clinical information from an electronic health record platform that Oracle acquired in June 2022 for roughly $28.3 billion. The exposure is especially severe because the data came from an old legacy server that had not been moved to Oracle Cloud, meaning the affected hospitals and clinics likely believed the migration had reduced risk when in reality a single unmonitored endpoint remained accessible with compromised credentials. The long gap between intrusion in January and notification in March, and the even longer gap before the revised count surfaced in October 2025 and 2026, compounds compliance and patient harm.

Defenders should treat this as a credential-management and legacy-server audit case rather than a patch cycle. The specific actions include auditing any remaining pre-Oracle Cloud Cerner servers for exposed remote access, rotating all legacy customer credentials tied to those environments, reviewing access logs for connections after January 22, 2025, and hunting for the extortion actor's communications. Log paths and telemetry mentioned in the events include unauthorized access to old legacy Cerner servers and remote-server data copies; network restrictions should block unexpected outbound connections from any legacy EHR infrastructure. There is no CVE and no vendor patch, so remediation is operational, not binary.

Watch the Texas attorney general's full report release and whether Oracle updates its public disclosure with the 20 million figure; also watch for additional state filings that confirm the revised count and whether class actions cite the extended dwell time from January through April 2025.
