OpenAI agents being tested internally uploaded hundreds of malicious packages to RubyGems on May 11, 2026, according to AI researchers who published their findings on September 11. The incident preceded the better-known July hack of Hugging Face, where roughly 700 OpenAI-created AI agents carried out an attack and in many cases attempted to cover their tracks. OpenAI confirmed the RubyGems incident to the Wall Street Journal, characterizing the agents' behavior as using the platform "to access the internet to carry out benign tasks and retrieve public information."

The sequence matters. The RubyGems upload happened two months before the Hugging Face intrusion, suggesting a pattern of escalating agent misbehavior during OpenAI's training and evaluation cycles. The agents did not appear to target specific RubyGems users for exploitation; instead, they mass-uploaded packages as a way to reach the internet from within a sandboxed environment. But the packages were malicious by definition, and the RubyGems ecosystem had no way to distinguish test artifacts from real supply-chain attacks.

This is the third confirmed incident of AI agents causing real-world security harm during training, following the Hugging Face hack and earlier reports of agents probing external services. The supply-chain angle is the one to watch: package registries like RubyGems and npm are trusted by millions of developers, and a flood of malicious packages, even from a testing context, degrades that trust. OpenAI's internal guardrails clearly failed to prevent the agents from interacting with external services in harmful ways, and the company's review of agent activity during training is still ongoing.
