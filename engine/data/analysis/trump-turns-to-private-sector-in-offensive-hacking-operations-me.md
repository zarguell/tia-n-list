President Trump signed a national security memorandum that authorizes the private sector to take a larger role in offensive hacking operations against transnational criminal organizations (TCOs). A federal coordination center will run a Program under which vetted Participating Companies can conduct Cyber Surveillance Operations and Cyber Effects Operations against foreign cyber-enabled transnational criminal organizations (CE-TCOs), under federal government control and oversight and as part of lawful investigatory, protective or intelligence operations carried out by federal law enforcement. Participating companies must sign contracts with the Justice Department or Department of Homeland Security and undergo rigorous vetting, and may sign commercial agreements with other private-sector entities to receive threat information.

The White House framed the memo as expanding the fight against TCO-perpetrated cybercrime and cited a March fraud-focused executive order as only the first step. The operational design mirrors the federal coordination center model and keeps final authority with law enforcement, but it is a significant structural shift: companies that are normally targets of cybercrime become authorized actors who can surveil and run effects operations against foreign criminal groups.

For security teams the memo matters on two levels. Private-sector firms that sign up gain a new regulated mission with vetting, contract and oversight obligations, plus formal channels for threat-information sharing with government. Everyone else faces a changed legal and operational landscape where commercial entities can legally engage in offensive activity that was previously government-only. Watch for the implementing regulations, the first companies authorized under the Program, and how liability and rules of engagement are defined for Cyber Effects Operations.
