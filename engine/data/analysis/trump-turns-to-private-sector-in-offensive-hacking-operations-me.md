President Trump signed a national security presidential memorandum on Wednesday that lets vetted private US companies run offensive cyber operations against transnational criminal organizations. The National Coordination Center will run the program: program executive directors from the Department of Homeland Security and the Department of Justice get authority to greenlight company operations, unless an authorization would result in loss of life, serious injury, or rise to the level of forced or armed attack under international law. Participating companies must sign contracts with DHS or Justice, pass government vetting, and operate under federal control and oversight.

The screening standards are not fixed yet. The program executive directors, in coordination with the Homeland Security Council, have 60 days from the memo's signing to draft the standards used to vet companies. The White House framed the move as expanding the fight against cyber-enabled transnational crime and cited a March fraud-focused executive order as the first step.

Experts are split on the memo, with the split likely to resolve inside that 60-day window. Supporters call it an overdue step toward aggressive cybercrime enforcement; critics warn of legal, practical and moral hazards. Former CISA official Michael Garcia called it a massive philosophical shift in cyber policy, while consultant Davi Ottenheimer said it risks legitimizing hack-back and compared the arrangement to letters of marque. The concrete question for security teams is what the implementing standards look like: vetting rigor, rules of engagement, liability for Cyber Effects Operations, and which companies get authorized first.
