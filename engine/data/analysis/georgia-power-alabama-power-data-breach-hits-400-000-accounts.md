An unauthorized third party pulled account data out of Southern Company's online customer portal, touching roughly 400,000 customer accounts across the holding company's electric utilities: about 300,000 at Georgia Power, about 100,000 of Alabama Power's 1.6 million accounts, and an undisclosed number at Mississippi Power. The exposed fields are limited to name, mailing address, phone number, email, the last four digits of Social Security numbers or business tax IDs, and basic account details. Bank account numbers, payment card numbers, full Social Security numbers, and driver's license numbers were not in the accessed set. Southern says it stopped the activity upon detection, engaged law enforcement, and has found no evidence of ongoing unauthorized access.

This is a portal-scoped disclosure, not a core network or billing compromise, and that scope defines the risk: the exposed record is a complete pretext kit. A name, home address, phone, email, and last four of the SSN is enough to make a fake disconnection notice or a vishing call indistinguishable from the mail Southern actually sends, and utility brands are unusually high-trust lures because customers react to any threat of service loss. The exposure also concentrates hard: 300,000 affected accounts against Georgia Power's 2.8 million customer base is roughly one in ten Georgia Power customers, and 100,000 against 1.6 million is about one in sixteen Alabama Power customers. Expect the abuse window to open immediately, since lures can reference this breach and the Equifax credit-monitoring offer before mailed notices finish arriving.

Affected customers get notification by U.S. mail and email plus one year of free credit monitoring through Equifax; the utilities list 1-800-900-6021 as a dedicated assistance line, weekdays only. Defenders should treat any inbound email, call, or text citing Georgia Power, Alabama Power, Mississippi Power, or this breach as elevated, and should expect lures that mimic the real notice, including fake enrollment pages. Organizations whose portals accept utility-bill data for address or identity verification should flag affected-state accounts, since the exposed fields are exactly what those flows check. Hunt the next two weeks of mail logs for breach and compensation keywords aimed at AL, GA, and MS recipients, and watch newly registered lookalike domains that borrow georgiapower, alabamapower, or southerncompany strings.

The notices reportedly do not state when the unauthorized access occurred, and that gap is the first thing to watch: a confirmed access date, and how long it preceded detection, determines whether this was a brief window or a long dwell. Also watch for a Mississippi Power count, class-action filings consolidating in Georgia or Alabama, and any second-stage activity, such as portal account takeovers, surfacing after notifications complete.
