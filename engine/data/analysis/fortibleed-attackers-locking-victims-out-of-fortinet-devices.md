The FortiBleed campaign is not a fresh vulnerability but a sustained credential-based takeover of internet-accessible Fortinet FortiGate firewalls and SSL VPN appliances. Since June 2026, a Russian initial access broker has used previously stolen credentials and brute-force to seize poorly protected devices; SOCRadar confirms approximately 86,644 compromised devices across 194 countries, noting that breached devices from months ago remain in the actor's validated inventory. A joint FBI and US Secret Service advisory released this week warns that attackers now change passwords and delete accounts to lock organizations out of their own appliances, turning access maintenance into an explicit denial operation rather than only a harvesting stage.

What separates this from routine firewall exposure is the scale combined with the lockout mechanism. The 86,644 figure is a count of confirmed-compromised devices, not an exposure estimate, which means defenders are not looking at theoretical risk but at validated, persistent access that the actor can revoke at will. Because the broker has already built an inventory spanning 194 countries, any organization with an exposed Fortinet appliance that has not rotated all accounts since June is potentially one account deletion away from total administrative lockout. The story demands priority not because Fortinet has a new CVE, but because the adversary has shifted from silent persistence to active denial, making recovery slower than prevention.

Defenders should treat account integrity as the primary control, not just patching. Audit all local and administrative accounts on FortiGate devices for unknown usernames or modified passwords, delete any attacker-created accounts immediately, and rotate every credential that has touched SSL VPN or firewall administration since June 2026. Restrict management interfaces to authorized source networks only, disable external-facing SSL VPN where not essential, and hunt for signs of account deletion events in system logs. The events reference a joint advisory PDF from the FBI and USSS; defenders should pull that document for specific IoC names and log patterns rather than relying on generic firewall telemetry.

Watch for Fortinet releasing defensive guidance that addresses post-compromise account recovery specifically, and monitor whether the broker's inventory is sold or leased to ransomware affiliates, which would shift the threat from access denial to full network compromise.
