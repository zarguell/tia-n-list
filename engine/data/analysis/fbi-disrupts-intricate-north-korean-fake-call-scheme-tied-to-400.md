The Justice Department and FBI announced two actions on 12 November 2026 against North Korean cryptocurrency theft operations. The larger is a court-authorized seizure or restraint of more than $1.2 billion taken from Binance, part of a theft of over $6 billion in virtual assets that the FBI attributes to North Korean military hackers it tracks as TraderTraitor. A. Tysen Duva, assistant attorney general for the Criminal Division, said investigators and private virtual asset service providers froze more than $2.7 billion of the stolen funds before they moved beyond law enforcement reach, halting about half of the breach.

The second action is the disruption of FaceStealer, an operation the FBI says has netted more than $400 million. Actors publicly tracked as Sapphire Sleet, BlueNoroff, APT38 and Stardust Chollima hijack accounts on Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord and invite targets to video meetings. Links resolve to typo-squatted domains serving imposter Zoom and Microsoft Teams interfaces, and the malware is delivered disguised as a fix for an audio problem. The FBI seized the supporting cloud infrastructure along with a library of more than 900 recorded webcam videos of victims, which the IC3 flash (publication 261112) describes as the material used to impersonate targets on camera.

One detail raises the stakes beyond crypto theft: the FBI said the same actors compromised the workstation of a maintainer of widely used software and used that access to insert malicious code into a distribution channel. The intrusion was detected and stopped before it spread widely, but the attempt shows the group reaching for software supply chain positions after the money.

Watch for follow-on attribution from the private tracing firms whose data underpins the $2.7 billion freeze figure, and for whether the restrained assets survive legal challenge. For defenders, the durable lessons are that the initial access vector is a social engineering lure on messaging platforms rather than a technical exploit, and that video meetings are no longer trustworthy identity proof. Any organization whose staff install meeting-client updates outside official channels should check what was installed and from where, and software maintainers should treat their build hosts as targets for this actor set.
