On November 12, 2026 the Justice Department and FBI announced two separate actions against North Korean cryptocurrency theft, and they should not be read as one operation. FaceStealer is the social engineering campaign: actors tracked as Sapphire Sleet, BlueNoroff, APT38 and Stardust Chollima hijack accounts on Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack and Discord, invite the contacts of prior victims to video meetings, and route those meetings to typo-squatted domains serving imposter Zoom and Microsoft Teams clients built to be indistinguishable from the real interfaces. The malware arrives disguised as a fix for an audio problem. Over 18 months the campaign took more than 400 million US dollars, and the FBI seized cloud infrastructure plus a library of more than 900 webcam videos of victims. The second action concerns TraderTraitor and the theft of more than 6 billion US dollars from Binance on October 29: 2.7 billion frozen, more than 1.2 billion under court-authorized seizure, with A. Tysen Duva describing the freeze as stopping the breach before it got beyond law enforcement reach, about half of it. The FBI Flash is 261112.

The seizure of hosting is the least durable part of this. A campaign that has spent 18 months recording more than 900 crypto and Web3 workers is not primarily an infrastructure operation; it is an impersonation library, and a library outlives the servers that collected it. Each successful call adds footage of a new trusted voice, which makes the next lure cheaper to produce, and nothing in the November 12 action removes recordings already taken. That makes the affected population narrower but deeper than a generic phishing campaign: the targets are people whose job gives them access to wallets, exchange accounts or signing keys, and the entry point is a colleague's face rather than a stranger's email. The developer compromise is the one leg with reach beyond crypto. The FBI says the actor broke into the computer of a maintainer of widely used software and added malicious code, then was detected before it spread widely. That bounds the exposure but makes it contingent on a detection the bureau did not describe, and it means the downstream question is not who downloaded a package but which package, and for how long.

The money is where the arithmetic deserves scrutiny. The bureau's own figures leave more than 3 billion US dollars of the Binance theft outside the 2.7 billion freeze, and the 1.2 billion figure is an authorized seizure, not returned funds. The bureau said it expected further laundering through DeFi conversion, which is the part of the pipeline the October 31 alert could not touch: that alert published 628 attacker addresses, 608 on Ethereum, 19 on Bitcoin and one on Solana, and asked firms to block them. Blocking known addresses does nothing about funds that have already been swapped or bridged.

Defenders should treat any unexpected video invitation arriving through Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack or Discord as hostile until verified on a second channel, and should treat any microphone or audio fix installer as malware without exception. Rotate credentials and revoke active sessions for anyone who accepted such an invitation, and hunt endpoints and build systems for imposter Zoom or Microsoft Teams clients and for recently created typo-squatted conferencing domains. Virtual asset providers should screen deposits against the 628 addresses in the October 31 alert, including the 608 Ethereum and 19 Bitcoin addresses, and expect the Solana address to matter for smaller transfers. Software maintainers should rotate Git credentials, SSH keys and access tokens, and review commits from the last several months for changes that add network calls or new dependencies. Watch for three developments: the FBI naming the compromised package and stating how many downloads received the malicious code, any conversion of the 1.2 billion seizure into actual forfeiture, and evidence that the stolen funds are moving through DeFi rather than staying in the wallets the bureau listed.
