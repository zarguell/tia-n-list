Authorities disrupted "FaceStealer," an 18-month North Korean campaign that used secretly recorded videos of victims to impersonate them in fake video calls, tricking people into installing malicious software disguised as an audio fix. The scheme netted more than $400 million in stolen cryptocurrency. Hackers used the resulting access to steal funds, take over accounts and approach the victim's contacts. The FBI seized cloud infrastructure used in the campaign and more than 900 webcam videos of victims.

The operation also had a software supply-chain dimension. The FBI said hackers broke into the computer of a developer who maintains widely used software, then used that access to add malicious code that could reach people downloading it. The attack was detected and stopped before it widely spread. Separately, the Justice Department announced court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion.

Defenders should treat unexpected prompts to install software fixes during video calls as high risk and verify meeting participants out of band. Because the campaign relies on recorded video of real contacts, it is harder to detect than text-based phishing. Organizations that maintain widely used open-source or commercial software should harden developer endpoints and review release pipelines for unauthorized changes.
