Federal prosecutors in Manhattan unsealed an indictment charging Jacob Soall, Sharon Hazan, and Guy Sheetrit with wire fraud, aggravated identity theft, and money laundering for a scheme that allegedly stole about $129 million from companies worldwide. From July 2024 through February 2026, the three and co-conspirators used AI to create deepfakes of chief executive officers and attorneys at major international law firms, tricking accounting staff into authorizing large payments to criminal-controlled accounts. Victims included a UK fintech firm that lost roughly $11.6 million, a Florida pet food company that lost $5.2 million, a Missouri hospital system that lost $4.8 million, a Massachusetts mutual fund company that lost $4.6 million, a California hospital system that lost nearly $3 million, a New York investment firm that lost $2.8 million, and a Rhode Island bank that lost $2.7 million.

The scheme typically began by impersonating a CEO to accounting staff with a request for help on a financial transaction, then routing the employee to a fake attorney who would vouch for the deal. Hazan was previously convicted of conspiracy to commit bank fraud in New York in 2008. The defendants, who are from Spain, the UK, Israel, and the United Arab Emirates, were arrested between June and September and reportedly use nicknames including "Rocky Balboa," "Superman Cash," and "Fat Boy."

The case highlights that AI voice and video cloning has become a reliable tool for business email compromise at scale, and that finance teams relying on phone or video confirmation alone are exposed. Defenders should require out-of-band verification through a known contact path for any payment instruction, especially those tied to acquisitions or legal matters, and treat urgent executive requests as a trigger for secondary approval rather than speed.
