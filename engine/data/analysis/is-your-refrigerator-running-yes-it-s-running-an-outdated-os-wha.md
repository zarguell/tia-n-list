Overnight on November 2, 2026, supermarket freezers in Sweden, Belgium, Luxembourg, and Ireland climbed to around 50 degrees Celsius and stores woke to spoiled stock. This is the second wave within a month, after a similar attack hit hundreds of French stores in October. No actor has claimed either wave and the attack vector has not been confirmed for either, which is itself the headline: two coordinated, cross-border temperature-sabotage campaigns against retail cold chains with no public attribution and no published intrusion method.

The pattern matters more than the individual incidents. Refrigeration and building-management controllers are embedded systems running outdated firmware, often internet-reachable for remote maintenance, and the consequence of a compromised setpoint is physical spoilage rather than a data breach. That makes them attractive targets for disruption and extortion even without data theft, and the food-retail sector has weaker segmentation between operational technology and corporate IT than most. The Dutch reporting on the same phenomenon frames it as a cyber-physical problem: the attack is invisible until a thermometer disagrees with the controller.

Operators should segment refrigeration controllers onto dedicated networks, monitor setpoint changes and out-of-band temperature readings independently, and treat remote-maintenance access as privileged. Watch for a claimed intrusion method or actor name, which would turn this from an unsolved pattern into a known playbook, and for any link between this wave and the French cold-storage attack on the blood service, since both involve reprogrammed freezers and no public attribution.
