The supermarket refrigeration attacks are a two-wave campaign against internet-exposed refrigeration controllers. Roughly 700 French stores had units forced into defrost mode overnight October 11-12 with temperatures reaching about 50 degrees Celsius, and more than 100 stores across Belgium, Sweden, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, Estonia and others saw the same overnight November 1-2. CERT-FR alert CERTFR-2026-ALE-012, first published October 12 and updated October 13, names Danfoss AK-SM and AK-SC series plus Copeland and Dixell XWEB systems. The enabling weakness is largely documented: Claroty's Team82 disclosed in August three flaws in the Danfoss AK-SM 800A (CVE-2025-41450 authentication bypass via a daily code, CVE-2025-41451 command injection, CVE-2025-41452 Nginx configuration injection), Danfoss patched them in firmware R4.3.1, and Censys counted 2,765 exposed AK-SM 800A management interfaces. Danfoss says most compromised units are end-of-life AK-SM 800 (support ended May 2021) and AK-SC 355 (discontinued 2016) that receive no patches, and Copeland has acknowledged a vulnerability in its XWEB line without linking it to the 23 XWEB Pro flaws Claroty reported this summer.

The exposure is the story, not the spoilage. Lupovis recorded suspicious traffic against Danfoss AK-SM sensors from October 20 to 30 peaking October 26, most of it factory-default credentials that Danfoss documents publicly, and Censys still counted 138 exposed StoreView instances in Belgium, 51 in the Netherlands and 50 in Sweden on the eve of the second wave, with only 68, 37 and 58 percent respectively running patched firmware. That means a known, published, patched weakness stayed reachable for weeks and the second wave hit the same controller families, which points to patching failure and end-of-life hardware rather than a novel zero-day. The physical consequence is what separates this from a data breach: food loss measured in millions of euros, fires at two French supermarkets, and blood-storage cold rooms at the French Blood Establishment affected, with Sabcobel's CEO stating a software update alone is insufficient and the affected hardware needs physical modification. This deserves desk attention over the other thirty stories today because it is a repeatable, low-skill playbook against unowned embedded devices with a human cost, and the defender's window is defined by hardware replacement cycles, not patch Tuesday.

Operators should remove every affected controller from direct internet reachability immediately, restore access only through a VPN or protected network, and treat IP filtering as an emergency stopgap rather than a fix. For Danfoss AK-SM 800A units, apply firmware R4.3.1 and set security mode to Strict with default credentials changed; for end-of-life AK-SM 800 and AK-SC 355, isolate and plan replacement since no patches exist. For Copeland and Dixell XWEB, follow CERT-FR's guidance and the Dixell secure-use recommendations. Hunt for the second wave's signature: evaporators forced into defrost mode, defrost cycles lengthened from one hour to as many as eight, setpoint changes to around 50 degrees Celsius, and login attempts using factory-default Danfoss credentials, plus out-of-band temperature or independent process alarms that a compromised controller would hide. Check remote-maintenance access and third-party integrator credentials, since Intermarche's systems were managed by Danfoss and Reuters reported November targets used the same technology.

Watch whether investigators link the two waves or identify an actor, since nobody has claimed either and the connection is unproven, and whether Censys's exposure counts keep falling or plateau, which tells us whether operators are patching or simply disconnecting. Also watch for any spread beyond retail into cold storage, healthcare, or blood services, and for vendor guidance on whether the affected hardware can be remediated in place or must be replaced.
