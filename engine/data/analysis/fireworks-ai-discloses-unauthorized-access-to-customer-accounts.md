Fireworks AI disclosed two separate intrusions at its model hosting and fine-tuning platform, both in October 2026, and the second one reached customer data the first was supposed to have missed. The initial incident was detected on October 6 at 9:00 AM PDT: an unauthorized third party used stolen internal Fireworks credentials to read the environment variables attached to evaluation jobs in a limited number of customer accounts, which included API keys and tokens stored as secrets. Fireworks revoked internal credentials, rotated employee API keys on October 9, and revoked additional user API keys and rotated service-account tokens on October 10. The second incident arrived through a supplier. A third-party cloud provider hosting Fireworks research and development machines was compromised on October 23, and on October 24 the actor used that foothold to reach Fireworks development machines and run an unauthorized workload. Fireworks detected it the same day and contained it, stating on October 25 that it had seen no further successful unauthorized access. The expanded bulletin published November 9, written with Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, confirmed what the first bulletin had ruled out: billing information, and for a limited set of accounts secrets, datasets, and incomplete model weights. Fireworks attributes the activity to a financially motivated group tied to LLMJacking.

The reason this matters beyond the usual vendor breach notice is that the victim set is AI builders and the stolen goods are not just records. LLMJacking, documented by Google Threat Intelligence Group and Mandiant on November 2, is a monetization business: the actor sells stolen inference capacity through illicit routing gateways called LLM Mixers, and Mandiant measured fraudulent charges above 100,000 USD in a single day at some organizations with cumulative totals reaching 1 million USD before access was cut. Billing metadata, resource identifiers, and incomplete model weights are the raw material for that trade, and Fireworks customers who fine-tune on proprietary data have their partial checkpoints sitting in an internal application that the company now admits was reachable. Notion is the visible downstream case: Fireworks notified it on November 2, Notion disconnected and deleted its Fireworks data by November 3, Fireworks said on November 6 that customer data may have been accessed, the final dataset was confirmed November 8, and Notion notified customers on November 9. Fewer than 1 percent of Notion workspaces were affected and most Notion AI chat transcripts in that dataset were unattributable to a workspace or user, but unattributable is a statement about labeling, not about content. Anything pasted into Notion AI during the window is exposed whether or not the transcript names its author.

For defenders, the sharpest point is that Fireworks-side remediation does not close the real exposure. The first incident was about secrets stored in evaluation job environment variables, and Fireworks can only revoke the Fireworks copy of a credential. A third-party API key or token that was stored as a Fireworks secret must be rotated with the issuing provider, because removing the stored value leaves the underlying credential live. Affected customers should work through the accounts in their incident notice, rotate those third-party credentials at the issuer, then review access logs at that provider for unauthorized reads, downloads, or changes, and rotate again even if nothing looks wrong. Separately, audit the Fireworks account itself for unrecognized API keys, users, evaluators, training jobs, and datasets, and ask Fireworks for secret names and credential fingerprints over a secure channel. Any organization consuming inference should watch token consumption for spikes and unexpected egress, since that is the footprint of the resale path. Notion customers using Insights should assume their transcripts are in the exposed set and treat secrets pasted into Notion AI during the window as compromised.

Two developments would move this from a disclosure to an attribution and scope story. The first is naming the third-party cloud provider that hosted the Fireworks research and development machines, which determines whether other tenants of that provider are exposed to the same actor. The second is whether Fireworks customers beyond Notion come forward, particularly those who fine-tune on proprietary datasets, since incomplete model weights and datasets are the assets with the longest useful life for a buyer and the hardest to detect once transferred.
