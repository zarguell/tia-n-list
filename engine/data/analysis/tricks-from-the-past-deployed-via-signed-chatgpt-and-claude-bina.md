Check Point Research tracks the campaign as Yoba and reports it exploits fake GitHub repositories posing as desktop AI applications - GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, and Claude Opus - to deliver malware. The attackers bundle a malicious DLL, YobaDropper, alongside legitimate signed executables from OpenAI and Anthropic, relying on DLL sideloading when Setup.exe runs. The payload uses Telegram, Tor, Catbox, and DNS over HTTPS for C2, avoiding dedicated infrastructure, and Check Point notes the actor operates primarily in UTC-4 with indicators pointing to Russian origins across roughly two months of activity.

This is not a commodity phishing campaign: the use of vendor-signed hosts means trust-based endpoint controls see a legitimate binary at load time, which lowers the chances of blocking. The victim surface is anyone searching for AI desktop installers on GitHub - a growing audience - and the malware chain installs the XMRig 6.26.0 cryptominer. With no dedicated C2, network defenders must hunt for the sideloading behavior rather than a static IP, making this harder to detect without behavioral telemetry.

Patch or restrict GitHub-download AI installers and block unapproved setup executables that load unexpected DLLs such as winmm.dll. Hunt for Setup.exe launching with a nearby YobaDropper DLL, check process ancestry for signed binaries sideloading untrusted DLLs, and inspect Telegram and DoH traffic from hosts downloading AI desktop applications. Watch for updates to the fake GitHub repositories and new signed binaries reused in similar sideloading packages.

If Check Point or other researchers publish IoC lists, updated DGA patterns, or evidence of broader actor infrastructure, reassess exposure immediately. A shift from cryptomining to a different payload, or reports of in-the-wild exploitation outside AI-tool searches, would change the priority.
