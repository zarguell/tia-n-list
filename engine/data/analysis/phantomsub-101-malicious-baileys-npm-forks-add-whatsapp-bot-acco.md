OX Security researchers documented PhantomSub, a cluster of 101 malicious npm packages that are modified forks of the Baileys WhatsApp library. At runtime the packages abuse a developer's authenticated WhatsApp session to follow attacker-chosen channels and join groups without consent, quietly enrolling WhatsApp bot accounts into attacker-controlled audiences. The packages accumulated roughly 490,000 downloads, about 116,000 of them in the 30 days before disclosure. Reported examples include ourin-baileys, @nexustechpro/baileys, @badzz88/baileys, @yudzxml/baileys, and my-auto-follow.

The activity has a documented history rather than a single disclosure. SafeDep flagged Baileys npm forks in August 2026 that silently made the installer's WhatsApp account follow author-controlled channels and injected the author's advertising URL into every image and video the bot sent. Xygeni later detailed a separate fork, @dappaoffc/baileys-mod, that subscribed the authenticated bot session to attacker newsletters. OX Security's writeup sorts 93 packages into three variants: 19 fetch channel IDs from GitHub at runtime, 60 embed them in cleartext, and 14 obfuscate them. One WhatsApp group involved is assessed to be Indonesia-based and advertises accounts for Mobile Legends: Bang Bang and TikTok.

The immediate risk is account and reputation abuse of WhatsApp bot operators rather than data theft, but the subscriber-fraud mechanics scale well: any developer pulling a typosquatted Baileys fork turns their bot's session into a growth engine for attacker channels and ad injection.

Watch for npm takedowns of the listed packages, additional forks outside the 101 identified, and whether WhatsApp takes action on the enrolled groups and newsletters. Teams running Baileys-based bots should pin the upstream library, audit dependencies for the listed names, and treat any unexplained channel follows or outgoing-media modification as compromise indicators.
