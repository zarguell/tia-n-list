GreyNoise tracks an active intrusion campaign against Check Point Security Management servers under the name GT13, using agentic tooling for scanning, exploitation and post-compromise work alongside hands-on-keyboard activity. The firm started tracking it in early October and assesses it is likely still ongoing. GT13 operated from 99 IPs, all either ExpressVPN egresses or compromised victim Check Point devices, across more than 1,400 network sessions with over 800 command line artifacts and 1,200 files downloaded, uploaded or created. BleepingComputer and The Hacker News report roughly 24 compromised management servers at 22 organizations in 17 countries, spanning energy, engineering, financial services, government, manufacturing, mining and transportation.

The July entry point was CVE-2026-16232, a CVSS 9.1 authentication bypass in SmartConsole login that lets an unauthenticated attacker obtain an application token and administer Security Management or Multi-Domain Management servers. Check Point confirmed in-the-wild exploitation against a small number of customers whose management IP was internet-reachable without Trusted Client restrictions, and CISA added it to KEV on July 22 with a July 25 federal deadline. Fixes shipped in Jumbo Hotfix R82.10 Take 36, R82 Take 118 and R81.20 Take 158; Rapid7 published a proof of concept on July 28. Hunt guidance includes SmartConsole audit entries showing "Authentication method: application token" and six attacker IPs published by the vendor.

Post-compromise reporting describes stolen CA files, newly generated certificates and unloaded firewall policies, with a Swedish report describing security policies taken from Check Point servers. In one case GreyNoise saw the actor patch a server it had broken into, apparently to keep other intruders out. Censys counted 3,836 hosts presenting the default cp_mgmt SIC identity, a role-presence figure rather than a confirmed vulnerable count, and separately reported CVE-2026-91843, a CVSS 9.8 pre-authentication stack overflow in the same management surface. Watch for anomalous certificates, policy changes and management access from unexpected networks, and check Trusted Client restrictions and hotfix levels on exposed servers.
