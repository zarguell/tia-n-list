RIKEN says an unauthorized party used the public dashboard that displays Fugaku's operational status as the path to multiple internal servers between 10 and 13 November 2026, then planted malware aimed at user data. The affected population is 4,597 users whose personal information and research data may have been exposed; RIKEN has not published the intrusion method, a CVE, a patch version, an attribution, or an IoC list. It also reports that some access keys were used in attempts to connect to additional systems, with no evidence found so far of further unauthorized access or theft of research results.

This is a control-plane compromise, not a monitoring nuisance. A dashboard meant to report operational state was sufficient to reach internal servers, so any research institute that treats status displays as read-only utilities has the same shape of exposure, even though this incident has no CVE to patch. The 4,597 figure is an exposure count, not a confirmed theft count, and the key-reuse attempts move the risk off Fugaku: credentials stored on a 537 petaflops machine used for drug discovery, severe-weather forecasting and airborne virus transmission work can be tried against unrelated systems that accept the same keys. Four days of access on a national research platform is why this outranks the day's patchable advisories.

RIKEN shut Fugaku down on 14 November, disabled users' login keys and told users to revoke and replace credentials they had stored on the machine. Account holders should treat those keys as captured, replace them everywhere they were reused, and check authentication records on other systems for attempts tied to the old Fugaku keys during and after the 10 to 13 November window. Internal hunt scope should include network flows from the public status dashboard into internal servers, malware on the servers reached through that path, and later use of Fugaku credentials, including attempts against Rikyu, the 64 petaflops system run by the same center. No patch version, workaround or named IoC has been published, so the available action is credential invalidation and access-path review rather than a vendor fix.

What would change the assessment is RIKEN publishing the exact intrusion method, or confirming that the planted malware actually read and exfiltrated research data rather than merely targeting it. Confirmation that the access-key attempts succeeded against another system, or that Rikyu credentials fall within the affected scope, would widen the incident beyond a single supercomputer.
