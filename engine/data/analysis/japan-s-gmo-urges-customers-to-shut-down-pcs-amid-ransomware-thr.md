The intrusion targeted GMO Internet's Onamae.com Desktop Cloud service, where a third party installed malicious programs in customer server environments beginning around October 20, 2026. The company confirmed unauthorized access and began shutting down customer servers at 04:00 local time on October 21, affecting up to 26,739 servers used for automated foreign-exchange margin trading. A threat actor then threatened a ransomware deployment and a noon deadline, demanding roughly $50 million and prompting GMO to tell customers to disconnect systems, back up data, and physically shut down PCs.

This is not a routine hosting incident: the service hosts always-on trading algorithms, so every hour of forced shutdown translates to missed market positions and direct revenue loss for thousands of traders. The 26,739 server figure comes from the vendor's own reporting, not an external estimate, and the October 20-21 intrusion timeline shows the attacker had at least a full week inside the environment before the ransomware threat was issued. Most hosting breaches stay contained; this one escalated to a public shutdown directive, which means the attacker either exfiltrated credentials or installed persistence that made selective remediation impossible.

Defenders should treat October 20-21 server logs from Onamae.com Desktop Cloud environments as the starting point for hunting, specifically looking for unauthorized program installations and any outbound connections made between October 20 and October 27. Patch or hotfix versions are not specified in the vendor advisories, so the concrete action is to follow GMO's directive: disconnect affected systems, scan for malware, restore from pre-October 20 backups, and confirm no malicious programs remain before reconnecting. Monitor the official GMO news articles (articles 246 and 247) for updates on encryption status or data publication, and watch whether the $50 million ransom deadline produces actual encryption or leaks.

The assessment would change if GMO confirms active encryption on customer servers or publishes IoC names, or if the threat actor releases stolen customer data instead of launching ransomware. Until then, the primary risk remains operational disruption across 26,739 trading-hosting servers rather than confirmed data destruction.
