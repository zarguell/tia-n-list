Hackers accessed a University of Copenhagen test environment containing personal data for 525,000 people, with an additional 20,000 staff and students affected by PIN code exposure. The intrusion was detected on October 20 and disclosed on October 23; the university blocked access and reported the incident to the Danish Data Protection Authority. Compromised records include full names, Danish national identification numbers, phone numbers, and email addresses.

This exposure is not routine institutional noise: 525,000 records combine Danish CPR IDs with contact details, giving attackers exactly the material needed for targeted social engineering and identity fraud at national scale. The university serves 36,800 students and 10,700 employees across six faculties and more than 200 research centers, meaning the victim pool spans researchers, staff, and students with access to sensitive academic and government-linked projects. The added PIN exposure forced temporary physical-access restrictions on campus, showing the breach crossed from data loss to operational disruption. That physical-security impact is the claim the event list does not spell out on its own: a test-environment breach altered building-access policy at Denmark's oldest university.

Defenders should treat Danish CPR IDs as high-value identity artifacts and hunt for phishing using university branding that references the October breach. Network monitoring should focus on any outbound connections from test-environment segments after October 20, and identity-protection services should be prioritized for the 525,000 exposed individuals. The university has distributed new access codes and gradually reopened restricted locations, but the underlying exposure of national identification data remains unchangeable.

Watch for the final scope assessment from investigators and any follow-on attacks using the exposed records. A second development that would change the assessment is confirmation that the attacker accessed operational systems supporting daily university activities, which the university currently denies.
