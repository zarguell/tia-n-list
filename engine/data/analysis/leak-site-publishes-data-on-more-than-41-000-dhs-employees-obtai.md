A hacktivist group calling itself the Department of Peace published a leak site on October 28, 2026 exposing data on more than 41,000 DHS employees and contractors, including undercover ICE agents and CISA staff, drawn from an Okta single sign-on service used by the agency. Distributed Denial of Secrets hosts the archive under the name Icepick and maia arson crimew built a search tool over it. CyberScoop verified numerous current CISA employees and some former leadership appear in the data. The records span DHS and seven component agencies, including ICE and CBP, were last updated August 19, 2026, and include names, offices, contact information, and assignments. The group said the breach answered DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin's July 17 promise to turn up the heat on ICE operations.

The exposure is not a password dump; it is an org chart with phone numbers, which is more dangerous for the named people than a credential leak. More than 41,000 records put undercover and sensitive personnel, and their families, at risk of targeting, and the archive identifies officials tied to covert operations, the government's hunt for leakers, and the failed BlueLeaks investigation. The earlier March 2026 Department of Peace leak of over 6,000 DHS and ICE contractor records shows this is a sustained campaign, not a one-off, and the October dump is about seven times larger. That is why this story matters more than the routine breach of the day: it maps command structure inside an agency running rendition and deportation programs, and it arrived from an actor with a stated political motive and a track record.

Defenders cannot patch this away; the data is already public, so the work is containment and monitoring. Agencies should treat every leaked email address and phone number as a targeting vector for spear-phishing and vishing, and rotate or mask contact details where operationally possible. Security teams should review Okta authentication logs for the affected tenants for anomalous sign-ins, impossible travel, and new device enrollments around the August 19, 2026 data cutoff and after, and force re-enrollment or step-up authentication for accounts in the archive. Affected employees, especially undercover and sensitive-role staff, need direct notification, physical-security review, and guidance on social-media and contact hygiene. DHS and Okta should publish a root-cause and scope statement so downstream defenders can size their own exposure.

Watch for DHS or Okta confirmation of how the Okta tenant was accessed, since the breach claim remains uncorroborated by the agency and Reuters could not verify it. A second release, targeting of named employees, or expansion of the archive beyond the August 19, 2026 snapshot would raise the assessment. Legal action against DDoSecrets or maia arson crimew, or a takedown of the Icepi.cx search tool, would change how widely the data spreads. Any evidence that the same access yielded authentication secrets rather than directory data would turn a privacy incident into a full identity compromise.
