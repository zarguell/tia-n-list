Vanderbilt University in Nashville is responding to a campus-wide ransomware incident that surfaced Monday, November 9, 2026, when the BYOD ransomware gang added the university to its leak site and Vanderbilt told its community to shut down university devices and disconnect from campus Wi-Fi. The gang claims it encrypted the university's databases and stole sensitive student, employee, and research data, including over 1 million SSNs, names, email addresses, phone numbers, and student and employee records, and asserts it took over the domain controller. Vanderbilt confirmed the incident in a statement on X, warning that university devices, systems, and cloud-based services may be affected, but has not disclosed the nature of the attack or confirmed whether data was stolen or systems were encrypted.

The exposure is broad and the verification is thin: Vanderbilt serves about 14,000 students and more than 6,000 staff, and the gang's claims of over 1 million affected records and full domain controller takeover, if accurate, would put nearly every campus account and machine at risk, including research data that cannot be reissued like a password. The university's own guidance treats the threat as live across endpoints and cloud storage, with VUIT staff telling faculty Monday morning that school-issued computers were not safe to use and warning of pop-up README tabs, permission changes in OneDrive or Box, and unexpected authentication requests. What makes this story matter more than the daily ransomware noise is the combination of a new and ramping actor, BYOD only emerged in September 2026 with its first victim on September 29, and a target whose research estate and student records are concentrated in a single Active Directory environment. The gang's claims remain unverified, and neither encryption nor exfiltration has been confirmed by Vanderbilt.

Defender actions are concrete and immediate: keep university devices powered off until IT clears them, stay off campus Wi-Fi, and do not investigate or troubleshoot suspected compromise; instead shut the device down and contact the IT service desk at 615-343-9999 from a different device. Hunt for the indicators Vanderbilt published: unexpected pop-ups or security notifications, missing or modified files, unfamiliar applications, unusual permission changes in OneDrive or Box, and unexpected password or login-approval prompts, plus pop-up README tabs reported by students. Users should close Microsoft Word documents and OneDrive and Dropbox folders, change any password reused outside Vanderbilt, enable multifactor authentication on personal accounts, and consider a credit freeze with Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion if Social Security numbers are confirmed exposed. Phishing risk is elevated after an incident like this, so treat unsolicited security-update or password-reset messages as hostile and rely only on official Vanderbilt channels. The AlertVU campus warning went out at 11:45 a.m. Monday, and the medical side is a separate organization: Vanderbilt Health stated its IT systems so far have not been impacted and patient care continues.

The assessment changes on a small set of developments: a formal incident report from Vanderbilt confirming or ruling out encryption and exfiltration, publication of sample files or a ransom demand by BYOD, follow-on disclosures affecting research data or student records, and any sign that Vanderbilt Health's separate IT environment is drawn in despite its current statement that patient care continues. Until then the picture stays incomplete and the safe assumption is that campus endpoints and cloud data are compromised until cleared.
