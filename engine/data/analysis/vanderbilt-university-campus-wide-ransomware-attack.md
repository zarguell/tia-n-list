Vanderbilt University in Nashville suffered a ransomware attack claimed by the BYOD ransomware gang, discovered Monday, November 9, 2026. The university told roughly 14,000 students and more than 6,000 staff to shut down university devices and disconnect from campus Wi-Fi as malicious software spread through computers, files, and digital infrastructure. Vanderbilt confirmed it was responding to an active cybersecurity incident affecting devices, systems, and cloud-based services, and warned of signs of compromise such as unexpected pop-ups, modified cloud files, missing files, unfamiliar applications, and unexpected authentication requests. The university has not publicly confirmed whether data was stolen or systems encrypted. Vanderbilt University and Vanderbilt Health are legally and financially separate, and the hospital was not affected.

BYOD listed Vanderbilt on its leak site, claiming databases were locked and that it holds over one million Social Security numbers plus full names, email addresses, phone numbers, student and employee records, and domain controller access. The group emerged in September, with its first victim appearing on September 29, and previously hit the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center, causing system outages and disrupted patient care. On campus, VUIT staff warned faculty Monday morning that school-issued computers were not safe to use, and the AlertVU campus warning went out at 11:45 a.m. By Tuesday morning, Vanderbilt restored select systems including campus Wi-Fi, Okta, Outlook, Brightspace, Zoom, and YES, allowed devices back online, and kept working with government agencies and partners; CampusPrint remained restricted.

What to watch: whether Vanderbilt confirms the million-plus SSN claim or whether BYOD publishes the data, since the group's claims are unverified. Service restoration and any further outages matter for a 14,000-student campus heading into the week. BYOD's targeting of academic and health care institutions suggests other universities should treat this as an active campaign indicator rather than an isolated incident.
