Vanderbilt University is responding to a campus-wide ransomware attack that the BYOD ransomware group has claimed on its leak site, saying it encrypted the university's databases and stole sensitive student, employee, and research data. The private research university in Nashville, Tennessee, which serves more than 13,800 students, confirmed on Monday, November 9, 2026 that it was handling an active cybersecurity incident that may affect university devices, systems, and cloud-based services. Its IT team told students, faculty, and staff to shut down university devices, disconnect from campus Wi-Fi, and watch for signs of compromise such as unexpected pop-ups, unusual changes to files or permissions in cloud storage, missing or modified files, unfamiliar applications, and unexpected authentication requests. Anyone noticing suspicious activity was told not to investigate on their own but to power down and contact the IT service desk.

BYOD emerged in September 2026 and has since claimed several attacks against academic and health care targets, including one last month against the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center that caused system outages, disrupted patient care, and affected academics. The group claims to hold more than one million Social Security numbers from the Vanderbilt intrusion and posted a video to YouTube in which a Vanderbilt computer plays an audio message. Vanderbilt has not publicly confirmed whether systems were encrypted or whether any data was actually taken, and it has not corroborated the group's data-volume claims.

By Tuesday morning the university said select campus-wide systems were safe to use again, including campus WiFi, Okta, Outlook, Brightspace, Zoom, and YES, while others such as CampusPrint remained restricted. Students, faculty, and staff were allowed to resume using Vanderbilt devices after the shutdown order. Vanderbilt said it continues to work with government agencies and other partners to manage the situation and would keep sending updates through the week.

What to watch: whether Vanderbilt confirms data theft or encryption, which would validate or undercut BYOD's claim of more than one million stolen Social Security numbers; whether the group publishes stolen data on its leak site; and whether the incident traces to the same initial access pattern seen in the University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center case. Higher education remains a repeated BYOD target, so peer institutions should review exposure of internet-facing systems and identity providers such as Okta.
