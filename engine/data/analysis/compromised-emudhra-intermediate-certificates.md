The eMudhra incident is a public key infrastructure failure rather than a conventional vulnerability. On 2026-10-17 a researcher scanning certificate transparency data reported that eight intermediate CA certificates issued by the Indian authority eMudhra had P-384 private keys generated with almost no entropy. The key values were 4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230 and 237, meaning anyone could enumerate 256 candidates and recover a key in under a second. Security researcher Scott Helme reproduced all eight on a laptop, and researcher known as Wayne published one key on GitHub, a value of 4 for the EM OV TLS CA - G3C certificate, which BleepingComputer confirmed matched a revoked certificate. Six of the certificates issued TLS certificates and two were code-signing intermediates.

The cause was an April 30, 2025 key generation ceremony for 24 subordinate CAs under emSign Root TLS CA - G3 and the cross-signed Comodo AAA Certificate Services. A wrap/unwrap step in a custom Python script, written to move CA keys between HSM vendors, generated a second set of weak P-384 keys that were mistakenly selected for signing because their labels resembled the correct keys. A later incident report from eMudhra disclosed eight additional revoked intermediates, bringing the total to 16, and said the same script also handled eight RSA private keys in the memory of the host rather than inside the HSM, so the company could not show they met its key handling policy. eMudhra has since announced it will stop issuing SSL/TLS certificates entirely, citing compliance cost and competition from free issuers.

Containment moved quickly at the browser and OS layer: Chrome and Firefox blocked the certificates on 2026-10-17, Apple on 2026-10-19, Microsoft on 2026-10-20, and Sectigo and Cloudflare took revocation and service-side action. Microsoft issued out-of-band updates KB5134133 for Windows 11 24H2, 25H2 and 26H2 and KB5134125 for Windows 10 21H2 and 22H2 on 2026-10-26 for systems that cannot reach the certificate update service, then applied the same removals to Edge on 2026-10-27. The Go project shipped the denylist fix as CVE-2026-111943 in Go 1.26.10 and 1.27.3, which makes crypto/x509 fail verification when a known bad intermediate appears in a chain.

The residual exposure is software that pins its own CA bundle and skips revocation checks: curl, Node, Python certifi and pip virtual environments, appliance TLS stacks, VPN clients, and unmaintained IoT and container images. Watch for issued certificates that still chain through the blocked intermediates, particularly code-signing artifacts trusted by Windows hosts that do not receive CTL updates, and for the eight additional revocations as downstream trust stores catch up. Any binary signed by one of the two code-signing intermediates should be treated as unauthenticated until proven otherwise.
