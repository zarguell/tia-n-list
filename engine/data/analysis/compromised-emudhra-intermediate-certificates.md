In October 2026 researchers disclosed that private keys for eight eMudhra intermediate certificate authority certificates were compromised because they were generated with far too little randomness, in some cases single-digit or double-digit integers reducing 384-bit entropy to 3 to 8 bits. The weakness originated during an April 30, 2025 key generation ceremony for 24 subordinate CAs under the emSign Root TLS CA G3 and a cross-signed Comodo AAA Certificate Services root, where a flaw in a wrap and unwrap step created a second set of weak P-384 keys that were mistakenly used for eight intermediates. Researcher Wayne signed messages with each compromised key to prove possession, and Scott Helme recovered all eight keys on a laptop in under a second, a task an AI agent later repeated from a single prompt.

The affected set includes six TLS intermediates used for domain-validated, organization-validated, and extended-validation certificates plus two code-signing intermediates. Major trust stores responded with emergency blocks: Mozilla received the initial report on October 17, 2026 at 20:06 UTC and blocked the intermediates through OneCRL; Cloudflare became aware on October 19 at about 17:00 UTC; Apple blocked on October 19, Microsoft on October 20, and Go released emergency updates on October 19. Go introduced CVE-2026-111943, CVE-2026-111944, and related identifiers, adding a denylist in crypto/x509 so certificate verification fails when a known untrusted intermediate key appears in a chain. Debian issued DSA-6572-1 and other ecosystems published guidance.

The consequences extend beyond browser trust. Because an intermediate CA can issue certificates for arbitrary domains, possession of its key allows forging browser- and app-trusted credentials, a fundamentally different risk from a single site certificate compromise. eMudhra revoked the compromised certificates, stopped issuing under its G3 root, and announced it would exit the SSL and TLS certificate business, citing rising compliance costs and competition from free certificate providers. Organizations should update trust stores and language runtimes, audit TLS certificate chains for the affected intermediates, and treat any certificate issued under them during the exposure window as suspect.
