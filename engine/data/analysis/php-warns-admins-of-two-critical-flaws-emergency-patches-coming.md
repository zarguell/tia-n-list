CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619 are two remote code execution vulnerabilities in PHP core, each rated CVSS v4.0 9.2, with leaked reports claiming a CVSS score of 10.0. The flaws reportedly affect every PHP version from 5 through 8, and details leaked on social media ahead of a coordinated disclosure that had been scheduled for October 22, 2026 at 18:00 CEST. In response, the PHP security team issued an advance notice on October 20 and moved the fix release to Wednesday, October 21, 2026 at 14:00 UTC, warning that it may ship earlier if details spread further or evidence of active exploitation appears. The team has not published technical details, affected functions, configurations, or fixed version numbers; watchTowr confirmed the flaws are real and reports no exploitation in the wild as of October 21, 2026.

The risk here is not just the severity but the combination of ubiquity and a pre-patch leak: roughly 2.24 million internet-facing hosts report an X-Powered-By PHP banner, and only PHP 8.2 and later will receive patches, leaving about 884,000 hosts on end-of-life branches with no vendor fix. Because the vulnerabilities reportedly reach back to PHP 5 and code from 2004 onward, a large share of the installed base cannot be remediated by patching alone and must either migrate or rely on compensating controls. The leak hands attackers a head start before any fix exists, while the absence of technical detail prevents defenders from scoping blast radius or writing targeted detections, so this is a preparation and exposure-management problem today rather than an incident-response problem. That is why it stands out from the routine PHP advisories: two core RCEs, no workaround, and a compressed defender window.

Defenders should inventory PHP versions now and prepare to upgrade to the fixed releases, 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36, and 8.2.35, as soon as they ship on October 21 at 14:00 UTC. Systems on PHP 8.1 or older need a migration plan or interim protection such as a WAF or virtual patch in front of PHP entry points, plus restricting which endpoints are reachable. With no public exploit, affected function, or indicators of compromise, detection must be behavioral: watch for unexpected child processes spawned by web server or PHP-FPM workers, new or modified files under document roots, and anomalous outbound connections from PHP processes. Administrators should also avoid exposing phpinfo and version banners and confirm which applications depend on older PHP branches before the release.

What changes the assessment is the arrival of the technical details and any proof of concept, which would convert an unpatched exposure problem into an actively exploitable one overnight, along with any report of in-the-wild exploitation, confirmation of the affected functions and configurations, or a decision by the PHP team to backport fixes to end-of-life branches. An accelerated release ahead of October 21 at 14:00 UTC would signal that exploitation evidence has already appeared. Until those developments occur, the right posture is rapid patching and exposure reduction rather than panic.
