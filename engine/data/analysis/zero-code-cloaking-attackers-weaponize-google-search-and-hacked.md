ADEX researchers documented an advertising fraud technique they call "zero-code cloaking" that chains three legitimate components to bypass Google Ads screening without any cloaking infrastructure. An advertiser points ad inventory at a Google search result, whose top hit is a compromised Thai educational domain (km.chpc.ac.th, restricted .ac.th zone). Clicking through lands on an illegal online casino. No user-agent detection, no bot-specific serving, no cloaking code is needed. Every component is what it appears to be; the fraud is in the arrangement.

The Thai college's web server was co-opted into an ad fraud chain serving double duty as student infrastructure and casino redirect. ADEX linked the operation to a broader market of compromised trusted domains used as reputation laundering for illegal advertising. The .ac.th suffix, like .edu and .gov, carries implicit trust that ad moderation systems and users rely on. This case shows that trust model is functionally broken: a compromised educational domain in a restricted TLD can serve as the linchpin of a fraud operation with zero custom evasion tooling.

This matters because it demonstrates that ad fraud and initial access share a common abstraction: borrowed trust. The same pattern of abusing legitimate infrastructure to bypass security controls appears in phishing, supply chain compromise, and now ad fraud. Ad platforms that rely on domain reputation as a signal need to account for compromised legitimate domains, not just newly registered or obviously suspicious ones. The technique requires no sophistication to replicate, making it likely to spread.
