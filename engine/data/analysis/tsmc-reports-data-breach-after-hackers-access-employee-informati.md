TSMC disclosed on November 12, 2026, via a Form 6-K signed by CFO Wendell Huang, that personal data on some current and former employees was found on an external website without authorization. The company verified that the exposed information matches data held in a cloud service provider's systems, stated it found no evidence that TSMC's own systems were compromised, and said the data is unrelated to company operations or customer information. A seller on DarkForums and BreachForums is advertising a claimed 46,000 employee records totaling about 6.9 GB for 3 BTC (roughly $220,000), with a reported 499-record sample and a threat to publish the full set within a week. TSMC has not confirmed the record count or the data types, and the cloud provider has not been named.

The exposure is a vendor-boundary failure rather than a TSMC intrusion: the company's own controls and production environment are not implicated on current evidence, but roughly 46,000 people's HR and identity records were already outside TSMC's control. That data is likely to include addresses, bank account numbers, and emergency contacts, which is enough for targeted phishing, payroll redirection, and credential-stuffing against a workforce that holds physical access to fabrication sites. The 3 BTC asking price is low for a dataset of this size, which reads more like a bulk dump priced to move quickly than a carefully negotiated sale. The reason this story matters more than routine leak notices is the combination of a single unnamed cloud vendor, a high-value semiconductor workforce, and a public sale that any criminal buyer can act on before TSMC finishes notification.

There is no patch or hotfix to apply because this is not a product vulnerability; remediation is administrative and vendor-driven. TSMC is notifying affected personnel and has engaged the cloud vendor, investigating authorities, and a third-party cybersecurity firm, so the immediate defender actions sit with identity and HR teams: force password resets and MFA re-enrollment for affected staff, invalidate sessions, and brief employees on phishing and payroll-change scams. Network and access teams should restrict and review connectivity to the HR cloud tenant, audit storage permissions and sharing links, and pull the provider's access logs for anomalous exports or bulk downloads around the suspected window. Because no log paths or telemetry field names were published, hunts should focus on cloud audit trails, IdP sign-in anomalies, and any appearance of the 499-record sample or 6.9 GB archive outside the forums.

The assessment changes on two developments: identification of the cloud provider and whether the same tenant or misconfiguration affects other large employers, and whether the seller publishes or a buyer redistributes the full 6.9 GB set. A confirmed provider compromise rather than a configuration error would widen the blast radius well beyond TSMC, while a quiet takedown with no further samples would suggest the exposure was limited and contained.
