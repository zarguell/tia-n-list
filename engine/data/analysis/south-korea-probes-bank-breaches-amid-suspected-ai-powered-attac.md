South Korea's Financial Services Commission convened an emergency meeting after attacks on three of the country's largest retail banks inside the same window. Shinhan Bank confirmed a leak of 25,000 customer records, KB Kookmin Bank disclosed credit card data for 119,000 clients, and Hana Bank reported a limited breach through its sales-support system. Shinhan and Kookmin each hold more than $400 billion in assets, so none of the victims are peripheral. On-site investigators are deployed and findings are flowing to KISA, with President Lee ordering a thorough probe of data leaks at financial and public institutions the day before the meeting. Attribution is open, but Yonhap reported an attack server carried an HTML page title containing Chinese-language text, and officials suspect a Chinese AI tool or AI agents were in the loop, on the theory that current models let low-skill actors run intrusions that once required specialist crews.

Three of the biggest retail banks breached at once is a working playbook, not three coincidences. The one confirmed entry point, Hana's sales-support system, is the part that should worry every other bank: peripheral business-support web applications hold customer data but receive a fraction of the monitoring that core banking platforms get, and the headline numbers show the exfiltration was real. The 119,000 Kookmin credit card records are directly monetizable, and card data converts to fraud within days, so a large share of the loss will land outside the banks on cardholders and merchants. The AI-agent claim, if it survives review, is the strategic story: it collapses the cost of intrusion and widens the capable-actor pool to anyone who can drive a model. Combined with the Chinese-language infrastructure clue, the profile fits a high-volume commercial operation scaling across multiple banks rather than a bespoke state crew sinking months into one deeply breached target. Even if the AI attribution dissolves, the demonstrated fact stands: commodity tooling defeated three systemically large banks simultaneously.

Treat internet-facing sales, marketing, and customer-support web applications as the priority audit surface, because the one confirmed entry vector was exactly that class of system. Pull web server and WAF logs for those hosts covering the days before the October 5 disclosures and look for mass-record query patterns and session volumes that do not match business hours. Hunt on the single public indicator: HTML page titles or page content with Chinese-language text on servers touched by suspicious sessions, plus egress from bank ranges to recently registered hosting. Card fraud controls should be tightened for Kookmin cardholders immediately, since misuse of the 119,000 exposed records will likely precede any public confirmation. Track KISA and FSC output from the on-site investigations, and assume the FSC's pledged regulatory review converts into mandatory control requirements for the sector.

Two developments would change this assessment. Official forensics naming the AI tool and demonstrating its role in the intrusion chain would turn the incident from anomaly into template and force every bank to assume adversaries with minimal skill and maximal output. And if the Chinese-language server evidence resolves into named infrastructure with additional victims, the playbook is still live and the victim list is incomplete. Compensation rulings and the realized fraud rate against the Kookmin cardholders will settle what the incident actually costs.
