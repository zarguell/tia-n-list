The mechanism reported is credential theft against a software vendor's source-control footprint, not exploitation of a product vulnerability. According to Der Spiegel, which published at 20:14 CET on 26 November 2026 under the internal track name Project Granite, attackers targeted SAP's GitHub presence; an insider said employee authentication tokens were stolen and then used to copy and modify source code. Handelsblatt reported the same evening that SAP restricted access to GitHub, suspended automated operations in software development and replaced signing keys. SAP's own line to SecurityWeek is narrower than the headlines: it detected suspicious activity in development infrastructure, took protective measures, and has found no evidence of unauthorized access to customer business data. No CVE, no attacker group, no initial access vector and no repository list has been published, and the token theft and code alteration remain single-source claims.

The load-bearing detail is the signing-key rotation, and almost every headline skipped it. Rotating signing keys is a statement about artifact authenticity, not about confidentiality, and it is the one action in this story that costs a company real operational pain. A vendor that believed the intrusion was confined to a source-code reading problem would rotate tokens and be done; SAP instead restricted GitHub access, froze automated development pipelines and replaced keys that vouch for what it ships. Whether or not altered commits reached a build, the containment implies SAP could not immediately prove they had not. That reframes the risk for anyone downstream: SAP publishes developer tooling, sample applications and open source projects on GitHub, so the exposed population is not only SAP tenants but every team pulling SAP-authored dependencies, SDKs or pipeline templates whose integrity now rests on a rotation nobody outside Walldorf can audit. SAP saying customer business data was untouched answers a question about confidentiality in its cloud; it does not answer the integrity question the key rotation raises. This story earns a desk slot over the other thirty today because it is the rare case where the vendor's own remediation, rather than a researcher's proof of concept, is the strongest available evidence of severity.

For defenders, there is nothing to patch, so the work is provenance and hunting. Pin any SAP-sourced package, action, container image or sample repository to signed releases and verify hashes rather than tracking branch heads, and treat commits pushed to SAP-owned repositories between roughly 24 and 27 November 2026 as untrusted until SAP says which repositories were touched. If your own integrations authenticate to GitHub with SAP-adjacent service accounts or app installations, rotate those credentials and drop any classic long-lived personal access token in favor of short-lived scoped ones. Hunt in the GitHub organization audit log for unusual token issuance and use, specifically personal access token grants to new OAuth apps, repository download-as-archive events performed with a personal access token, force pushes or history rewrites on protected branches, additions of deploy keys, signing keys or webhooks, and edits to CI workflow files that ran outside business hours. Export that audit log now, before the retention window closes, and correlate the actors involved against your CI logs for jobs that changed artifact contents without a matching pull request. On the SAP side, watch the support portal and Trust Center rather than press coverage; no customer-facing advisory or indicator list has appeared yet.

Two developments would move the assessment. First, forensic scope: if SAP confirms that any altered commit entered a build that produced a released artifact, a signed package or a customer-delivered update, this stops being a vendor incident story and becomes a supply-chain integrity event with a remediation deadline for every consumer of SAP software. Second, disposition of the single-source claims: independent confirmation of the token theft, publication of an initial access vector, or an SAP statement revising the customer-data finding would each change the confidence level, and mention of agentic AI assistance in the Spiegel account remains unevidenced and should not be repeated as fact. A named attacker group or a BSI warning would signal that German authorities see more than SAP has disclosed.
