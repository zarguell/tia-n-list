The n0n extortion group listed Visa and JPMorgan Chase on its leak site on November 16 with a 48-hour countdown, then published data it claims came from the three firms. A Bitdefender post carried on November 20 says all three corporations were given 48 hours to negotiate and none paid. Newsweek reported about 16,000 customer email addresses, phone numbers and JPMorgan account IDs, apparently tied to the invitation-only Reserve program for clients with at least $10 million in assets under management. Other reporting cited more than 6,000 Visa premium cardholders and a 502-kilobyte leaked file whose completeness researchers could not establish.

On November 19 the companies confirmed incidents at outside providers. JPMorgan said vendors were targeted and attackers reached marketing data that may include customer names and email addresses, with no service disruption and credit monitoring where appropriate. Visa tied the claims to a third-party provider in Asia Pacific and said its own systems and payment data, including card numbers, were not involved. Mastercard said a provider exposed a limited number of email addresses for a small subset of cardholders in a benefit program, and that its payment network was unaffected. BleepingComputer's November 20 vendor-breach report restated the same divisions between vendor data and payment systems.

JPMorgan had attributed November 16 transaction delays to a temporary software bug in on-premise hardware, with no evidence of a cyber cause. Its later statement says the vendor intrusion is unrelated to those delays. SecureBlink's earlier account described n0n as financially motivated, with financial services making up 23 percent of claimed victims, and linked documented intrusions to weak or reused credentials and credential stuffing against more than 300 FortiGate SSL-VPN appliances rather than vulnerability exploitation.

The concrete details to watch are the unnamed Asia Pacific provider, confirmation of the 16,000-record set and any further n0n publication. No payment card numbers have been reported in the leaked material, but contact data and account identifiers tied to premium banking programs are useful for targeted phishing against high-net-worth clients.
