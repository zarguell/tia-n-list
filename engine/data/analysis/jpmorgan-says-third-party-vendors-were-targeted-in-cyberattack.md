The n0n extortion group listed Visa and JPMorgan Chase on its leak site after a 16 November claim, giving a 48-hour publication deadline. Visa said the activity it was investigating began at a third-party provider in Asia Pacific and that its own systems and payment card data were not involved. JPMorgan said its vendors were targeted and that attackers may have reached marketing data containing customer names and email addresses. The bank also said there was no service disruption and no connection to the transaction delays it had attributed to an on-premise software bug.

Leaked material is narrower than the initial bank names suggest. Newsweek reported about 16,000 customer email addresses, phone numbers and JPMorgan account IDs, apparently tied to the invitation-only Reserve program for clients with at least 10 million dollars in assets under management. Cybernews cited more than 6,000 affected Visa premium cardholders and a 502-kilobyte file whose completeness researchers could not establish. SecureBlink describes n0n as financially motivated, with financial services making up 23 percent of claimed victims, and says documented intrusions used weak or reused credentials rather than vulnerability exploitation, including credential stuffing against more than 300 FortiGate SSL-VPN appliances.

Watch for the vendor name, the exact data fields and whether Mastercard customer data is confirmed rather than inferred from roundup headlines. The leak samples and the bank statements still leave several gaps: which provider was accessed, whether the file is complete, and whether the same actor reached all three card brands. Also keep the 16 November outage separate from the breach reporting. CNBC and JPMorgan attributed the delayed transactions to a temporary software bug in on-premise hardware, and the cyber extortion claims came later. The operational lesson is third-party concentration: one provider can put several large financial brands on the same leak site.
