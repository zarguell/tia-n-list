JPMorgan Chase, Visa and Mastercard each confirmed incidents at outside service providers after the n0n extortion group claimed customer data from all three. JPMorgan said vendors holding its marketing data were targeted and that names and email addresses may have been accessed, with no disruption to bank services. Visa tied the claims to a third-party service provider in Asia Pacific and said no payment data such as card numbers and none of its own systems were involved. Mastercard described exposure of a limited number of email addresses for a small subset of cardholders in a specific benefit program, with its payment network unaffected. None of the companies named the provider.

n0n listed Visa and JPMorgan on 16 November with a 48-hour publication deadline, then posted material. Newsweek reported roughly 16,000 customer email addresses, phone numbers and JPMorgan account IDs, apparently tied to the invitation-only Reserve program for clients with at least $10 million in assets under management. Cybernews cited more than 6,000 affected Visa premium cardholders and a 502-kilobyte leaked file whose completeness it could not establish. SecureBlink describes n0n as financially motivated, with financial services at 23 percent of claimed victims, and links documented intrusions to weak or reused credentials, including credential stuffing against more than 300 FortiGate SSL-VPN appliances rather than vulnerability exploitation.

JPMorgan separately attributed transaction delays on 16 November to a temporary software bug in on-premise hardware and said the incident is unrelated. Reports across JPMorgan, Bank of America, U.S. Bank, Wells Fargo, PNC, Citigroup, Capital One and Zelle that day came from user-submitted Downdetector reports, and U.S. Bank said its systems were not directly affected. The bank has said it will contact affected customers and offer free credit monitoring where appropriate.

Watch for identification of the provider or providers, confirmation of whether one vendor or several were involved, and a full inventory of the posted files. The mix of high-net-worth customer identifiers and benefit-program email addresses makes targeted phishing the near-term risk; no card numbers or payment data have been reported in the leaked material.
