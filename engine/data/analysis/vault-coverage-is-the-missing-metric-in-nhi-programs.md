GitGuardian argues that non-human identity (NHI) programs measure the wrong thing. Teams can see which machine credentials made it into the secrets vault, but cannot see the full population of machine credentials still living in repositories, pipelines, cloud environments, and collaboration tools. The argument is that vault coverage, the share of machine credentials actually managed inside the vault, is the metric that predicts exposure, and most programs do not track it.

The point is structural. NHI programs are organized around the secrets manager, so their dashboards report what the vault holds. The risk lives in what it does not: service accounts, workload identities, and AI agent credentials scattered across code and configuration. A vault with high adoption inside its own walls can still leave a wide unmanaged tail, and that tail is exactly what attackers enumerate.

Watch for vendors folding vault coverage into their NHI reporting, since the metric is easy to compute once you scan repositories and pipelines against vault records. For practitioners the actionable step is measuring the gap directly: inventory all machine credentials, cross-reference against the vault, and treat the uncovered set as the priority queue.
