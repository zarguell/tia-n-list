Attackers breached the registry operators behind three country-code top-level domains (.gh Ghana, .sl Sierra Leone, .as American Samoa), edited authoritative DNS records, and passed certificate-authority validation to obtain counterfeit TLS certificates for Google domains and other organizations. Google's disclosure puts the count at unauthorized certificates confirmed through Certificate Transparency logs; one report cites 12 forged certificates. The certificates were genuine, CA-signed documents issued to an attacker who could demonstrate DNS control rather than real ownership.

The technique bypasses the trust model rather than breaking encryption. Domain validation for HTTPS issuance typically relies on a TXT record challenge; control over authoritative DNS answers that challenge, so the attacker obtained valid certificates for domains they never controlled. Because ccTLDs issue domains to anyone regardless of geography, this exposed users well beyond the three countries. Successful abuse enables convincing brand impersonation with a padlock in the browser.

Google blocked the unauthorized certificates for its properties in Chrome via CRLSets and worked with the issuing CAs to revoke them. The company states its own systems were not compromised and that it has no reason to believe the CAs acted improperly. Ars Technica first reported the story on October 6; Google published its Chrome response on October 8.

What to watch: CT logs for additional certificates issued under .gh, .sl, or .as, whether the other affected organizations disclose, and whether the ccTLD operators publish incident detail. Registry-level compromise of this kind is rare and the response playbook for it, CA/Browser Forum side, is still unsettled.
