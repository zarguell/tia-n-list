Attackers compromised the third-party operators behind three country-code top-level domains, Ghana (.GH), American Samoa (.AS), and Sierra Leone (.SL), and modified authoritative DNS records. With that control they passed certificate-authority domain validation and obtained 12 unauthorized HTTPS certificates, including for several Google domains plus domains of other organizations under those ccTLDs. Possession of publicly trusted certificates for domains the attacker does not own enables convincing impersonation: a visitor or client trusting the CA chain would land on attacker-controlled infrastructure with a valid padlock. Google states its own systems were not compromised and that it found no sign the issuing CAs acted improperly.

The mechanism matters more than the Google branding. Certificate authorities issue after a domain-control check, typically a TXT record with a challenge value, so whoever edits authoritative DNS for a registry can mint certificates for every domain beneath it. Pointing the affected records at attacker infrastructure also allowed content substitution on the affected domains themselves. Google blocked the unauthorized certificates for its properties in Chrome via CRLSets and worked with the issuing authorities to revoke them, which extends protection to other clients that honor the revocations; the incident surfaced through Certificate Transparency log review.

Watch three things: whether the ccTLD registry operators disclose how access was obtained and whether other registries quietly rotate their DNS and management credentials; whether CT logs turn up further certificates from these registries beyond the 12 reported; and whether the CA/Browser Forum tightens validation or monitoring requirements for ccTLDs with outsourced registry operations. Organizations with .GH, .SL, or .AS domains should review their DNS records and certificate transparency entries for their names.
