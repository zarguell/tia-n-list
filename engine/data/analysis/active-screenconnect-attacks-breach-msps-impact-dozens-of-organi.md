An attacker abused access to multiple cloud-hosted ConnectWise ScreenConnect panels on November 11, 2026 to push rogue ScreenConnect clients to at least 288 endpoints across 48 organizations and 6 managed service providers, hitting all 288 hosts within roughly 90 minutes and about a third in the first 10. The payload is SCKit, a worm-like toolkit providing persistent backdoor access, session-cookie interception and self-replication: it waits for a technician to connect to a compromised endpoint, infects that workstation, then spreads to other sessions and panels. Microsoft described the chain as a batch launcher retrieving a PowerShell decoder that unpacks SCKit with device-specific key material, with persistence through scheduled tasks and registry Run entries and a RelayTrap component aimed at connecting technicians.

Huntress traced initial access to RMM+, a third-party extension that manages ScreenConnect sessions without the console and authenticates with a shared access key; in versions 1.0.31 and 1.0.32 that key defaulted to blank, so the authentication check did nothing. ConnectWise said it found no evidence of compromised ConnectWise infrastructure or a vulnerability in ScreenConnect itself, attributing the activity to abuse of independent third-party extensions. It disabled five third-party extensions across roughly 400 partner environments, advised on-premises customers to disable them manually, and warned that disabling extensions does not remove established malware or persistence. After completing its review, ConnectWise recommended technician clients running 26.6.5 or later.

The recovered toolkit also carries exploit code for CVE-2026-84869, a CVSS 9.9 previously patched ScreenConnect flaw. Early reporting said Huntress and Blackpoint Cyber had not observed successful use during these incidents and that the exploit failed against a patched client in testing. Later reporting described a November 12 compromise in which a guest-pushed batch file notified a Telegram bot, downloaded an installer and established a rogue ScreenConnect connection in about nine seconds, so the technician-pivot path deserves separate verification from the initial panel abuse. Huntress also found more than 2,200 fake always-online computers with crypto-related names on over 100 ScreenConnect servers.

Affected MSPs should treat every endpoint that received a rogue client as compromised, reimage those hosts, and audit ScreenConnect logs for Windows Script Host or PowerShell execution, Run Command history, extension installs and authentication activity. Any technician who connected to an infected endpoint during the window needs a workstation check before further remote work, and RMM+ should be removed or updated.
