The campaign started on 11 November 2026, when an operator with access to multiple cloud-hosted ConnectWise ScreenConnect panels pushed commands to managed endpoints. Huntress counted at least six compromised MSPs and 288 hosts across 48 organizations, with roughly one-third hit in the first 10 minutes and all of them within about 90 minutes. The attacker installed rogue ScreenConnect clients and a worm-like toolkit Huntress named SCKit, which gives persistent backdoor access, intercepts technician session cookies and waits for a technician to connect before infecting that workstation and spreading to other sessions and panels. Microsoft described a batch launcher, a PowerShell decoder that unpacks SCKit with device-specific key material, scheduled tasks and registry Run persistence, and a RelayTrap component aimed at connecting technicians. ConnectWise disabled five third-party extensions across about 400 partner environments and said it found no compromise of its own infrastructure.

Two initial-access paths run through this story. Huntress traced the November wave to RMM+, a third-party ScreenConnect extension that authenticates with a shared access key instead of user accounts, roles and MFA; versions 1.0.31 and 1.0.32 defaulted that key to blank. A separate wave reported on 17 November has been underway since 20 October, targeting MSP ScreenConnect servers with CVE-2026-84869, a CVSS 9.9 missing-authorization flaw patched on 8 September and added to CISA KEV on 11 September. Huntress saw at least 688,000 guest "run file" requests named update*.bat against 42 MSP servers from 29 IP addresses, with a batch file notifying a Telegram bot and a rogue client connecting within about nine seconds. Earlier reporting noted that researchers had not observed successful CVE exploitation during the SCKit wave and that the exploit failed against a patched client in testing, while later reporting describes confirmed abuse of the flaw.

ConnectWise has closed its investigation and recommends technician clients at 26.6.5 or later, but activity against on-premises environments with the third-party extensions still enabled continues to be reported. Affected builds in the SCKit wave included 26.6.6.9747 and 26.7.6.9783, so a current client alone is not a clean bill of health. Other observations include more than 2,200 fake, always-online computers with crypto-related names on over 100 ScreenConnect servers. Watch for guest Run Command and file-transfer records without matching tickets, new extension installations, Entra credential additions, and any evidence of technician-workstation compromise. Reimaging is the recommended recovery for hosts that ran the stager chain, because disabling extensions does not remove established persistence.
