ConnectWise is investigating a live malware campaign that abuses compromised ScreenConnect environments to push rogue remote-access clients onto customer devices. Huntress first flagged the activity, with its SOC starting to track it at roughly 17:47 UTC on 2026-11-11 and reporting six compromised managed service providers, more than 286 affected endpoints, and 47 organizations impacted, a tally still climbing at the time of reporting. The technique relies on ScreenConnect's Run Command functionality to deploy unauthorized ScreenConnect clients on managed endpoints, giving intruders a remote-access channel that lives outside the legitimate MSP's own tenant. ConnectWise has since disabled five ScreenConnect extensions across roughly 400 partner environments after finding unauthorized access to ScreenConnect partner accounts, and it warns that on-premises customers must disable those extensions manually. Initial access is attributed to compromised credentials or hijacked operator sessions rather than a flaw in ScreenConnect itself, but one link in the chain is defective third-party code: Huntress reports the attackers used RMM+, an extension that manages ScreenConnect sessions through a shared access key, and in versions 1.0.31 and 1.0.32 that key defaults to blank when it is not configured, which allows authentication to be bypassed. No CVE has been assigned to the campaign. Attacker infrastructure named in the reporting includes relay 185.99.255.28, C2 domains newnewnew.gotdns.org and soxnewwork.homedns.org, possible additional C2 cdn007-digitaldownloads.com, secondary C2 sc.payproofkit.com at 155.94.150.39, stager hxxps://ns.mo7sen.dev/EadF.php?p=4, and rogue client IDs 2588ef5c15954b10 and ed630cc2c873b091. Later reporting puts the tally at 288 endpoints across 48 organizations and names the toolkit Huntress calls SCKit, which gives persistent backdoor access, intercepts session cookies and self-replicates by waiting for a technician to connect to a compromised endpoint before infecting that workstation and spreading to other panels. Affected ScreenConnect builds include 26.6.6.9747 and 26.7.6.9783.

The exposure math is the reason this matters more than the average remote-access abuse story: 47 downstream organizations trace back to only six compromised providers, and 286 endpoints trace back to those same six, so a single MSP compromise fans out across many customers and the full blast radius is still being counted. The rogue clients also give the attackers a channel that survives the extension disablement, because they are separate ScreenConnect instances rather than the MSP's own tooling. Huntress notes that sequential attacker-assigned tags suggest additional MSPs may have been compromised earlier, which means the current victim count may be an undercount. A second, potentially more damaging path involves an MSP technician's workstation being compromised when the technician opens a ScreenConnect session against an already-infected customer device, though ConnectWise says those reports are still being confirmed and it is not yet clear whether that reverse-pivot abuses legitimate functionality or a flaw. Related worm-like ScreenConnect activity observed by Huntress in August 2026 shows the same tradecraft class is mature, not novel.

Defenders should treat this as a credential and session-control incident first and a patching incident second, since the abuse runs through legitimate ScreenConnect functions and the only defective component identified, the RMM+ extension, is third-party code that should be removed rather than patched. Containment steps from ConnectWise are to verify the five extensions are disabled, with on-premises partners disabling them immediately, and to avoid re-enabling or reinstalling them pending further guidance. Partners should review authentication logs, extension installation records, administrative activity, Run Command history, and Toolbox records for suspicious activity, and should revoke active ScreenConnect and identity-provider sessions, reset relevant credentials, enforce MFA, and use unique passwords. Technicians should avoid interactive ScreenConnect sessions with potentially compromised devices and investigate through separate security tooling instead, including any technician workstations that connected to those devices. Hunt teams should look for the rogue client IDs 2588ef5c15954b10 and ed630cc2c873b091, relay 185.99.255.28, the named C2 domains, stager hxxps://ns.mo7sen.dev/EadF.php?p=4, installer pattern `/Bin/ScreenConnect.ClientSetup.msi?...&c=ExploitedN`, and files Panel-Lateral.ps1, runnew.cmd, ToolboxRelayUploader.exe, and Run-Lateral-Full.bat, and should reimage endpoints where a rogue client is confirmed rather than simply removing the client.

The assessment changes if the reverse-pivot turns out to be a vulnerability in ScreenConnect itself, which would widen the scope beyond compromised partner accounts and could force emergency patching across every ScreenConnect deployment. Other developments to watch are the final victim count, whether attackers move to unmanaged remote-access tooling once the five extensions are disabled, and whether the July 2026 batch script that VirusTotal recorded referencing one of the rogue client IDs and the August 2026 worm-like activity connect to the same actor behind this campaign.
