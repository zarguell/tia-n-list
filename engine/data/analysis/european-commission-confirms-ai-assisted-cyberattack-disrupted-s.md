The Commission says an intrusion detected on 22 October and confirmed on 26 October disrupted internal services for more than 10 days, taking Sysper, MyPMO and translation systems offline; spokesperson Thomas Regnier told reporters an AI tool was used but declined to name it, attribute the activity or confirm a ransom demand, and the investigation has found no evidence of a staff data breach. Earlier reporting closes part of that gap: Politico's 19 November Playbook cited an EU official saying CERT-EU had detected international hackers using the publicly available ARTEX framework and that such actors had targeted the Commission, while Euronews identified the tool as ARTEX. Attribution and data access remain unconfirmed by the Commission, so the honest reading is an unnamed intruder with a named tool class, not an unqualified denial.

This matters beyond downtime because ARTEX turns a natural-language objective into autonomous reconnaissance and exploitation. The FBI's 13 November FLASH says some ARTEX intrusions went from initial targeting to privileged access in under three hours, and it documents a South Korean financial campaign that exfiltrated personal data on about 67,000 people plus a ConnectWise Cloud Backup compromise with attempted sign-ins to more than 2,250 downstream tenants. Censys counted 1,465 exposed ARTEX consoles on 4 November, 155 of which also ran at least one other detected threat. If a similar agent reached HR and reimbursement systems, the consequence is not a delayed payslip but a reachable catalog of names, pay grades, home addresses and contact details, with internal documents held separately according to Euronews. That is the exposure the union is pressing the Commission to bound, and the reason this story outranks a routine outage.

Defenders should treat this as automation over known surfaces, not a CVE to patch. CERT-EU's 5 November guidance calls for isolating management panels, emergency patching of internet-exposed assets, MFA on critical systems and hunting for automated scanning patterns and ARTEX artifacts; its technique mapping covers T1595, T1588.002 and T1190. The FBI's 43-technique alert gives the concrete hunt: Azure SAS tokens, service principals, Kudu credentials and storage keys checked in under a second, administrator passwords in JavaScript bundles, unrotated signing keys used to forge tokens, LAPS passwords read after more than 110,000 Active Directory objects were enumerated, and DCSync against KRBTGT. Sweep configurations, pipelines and shipped front-end bundles for those secrets, rotate what is found, restrict deployment and publishing credentials, and alert on new OAuth credentials, SSH keys, privileged role assignments and spikes in API traffic. Check your own range for exposed ARTEX consoles and open directories, since Censys found 32 leaking post-exploitation kits and stagers, and 155 of the observed hosts also carried Vshell, Cobalt Strike or Adaptix C2.

The assessment changes on two findings: confirmed data access on Sysper, MyPMO and the translation systems, and an on-record attribution of the tool. Watch for a Commission or CERT-EU post-incident review that names ARTEX, and treat the timeline as unresolved until the date conflict is settled: SecurityWeek reports confirmation on 26 October, Euronews quotes Regnier saying detection and confirmation both happened on 22 October. Also watch the tooling supply: the developer pulled the GitHub repository on 8 October, but CERT-EU and the FBI both describe surviving forks, archives and a commercial ARTEX PRO, so a takedown alone will not shrink the exposure.
