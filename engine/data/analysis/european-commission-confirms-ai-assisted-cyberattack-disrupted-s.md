The European Commission detected the attack on October 22 and confirmed it on October 26. Reporting described more than 10 days of disruption to the Sysper HR platform, MyPMO medical reimbursement system and translation services. Most applications had been restored by November 19, although Sysper failed again and the JSIS health portal remained unavailable at that point. The Commission confirmed use of an AI tool but did not name it; Politico and CERT-EU identified ARTEX as the suspected framework, with CERT-EU assessing similar activity against EU institutions as highly likely to involve it.

On November 23, spokesperson Thomas Regnier confirmed a further consequence: names and email addresses of external EU Academy users had leaked. The learning platform had been taken offline on November 11 and was expected to remain closed until January 14. The European Data Protection Supervisor was notified. The exact number of affected users remained under investigation, so the Commission posted a general warning on the site rather than contacting individuals directly. This was distinct from its earlier statement that it had found no evidence of a breach affecting Commission staff broadly.

The incident confirms AI-assisted tradecraft against administrative systems, while reporting links it to an off-the-shelf AI penetration-testing framework. The EU Academy leak turns that exposure into a direct phishing and identity-theft risk for users. Microsoft attributed broader ARTEX campaigns to Storm-0940, but the Commission has not attributed its own incident. Watch for actor attribution, a final affected-user count, further service restoration and evidence that low-severity findings were chained into privileged access.
