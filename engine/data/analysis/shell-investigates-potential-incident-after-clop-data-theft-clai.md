Clop is running a mass data-theft campaign against internet-exposed PTC Windchill and FlexPLM instances, exploiting CVE-2026-12569, an improper input validation flaw. In its latest leak-site drop the gang listed 43 new victims at once, including Shell, General Electric, and Philips, and claims 89GB taken from Shell: engineering drawings, scans of facility testing reports, photos of facilities, and project plans. For GE and Philips it advertises backups, system files, projects, drawings, diagrams, and blueprints. Shell says only that it is aware of a potential incident and has activated its response procedures, with no reported disruption to refinery, drilling, or production networks. Philips has gone furthest, confirming a compromise of a specific enterprise server that it says is contained with no customer impact, while GE is still assessing. The CVE sits on CISA's KEV catalog, and the batch-of-43 listing pattern points to automated exploitation at scale rather than hand-picked targets.

The real exposure here is not "energy companies," it is every organization running Windchill or FlexPLM on the public internet, and the damage model is not PII or payment cards. PLM repositories hold the drawings, test results, and facility layouts that describe how plants and products are actually built, so a successful theft is a competitive and physical-security loss for an oil major that operates tens of thousands of sites serving 20 million customers a day. Treat the 89GB figure with skepticism: Philips' own statement describes an attempted, contained compromise while Clop lists it as a victorious victim, which shows the gang counts partial or failed access in its totals and that listing size is a ransom-pressure number, not a forensic one. The cadence supports that reading; a day before the Shell listing, Clop claimed 8TB from Zebra, complete with the target's revenue, wording built for negotiation leverage rather than verification.
