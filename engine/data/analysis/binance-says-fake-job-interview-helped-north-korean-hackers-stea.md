On October 29, 2026, attackers drained roughly $6.3 billion (TRM Labs calculated $6.36 billion) from Binance hot wallets across about ten blockchains in roughly 20 minutes, the largest cryptocurrency theft on record at about four times the $1.5 billion Bybit heist of February 2025. The FBI attributed the theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor group, also tracked as Lazarus Group, Jade Sleet, and APT38, and released 628 associated wallet addresses for blocking. Binance traced the intrusion to a months-long campaign that compromised engineers' endpoints rather than leaking wallet private keys: in June a Wallet DevOps engineer downloaded a tampered Homebrew installer while searching for an AI coding tool, and in September a second engineer was recruited through a fictitious company's fake interviews and tricked into running a malicious coding assignment. Attackers then accessed a service container and Binance's wallet signing infrastructure, used the EIP-7702 mechanism on Ethereum to delegate wallet behavior, and pushed unauthorized withdrawals. About $2.8 to $3 billion was frozen on-chain by Tether, Circle, and blockchain partners, and Binance pledged to cover remaining losses from the SAFU fund and company funds while suspending deposits and withdrawals during remediation.

The operation was staged in advance: TRM Labs found the operational wallet funded through small Tornado Cash withdrawals and the malicious code trialed on other wallets before going live, and Chainalysis traced rapid cross-chain outflows beginning around 4:20 p.m. ET. Binance acknowledged multiple breach points beyond the initially cited laptop and an internal AI tool's role, while noting no evidence of leaked private keys. The DPRK denied involvement through state media. RUSI commentary framed the incident around private-sector gatekeepers' growing role in tracing and interrupting illicit flows, questioning whether FATF keeps pace.

What to watch: laundering of the unfrozen remainder through DeFi platforms and instant swap services, follow-on phishing and fake support scams targeting Binance users, the pending technical post-mortem, and whether additional engineers or systems were compromised. The case underscores endpoint and developer-laptop security: API keys, cloud tokens, and SSH credentials on engineer machines can enable lateral movement into signing infrastructure.
