WhiteArmor, a data-extortion crew that first surfaced in late October 2026, posted Maersk to its leak site on 2026-11-07, claiming roughly 100 GB exfiltrated from the OCF / LF Logistics platform that Maersk absorbed in its acquisition of LF Logistics, and demanding $5,000,000. There is no CVE and no encryption event; this is theft and extortion. Maersk confirms only that a system belonging to a previously acquired company was breached, that current evidence shows no sensitive data disclosed, and that the incident is isolated to a local system with no indication of impact on core Maersk systems or on customers and partners connecting through APIs and EDI.

That containment statement is only as good as the boundary between the legacy unit and the rest of Maersk, and Maersk has not named the system, the data types, or the initial access vector. Maersk runs 700 container ships, about 13.5 percent of global fleet capacity, 60 terminals in 35 countries and 100,000 employees, with $54 billion in revenue last year, so a breach that stays inside an acquired logistics subsidiary is a manageable incident while one that reaches the booking, API or EDI layer becomes a supply-chain event for every shipper plugged into that carrier. This story matters more than a routine leak-site post because WhiteArmor's claims usually go unconfirmed, yet here the victim itself acknowledged the intrusion, which converts an extortion listing into the first verified breach against a top-tier carrier. One detail the event list does not spell out: the $5,000,000 demand is the same figure WhiteArmor attached to Hisense for 19 GB, which points to a flat-rate extortion template rather than a price scaled to Maersk's data, so the dollar amount is not evidence of how much was actually taken. The listing itemizes 16,093 driver records with plaintext passwords, 16,123 staff accounts including 33 super-administrator accounts, and 27,842 mobile device logs; those counts are attacker claims, not Maersk's, and the company has confirmed only unauthorized access and data extraction from the legacy system.

Defenders outside Maersk should treat the exposed asset as the LF Logistics / OCF platform and rotate any credentials that transit it or that Maersk-related integrations hold, since one leak-site claim references roughly 16,000 driver passwords allegedly stored in plain text, unverified but consistent with a bulk-credential haul. Customers integrating through Maersk APIs and EDI should confirm no shared secrets flow through the affected unit and watch for anomalous booking or shipment queries. The concrete hunt is external for now: monitor ransomware.live, which recorded the Maersk listing on 2026-11-07T03:48:00Z, and WhiteArmor's leak site for the promised 100 GB drop and for follow-on victim names, because that drop is the only independent test of Maersk's "no sensitive data disclosed" line.

Two developments change the assessment. First, publication of the claimed data, especially if it contains plaintext credentials that reach Maersk's booking or EDI estate rather than only the isolated subsidiary. Second, any additional shipping or logistics victim named by WhiteArmor, which would mark this as a sector campaign against a common platform rather than a single opportunistic intrusion.
