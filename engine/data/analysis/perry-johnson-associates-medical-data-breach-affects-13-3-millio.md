The case is a civil settlement, not a new intrusion. Perry Johnson & Associates, a Nevada medical transcription vendor, and four related entities agreed on November 6, 2026 to pay $300,000 and accept a corrective action plan, with up to $9.7 million in added penalties suspended if they miss its terms; the New York Attorney General's office, joined by Connecticut, Michigan, and New Jersey, describes the deal as a $10 million settlement. The underlying breach ran from March 27 to May 2, 2023, and PJ&A learned of it on May 2 only when the attackers contacted the company to demand payment. The settlement documents put the toll at more than 13.3 million people, including roughly 4.2 million New York residents, up from the 8,952,212 individuals PJ&A reported to the HHS Office for Civil Rights in November 2023. The findings describe remote access without multifactor authentication, nearly identical passwords circulated by email, sensitive files without file-level encryption, and security software that detected malicious activity but never alerted staff.

The number that matters is not the $300,000. It is the gap between the upfront payment and the $9.7 million suspended penalty, which is the only lever that makes the corrective plan enforceable. A successful attack on a transcription vendor is not a single-record exposure: PJ&A held data for hospitals and health systems across the country, and by January 2024 clients had already reported the breach as affecting more than 14 million people, surpassing the 11 million-record HCA Healthcare incident as the largest U.S. health sector breach of 2023. The settlement's 13.3 million figure is lower than that 2024 tally, which suggests the parties settled on a number tied to what PJ&A itself reported rather than the full client-reported count. The exposed data includes Social Security numbers, diagnoses, test results, medications, and treatment dates, so the victim risk is medical identity theft and fraud, not just credential reuse. This story stands out because it turns the largest health sector breach of 2023 into a compliance baseline with a measurable penalty structure, in a sector where business associates hold sensitive records for the providers they serve.

Defenders should treat the settlement as a checklist. Enforce multifactor authentication on every remote access path, eliminate shared passwords, encrypt sensitive files at rest, and verify that detection tooling actually pages a human. Healthcare organizations that use transcription or other business associates should audit those vendors now: request evidence of MFA and file-level encryption, review business associate agreements, and check the HHS OCR breach portal for any filing that names your entity or its clients. The hunt here is compliance-shaped rather than malware-shaped. No malware family, command-and-control infrastructure, or indicator of compromise was disclosed in the settlement or the 2023 reporting, so there is no IoC to block. Review remote access logs for accounts that predate the MFA mandate and confirm that alerts from security tooling reach an on-call queue.

Two developments would change the assessment. First, whether PJ&A completes the mandated reforms and avoids the suspended $9.7 million, which will show whether the corrective plan is real or paper. Second, whether the federal criminal investigation produces charges, which would move the case from a state civil settlement to a federal prosecution and could surface the attacker infrastructure that has never been publicly identified.
