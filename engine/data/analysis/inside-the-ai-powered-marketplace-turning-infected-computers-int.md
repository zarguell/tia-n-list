The marketplace advertised on DarkForums on November 14 turns infostealer logs into plain-language victim profiles, with natural-language search over owner, employer, applications and access plus country, operating system and price filters. The seller claims tens of millions of logs, more than one billion parsed accounts and prices from $0.25; a sample query for "citrix access to healthcare provider" returned a $10 Canadian clinic listing showing 11 passwords and 574 cookies. Hudson Rock matched a Firefox profile identifier in that listing's file preview to a Cavalier record, and its Enki agent tied the artifacts to a reception workstation with Citrix and billing records. The inventory figures and profile confidence remain seller assertions, not verified facts.

This is not a new stealer or a new breach wave. Automated victim profiling was already documented in September's Bee Stealer v2.1, which wrote ProfileEn.txt and ProfileRu.txt, and in BI.ZONE's October review of Bee Stealer and AVENGER, so the shift here is placement: the ranking layer has moved into the storefront where buyers search by role instead of by URL. That changes the unit of sale from a raw credential to an inferred job and access level, which lets a buyer turn a family PC or shared clinic workstation into a corporate-access target without knowing the victim. Lunar's November 16 comparison found the catalog nearly identical to Russian Market's, including 14,125,159 versus 14,090,067 listed logs and the same 381 country values, which makes this look like cheaper retrieval over existing exposure rather than fresh inventory. A $9 defense-contractor household listing scored 100/100 and a $10 government-employee family profile matter because of how they are discoverable, not because either proves an intrusion.

Treat this as an exposure-management trigger, not a malware signature alert. Query internal and licensed infostealer data for corporate domains, Citrix and billing URLs, Firefox profile identifiers and other file-preview artifacts, and hunt for Bee Stealer output files ProfileEn.txt and ProfileRu.txt where that stealer is in scope; keep credential-save dates separate from compromise dates and marketplace listing dates. Revoke affected sessions and tokens, reset exposed or reused credentials from a trusted device, and review authentication and audit logs for unfamiliar sessions, account changes and access to sensitive resources. Include home and shared machines in scope, because the profiles deliberately blur personal and corporate use. Blocking unauthorized outbound transfers still matters: the profiling happens after exfiltration, so logs that never leave the device cannot be catalogued.

The assessment changes if the inventory and freshness claims are independently confirmed, or if the profiles are shown to map reliably to real employees and working sessions rather than to noisy browser clues. Operator attribution to Russian Market, evidence of a supply relationship, or copycat storefronts adding the same search layer would also raise the priority; a takedown of the storefront alone would not remove the underlying logs.
