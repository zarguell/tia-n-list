The DeadLock ransomware operation has moved its infrastructure onto blockchain-backed services. Microsoft's research describes a setup where the Polygon blockchain stores configuration data and leak-site posts, and the victim chat page resolves its current proxy address by querying a smart contract instead of using a fixed Tor URL. DeadLock also uses the decentralized Session network for victim communications and hosts stolen files on Wasabi cloud storage.

The design means operators can rotate their chat and C2 infrastructure with a blockchain transaction, with no change needed on the victim side, which makes takedown and sinkholing far harder. DeadLock emerged in mid-2025, uses double extortion, and by July had listed 80 mostly European victims across IT, mining, transportation, manufacturing, hospitality, and consumer goods. Microsoft observed deployment by affiliates previously linked to the Lynx and INC ecosystems. Organizations should expect blockchain-anchored resilience to become more common in ransomware operations and should not count on infrastructure seizure to end a campaign.

A new technical analysis from SocFortress adds detail on the encryptor itself. DeadLock is written in Rust, which gives it a memory-safe, modular base that evades signature detection while staying cross-platform. Its recovery architecture leans on Web3 technology and onion-routed messaging rather than a centralized C2, so the group keeps its extortion workflows running even after domain seizures. The same report puts the victim count at more than 80 organizations listed on the DeadLock blog as of mid-2026, over half in Europe, with hits across IT, manufacturing, mining, transportation, and hospitality, and links current operators to affiliates of the Lynx and INC ransomware ecosystems.

Watch whether the affiliate model expands the victim base faster than the leak site suggests, and whether other ransomware groups copy the blockchain-anchored takedown resistance. The practical takeaway is unchanged: infra seizure alone will not stop DeadLock, so defenses should assume the negotiation and leak infrastructure stays up and focus on the pre-encryption behavior, which disables Windows Defender, deletes backups, and clears event logs before the encryptor runs.
