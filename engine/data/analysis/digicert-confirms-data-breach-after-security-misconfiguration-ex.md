DigiCert confirmed unauthorized access to an internal business system containing archived customer information, caused by a server migration in February 2025 that mistakenly exposed the application to the internet for about 16 months. Attackers abused a self-registration feature in the system's account manager, which did not require multi-factor authentication, to create a read-only account and accessed archived customer data between June 27 and July 2, 2026. DigiCert blocked the unauthorized account on July 2, initially treating the incident as an account-creation event, then began suspecting data access on October 12 and launched a broader investigation. The exposed data includes names, business email addresses, phone numbers, and business addresses, and for some customers driver's licenses, passports, utility bills, and other documents containing a name and address. The access was read-only and could not write records, act as a validation approver, or issue certificates, and DigiCert says its certificate authority systems run on separate secure network segments and were not affected. The company filed the incident through Mozilla's Bugzilla on October 21 and has begun sending breach notifications while still reviewing identification documents to determine the full affected population.

The 16-month exposure window is the notable operational failure: a routine network-segment move in February 2025 left an internal archive reachable from the internet, and the self-registration feature intended for another purpose provided an unauthenticated entry point. The lag between blocking the account in July and recognizing data access in October suggests detection gaps in monitoring the internal business system. Because DigiCert is a certificate authority, the exposed records are verification documents submitted for certificate enrollment, which raises the stakes for affected customers even though no certificates were issued or compromised.

What to watch: the final count of affected customers once the identification-document review is complete; whether the self-registration feature is removed or gated behind MFA across all internal systems; and whether any misuse of the exposed PII and identity documents surfaces. Customers who submitted verification documents to DigiCert should treat those documents as potentially compromised and monitor for identity fraud. The incident does not affect certificate trust, but it does erode confidence in the CA's internal data handling.
