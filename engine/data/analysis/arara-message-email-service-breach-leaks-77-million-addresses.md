Arara Message, a Japanese bulk email delivery service owned by PayCloud Holdings, is disclosing a breach that reached roughly 77 million email addresses across about 380 corporate clients and up to 97 servers. The first report, based on PayCloud's initial disclosure, put the loss at about 3.01 million addresses from one shared system and cited a web server vulnerability; a later disclosure revised the impact to 77 million after the intrusion spread to additional servers. Reconnaissance began October 27, unauthorized logins started October 28, the service was suspended October 31, and about 7.13 million phishing emails impersonating a credit card company were sent from compromised Arara infrastructure between October 28 and 30. Arara invalidated stolen API keys and administrative passwords.

SEGA, a downstream client, confirmed on November 9 that 2,196,044 customer email addresses were stolen through Arara, including 92,273 records with newsletter delivery information, across 11 SEGA and SEGA Fave newsletters. SEGA said the exposed data did not include names, dates of birth, addresses, phone numbers, credit card details, or account passwords, and that SEGA Account addresses sit on a separate distribution system. Arara had told SEGA on October 29 that no data was compromised before reversing that assessment on November 3.

The supply-chain dimension is the real exposure: every downstream brand trusted Arara with its subscriber list, so one vendor breach cascaded into roughly 380 companies, and the stolen addresses were then used as both targets and delivery channel for the 7.13 million-message phishing campaign. Public analysis has tied the intrusion to the PHP2Shell flaws CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, fixed in PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35, although Arara's own disclosures cite only a web server vulnerability and system-environment issues. GreyNoise counted at least six successful exploitations of those flaws after they entered CISA's KEV catalog, so the runtime exposure is active independent of Arara.

Watch for further downstream breach notices, confirmed misuse of the leaked address sets, and any enforcement action by Japan's Personal Information Protection Commission. Defenders should treat this as both a vendor-risk and a runtime-patching incident: rotate API keys and tokens issued to Arara, review management-console logs for the October 27 to 31 window, and pre-brief users on phishing that names Arara customers or references credit-card statements.
