A threat actor using the ShinyHunters handle on a newly emerged underground forum listed alleged Binance data covering 335 million users (335.9 million lines per ThreatMon) for $900,000, claiming KYC documents, account passwords, wallet private keys, transaction history, emails, and phone numbers. Binance denies any breach, stating it found no evidence that systems, customer KYC data, credentials, private keys, or internal infrastructure were compromised, and notes the exchange does not store passwords in plaintext, does not hold private keys for external wallets, and no longer operates in some referenced regions. The listing's internal-consistency failures and the $900,000 price point are the tell: a genuine 335-million-record dump from the world's largest exchange would command far more, and the combination of identity documents, wallet keys, and infrastructure details in one bundle is more consistent with recycled infostealer data or a scam than a single extraction.

The real exposure is not a Binance database leak but customer-side credential reuse and phishing: Brandefense observed near-identical advertisements circulating on Telegram from October 30 to November 2, 2026, and comparable past listings have involved infostealer malware harvested from users' own devices. That means even a fully false listing can still drive credential-stuffing and extortion attempts against customers whose endpoints were already compromised, and the 335.9 million line count is a marketing number, not a count of affected individuals. The story matters because it separates a vendor breach from endpoint theft, and the wrong response (mass password resets, panic withdrawals) costs more than the listing itself.

Defenders and users should treat this as an infostealer and phishing readiness drill: enforce MFA and hardware-key login where possible, rotate any password reused across Binance and other services, and verify all communications through official Binance channels rather than links in unsolicited messages. Hunt on the endpoint side for stealer artifacts and unexpected browser credential access, and review Binance account activity for unusual API key creation or withdrawal attempts; no Binance-side patch or hotfix is applicable because no Binance system was compromised. Binance's own guidance is to never disclose passwords, 2FA codes, seed phrases, or private keys.

Watch whether any sample from the listing is independently validated, whether the seller is identified as an impersonator or the real ShinyHunters group, and whether Binance issues customer notifications or MFA-hardening guidance. A validated sample would convert this from a scam assessment into an incident requiring a different response.
