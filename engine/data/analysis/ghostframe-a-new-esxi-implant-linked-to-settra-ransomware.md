GhostFrame is a previously undocumented command-and-control and post-exploitation framework for compromised VMware ESXi hosts, disclosed by Recorded Future's Insikt Group on November 10, 2026 and attributed to TAG-218, a threat activity group whose operations overlap with Settra ransomware. It requires pre-existing root access to ESXi and does not exploit a vulnerability; the implant, named vmhostd-core, persists at /opt/vmhostd-core by modifying /etc/rc.local.d/local.sh, disables the ESXi firewall and the execInstalledOnly control roughly every five seconds, and runs /sbin/auto-backup.sh to survive reboots. C2 uses simultaneous TCP connections across ports 53, 2222, 3389, 4444, 8443, and 9443, then acts as an in-memory shellcode loader. Capabilities include VM enumeration, power and snapshot control, VNC relay, an ESXi Host Client tunnel, a SOCKS5 proxy named gfsox, credential interception via sdkmon, and VMkatz extraction of NTLM, WDigest, Kerberos, SAM and LSA secrets, and NTDS.dit data from VM memory and disks. TAG-218 targeted VPN appliances across the US and Europe from late August through October 2026, predominantly SonicWall, Palo Alto Networks, and Fortinet devices; four of those organizations later appeared on the Settra leak site, and on October 12, 2026 a Fortinet VPN appliance was observed beaconing to both GhostFrame and a MeshCentral server at 193.5.65.114 that Huntress had previously attributed to Settra intrusions.

The risk is positional rather than incremental: one compromised ESXi host exposes every guest VM and its data without per-guest malware, so a single root compromise becomes a platform for credential theft, VM takeover, and lateral movement across the whole virtualized estate. That makes GhostFrame more than a routine post-exploitation tool; it converts virtualization infrastructure into a persistent, multi-tenant access point that ransomware operators can ride into encryption. The observed cluster is small, with four published victims, but the technique maps to a documented broader pattern in which attackers pivot from internet-facing VPN appliances into vCenter and ESXi and disable execInstalledOnly, so any organization running ESXi with reachable management or shared VPN credentials is in the blast radius. The story matters more than the average ransomware item because it shows Settra-linked operators maturing from MeshAgent-based Windows intrusions in July through September 2026 into hypervisor-level tooling, signaling that ESXi is now an explicit target rather than collateral.

There is no ESXi patch for GhostFrame because it is post-exploitation tooling, so remediation is access control and detection. Restrict ESXi management to trusted systems, enforce MFA on hypervisor and VPN administrative access, and investigate unexpected privileged access or configuration changes. Hunt for persistence by checking modifications to /etc/rc.local.d/local.sh, the presence of /opt/vmhostd-core or a vmhostd process, ESXi firewall or execInstalledOnly being set to 0, and /sbin/auto-backup.sh preserving changes. Network defenders should look for six near-simultaneous TCP SYNs from an ESXi host to the same destination IP across ports 53, 2222, 3389, 4444, 8443, and 9443; indicators of compromise include 45.158.196.81, 45.158.196.141, 193.5.65.114, and SHA-256 a05eae6d07b82e2e5451a72266f21fbaf76486ef4920a820988de93e15353a10. Audit SonicWall, Palo Alto, and Fortinet VPN appliances for compromise, apply PAN-OS CVE-2026-0257 fixes where relevant, and rotate credentials reachable from affected hypervisors, including the NTDS.dit, SAM, and LSA material that VMkatz can extract.

Two developments would change the assessment: whether GhostFrame spreads beyond TAG-218 and Settra to other ransomware affiliates or initial access brokers, which Insikt Group considers highly likely given its generalized ESXi capabilities, and whether GhostFrame appears in environments without prior VPN compromise, which would indicate an additional initial-access vector beyond the VPN-to-hypervisor chain currently documented.
