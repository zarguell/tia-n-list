WIRED reporters Matt Burgess and Lily Hay Newman described two unrelated, unauthenticated databases that together exposed more than 3.5 billion records. UpGuard researcher Greg Pollock found a single-node Elasticsearch 8.13.0 cluster in Docker with 99 indices, 1.64 billion documents and about 259 GB of data. Its index families included eryaosu_*, sanyaosu_*, alipay_v1, person_v1, xueji_* and enterprise_v1. UpGuard estimated at least 840 million distinct Chinese national ID numbers, nearly 300 million phone records, 11 million student enrollment records and 7.8 million business registrations. Pollock could not identify an owner and said the data was still being updated in the week before discovery.

The second store, found by Jeremiah Fowler, held roughly two billion credentials without encryption or a password. It contained email addresses, login names, passwords, session tokens, cookies and device information across 8,471 plaintext folders, some divided by country, plus about 400,000 documents in per-victim folders associated with more advanced malware. Fowler also saw thousands of Tor .onion addresses alongside account names and passwords. He did not download the files, and the hosting provider restricted access after he reported the exposure. ExpressVPN and later reports repeated the two billion figure without establishing a larger victim count.

Neither researcher could determine how long the databases were open or whether other parties copied the contents. Pollock's cluster became inaccessible during the investigation, possibly as part of a ransomware attack or destructive campaign. One trove is an identity lookup index and the other is a credential and malware-output dump, so the practical risks include account takeover, reuse of exposed passwords and identity fraud. Affected users should change credentials and revoke sessions from a clean device, while organizations should audit internet-facing Elasticsearch and storage services for unauthenticated access.
