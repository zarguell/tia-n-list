WIRED reporters Matt Burgess and Lily Hay Newman described two unrelated, unauthenticated databases that together exposed more than 3.5 billion records. UpGuard researcher Greg Pollock found a single-node Elasticsearch 8.13.0 cluster in Docker with 99 indices, 1.64 billion documents and about 259 GB of data. The index families included eryaosu_*, sanyaosu_*, alipay_v1, person_v1, xueji_* and enterprise_v1, and UpGuard estimated at least 840 million distinct Chinese national ID numbers, nearly 300 million phone records, 11 million student enrollment records and 7.8 million business registrations. The data was still being updated in the week before discovery, and Pollock could not identify an owner.

The second store, found by Jeremiah Fowler, held roughly two billion credentials without encryption or a password. It contained email addresses, login names, passwords, session tokens, cookies and device information across 8,471 plaintext folders, some split by country, plus about 400,000 documents with per-victim folders tied to what looked like advanced malware. Fowler also saw thousands of Tor .onion addresses alongside account names and passwords. He did not download the files; the hosting provider restricted access after he reported the exposure.

Neither researcher could establish how long the data was open or whether other parties copied it. Pollock's database became inaccessible during the investigation, which the reporting describes as possibly part of a ransomware attack or destructive campaign. Those unknowns matter more than the headline count: one trove is an identity lookup index built on national ID numbers and phone records, the other is a credential and malware-output dump. Anyone whose credentials or identity data appear in either set should treat password reuse and stale session tokens as live risk, and organizations should check for exposed Elasticsearch nodes and unauthenticated storage services in their own environments.
