Two unrelated unauthenticated databases, not one breach and not one software exploit, held more than 3.5 billion records. UpGuard's Greg Pollock found a public single-node Elasticsearch 8.13.0 cluster in Docker with 99 indices and 1.64 billion documents, about 259.3 GB, organized as an illicit Chinese identity-lookup service. The index families included `eryaosu_*`, `sanyaosu_*`, `alipay_v1`, `person_v1`, `xueji_*` and `enterprise_v1`, with at least 840 million distinct national ID numbers, nearly 300 million phone records, 11.2 million student records and 7.8 million business registrations. Black Hills Information Security researcher Jeremiah Fowler found a separate passwordless, unencrypted database with about 2 billion credentials in 8,471 plaintext folders, including cookies, session tokens, device data and about 400,000 per-victim documents. No CVE or named actor is attached; Fowler said there was no indication individual companies were breached to compile the data. Pollock said the China trove was still being updated shortly before discovery and then went inaccessible, possibly amid a ransomware or destructive campaign, while Fowler said he did not download the files and could not establish who else accessed them.

The headline sum hides two different failure modes that matter more than the 3.5 billion count. The China data is a permanent identity layer: national ID numbers encode birth date, sex and registration place, and they cannot be rotated after exposure, so name, ID and phone triples can feed telecom fraud, account resets, mule accounts and scams that target children and the deceased. The credential set is the opposite, a live access layer where stolen cookies and session tokens can outlast a password reset and defeat a login flow even after MFA is enabled. That is why this story outranks a routine breach notice: it pairs a population-scale identity corpus with a malware-derived collection that included hundreds of millions of mainstream email accounts, more than 100 million social media accounts and tens of millions of .gov, .edu and .org addresses, and neither owner nor exposure window is known.

No vendor patch fixes an exposed index, so defenders should verify that Elasticsearch 8.13.0 and similar clusters require authentication, are not reachable from untrusted networks, and cannot be read anonymously at the index level. Hunt for clusters exposing the named indices and for access logs showing external queries against `eryaosu_*`, `sanyaosu_*`, `alipay_v1`, `person_v1`, `xueji_*` and `enterprise_v1`, plus exports with folder names applications, cookies, edge, important files and operating systems and files labeled system, process, clipboard, brute force and passwords. Where organizational accounts overlap the credential trove, force password resets, revoke active cookies and session tokens, and review authentication events for logins paired with new device information or .onion-linked account names.

The assessment changes if access logs show third-party reads before the hosting provider restricted the databases, or if the China-linked cluster is confirmed as copied rather than destroyed. A second trigger is evidence that the stolen cookies and session tokens were replayed at scale; that would move this from an exposure story to an active account-takeover campaign and would make token revocation more urgent than password resets.
