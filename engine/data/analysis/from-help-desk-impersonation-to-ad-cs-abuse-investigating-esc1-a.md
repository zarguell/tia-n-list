Microsoft Threat Intelligence investigated two human-operated intrusions that began with help-desk impersonation over Teams: attackers messaged and called users posing as IT support, then socially engineered them into granting interactive remote access through Quick Assist. No Teams vulnerability was exploited. Once connected, the operators staged portable Python, Bun, or Java runtimes, performed host and network discovery, and assessed Active Directory Certificate Services for certificate-based authentication paths before diverging at the certificate layer.

In the first case the actor abused an ESC1-vulnerable certificate template to mint authentication certificates for privileged accounts and performed DCSync. In the second, the actor used PetitPotam to coerce a Windows server into authenticating, then relayed the domain controller's NTLM authentication to AD CS Web Enrollment (ESC8) to obtain a certificate for the domain controller. These two paths reflect the ESC1 through ESC8 misconfiguration classes documented by SpecterOps in their Certified Pre-Owned research, with PetitPotam disclosed by topotam in July 2021 and mitigated by Microsoft's KB5005413 guidance on disabling NTLM on AD CS web enrollment endpoints and enabling Extended Protection for Authentication.

The two intrusions shared the same social-engineering entry but produced different outcomes at the certificate layer, underscoring that AD CS misconfigurations remain a reliable privilege-escalation path years after disclosure. Defenders should audit certificate templates for ESC1 conditions, harden AD CS web enrollment against NTLM relay, and treat help-desk impersonation via Teams as a likely initial-access vector.
