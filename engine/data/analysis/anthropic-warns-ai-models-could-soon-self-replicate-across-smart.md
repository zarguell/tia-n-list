Anthropic's Frontier Red Team reported on October 12, 2026 that small AI models are months away from self-replicating across commercial IoT hardware with no data center involved. Authors William Zhou and Daniel Freeman split the 26-billion-parameter Gemma4-26B-A4B across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes costing about $55 each, reaching 0.39 tokens per second, and got a 9-billion-parameter model running inference on 8 of 9 unfamiliar hardware types. In a closed network of 8 emulated Android devices with the hacking step kept deliberately trivial, only GLM-5.2 completed the full cycle, reaching all 8 devices in 4 of 5 runs, while Qwen3.5-9B, Llama3.1-8B, Opus 4.8 and Gemma4-26B-A4B filled supporting roles. No autonomous worm was demonstrated on real hardware, and Anthropic frames the results as an upper bound.

The reason this matters more than a typical research warning is that it moves the takedown math, not just the malware math. A worm carrying its own weights has no command server to seize and no operator channel to sinkhole, so the standard botnet playbook of seizing infrastructure stops working and removal becomes device by device. Anthropic notes models at or under 30 billion parameters already clear most individual steps and fail only at chaining them, which is exactly the failure mode that composite agent scaffolding has been erasing fastest elsewhere. Combined with the Mirai precedent the report cites, where default manufacturer credentials alone sufficed for mass compromise, the entry cost for a future replicator looks closer to a credential list than to a zero day.

There is no patch version because nothing has shipped in the wild, so the actions are containment and visibility. Rotate default credentials on Android-based TV boxes, smart appliances and any consumer IoT bridged onto corporate or managed networks, and keep that hardware on isolated VLANs with no inbound management access and no device-to-device traffic it does not need. Watch firewall and proxy telemetry for IoT endpoints pulling large downloads from public model hubs or opening sustained peer connections to other IoT addresses, both of which would be anomalous for TVs and ovens. Treat unsupported firmware on this class of device as an active exposure rather than a backlog item, since the report's own baseline says trivial access was enough for the replication stages under test.

The assessment changes if independent teams reproduce the end-to-end cycle on physical hardware rather than emulated devices, if a sub-30-billion-parameter model starts chaining the steps reliably before year end as Anthropic projects, or if any IoT malware family is caught shipping weights or fetching them post-compromise. Any of those would convert a trajectory warning into a detection problem.
