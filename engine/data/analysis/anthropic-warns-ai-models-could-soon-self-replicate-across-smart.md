Anthropic's Frontier Red Team published research on October 12, 2026 (William Zhou and Daniel Freeman) testing whether small AI models could self-replicate across consumer IoT devices with no data center behind them. The team bought 15 off-brand Android TV boxes at about $55 each and split a 26-billion-parameter open-weight model, Gemma4-26B-A4B, across them, where it ran at 0.39 tokens per second. A 9-billion-parameter model got inference running on 8 of 9 unfamiliar hardware types. In a closed network of 8 emulated Android devices, only GLM-5.2 completed the full replication cycle, reaching all 8 devices in 4 of 5 runs; Qwen3.5-9B, Llama3.1-8B, Opus 4.8, and Gemma4-26B-A4B filled supporting roles.

No self-replicating AI worm on real hardware was demonstrated. The hacking step in the emulated cycle was deliberately trivial, and Anthropic frames its results as an upper bound. The significance is trajectory: models of 30 billion parameters or fewer already complete the individual steps but fail when chaining them, and Anthropic estimates self-replication capability could arrive by the end of the year if models keep improving at their historical rate. A worm that carries its own model would have no central point to shut off, though propagation would be slow, with a doubling time of days. The Mirai precedent is the right baseline: past IoT botnets needed little more than default manufacturer credentials.

Watch for independent replication of these results, cheaper edge hardware crossing the tokens-per-second threshold, and IoT malware that actually ships model weights. The attack surface itself is unchanged: exposed consumer devices, default credentials, and unsupported firmware remain the entry points. The report changes what the payload can be, not how defenders should close the front door.
