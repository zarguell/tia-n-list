The incident is not a product vulnerability but a supply-chain compromise of Brevo, the third-party email marketing platform Trezor uses for newsletter campaigns. Attackers exploited a SAML SSO scoping flaw in Brevo to access 138 customer accounts, then used Trezor's legitimate domain to send phishing emails to roughly 347,000 newsletter subscribers with the subject line "Critical Security Alert: STM32 Entropy Vulnerability" and the sender address help@trezor.io. The message claimed a fake hardware-level vulnerability in STM32 microcontrollers could expose seed phrases to brute-force cracking, and it directed recipients to a malicious link. Trezor suspended the Brevo account, took the domain down at the DNS level within 20 minutes, and stated that about 2,500 people had clicked the link before it was disabled. No Trezor wallet or account system was breached.

A successful attack on this campaign means direct theft of cryptocurrency: any user who entered a wallet backup through the linked application surrendered their seed phrase. That makes this story materially different from routine software vulnerability news because the exposure is not theoretical; it is a targeted, domain-authenticated phishing operation against self-custody users who hold high-value assets offline. The 347,000 subscriber count represents a massive pool of potential victims, and the timing compounds the risk because it follows the August ShipMonk breach that exposed order data for 81,000 customers across the U.S., Brazil, Colombia, and Italy. That earlier breach gave attackers names, addresses, emails, and phone numbers, giving the Brevo phishing campaign richer context to craft convincing messages.

Defenders should treat any email referencing "STM32 Entropy Vulnerability" or asking for wallet backups as malicious and delete it immediately. Trezor has confirmed it will never ask for a wallet backup by email. Users who clicked the link and entered any recovery phrase must move funds to a new wallet immediately. On the network side, the malicious domain used in the campaign has been disabled by Trezor, but defenders should still hunt for references to the domain in proxy and DNS logs. Monitor for additional phishing attempts that leverage the 43 Brevo accounts from which contacts were exported, since those lists could fuel secondary campaigns. There are no product patches to apply; the remediation is vendor-side suspension of Brevo and user-side awareness.

Watch for Brevo's full postmortem confirming the full scope of the 138 compromised accounts and any new weaponized accounts. Also monitor whether attackers deploy additional supply-chain campaigns through the 43 accounts that exported contact lists, since those lists include cryptocurrency subscribers beyond Trezor who could receive similar domain-authenticated phishing emails in the coming weeks.
