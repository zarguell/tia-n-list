Sony Network Communications disclosed an unauthorized intrusion into its So-net member database on October 19, 2026, detected October 23, with confirmation of data exfiltration by October 27. The scope is exact: 2,585,481 customer records, split as 2,141,986 So-net entries and 443,495 Plala (S Course) entries, plus 54,401 email-password pairs across Mail Service (G), A-Box, So-net VAIO Mail, and PostPet. The company shut the affected server and blocked attacker traffic on discovery; no banking, payment card, or primary membership passwords were in the compromised dataset.

What makes this more than another ISP notice is the paired exposure of structured identity records and live email credentials in a single incident. With 2.58 million names, birth dates, gender, phone numbers, user IDs, and ISP connection IDs in circulation alongside 54,401 working email passwords, an attacker can chain identity correlation into credential-stuffing or targeted phishing against Plala subscribers served under NTT Docomo contracts. Roughly 293,000 records belong to users who left the services in March 2026 or later, meaning the dataset includes recently active subscribers whose contact details remain current. That concentration raises the value of the leak above a generic historical dump.

Defenders should treat the 54,401 email accounts as actively compromised: Sony reset those passwords on October 27, but users who reused those credentials elsewhere remain exposed. Hunt for logins to Mail Service (G), A-Box, VAIO Mail, and PostPet from new geographies or user-agents after October 19; inspect So-net and Plala subscriber portals for anomalous connection-ID lookups. Network-level restriction is limited here because this was a database breach rather than a live service vulnerability, but users should rotate any shared passwords and watch for phishing that references d POINT Club or Universal Studios Japan campaigns, which NTT Docomo warned about in the same notification chain.

Watch for confirmation of the initial access vector and whether the 54,401 email-password pairs appear in public credential dumps; either would shift the assessment from contained disclosure to active misuse.
