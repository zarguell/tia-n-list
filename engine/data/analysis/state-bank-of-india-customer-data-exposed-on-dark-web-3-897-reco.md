State Bank of India disclosed on 2026-10-21 that a preliminary analysis of roughly 375,000 records found 3,897 customer records containing personally identifiable information exposed on the dark web. The exposed fields include customer names, registered mobile numbers, dates of birth, gender, email addresses, residential addresses, branch and Customer Information File identifiers, Lead IDs, and metadata tied to loan and lead records. A threat actor using the name Siriusim claimed to have released 2,722 PDF documents including loan applications and account opening forms, though SBI has not confirmed that claim and says it is still investigating whether the data originated from its own systems or from a third party.

This is not a routine disclosure: SBI is India's largest bank by balance sheet, alerted by the Defence Cyber Agency and CERT-In, and the 3,897 confirmed PII records sit inside a 375,000-record dataset that includes loan and lead metadata, which makes targeted phishing and identity fraud the probable near-term harm rather than direct financial theft. The bank claims no material impact on core operations, but the unverified Siriusim claim of 2,722 PDFs and the open question of whether a third-party processor was the actual source mean the exposure footprint could expand; the reason this story stands out today is that a top-tier national bank with CERT-In-level oversight is still unable, days after disclosure, to confirm the data source or the full affected count.

Defenders at institutions with SBI-linked supply chain ties or Indian banking exposure should treat this as an active third-party risk event: review any shared loan-processing or lead-management integrations, hunt for references to the exposed data fields (names paired with mobile numbers and branch identifiers) in internal logs, and monitor for phishing using the specific PII categories mentioned. SBI states it has activated incident response protocols and engaged an independent cybersecurity agency empanelled by CERT-In; external observers should watch for the independent agency's determination of source (SBI systems versus third party) and any updated record count that supersedes the 3,897 figure, as either finding would change the risk calculus for partners and customers.

Watch for the independent CERT-In-empanelled agency's source determination and any regulator-mandated customer-notification count; a second-wave phishing campaign using the leaked mobile numbers, residential addresses, and lead metadata is the more likely immediate consequence, so customer-facing teams should reinforce authentication checks rather than relying solely on the bank's operational-reassurance messaging.
