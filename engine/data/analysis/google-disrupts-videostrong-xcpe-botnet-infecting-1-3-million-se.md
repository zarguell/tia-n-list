Google, working with Plume, TrendAI, Lumen, HUMAN Security, Synthient, and Japan's National Police Agency, dismantled the XCPE botnet embedded in the firmware of more than 1.3 million Android TV set-top boxes sold across over 200 countries. Google attributes the backdoor with high confidence to Videostrong Technology Co., a Chinese device manufacturer, which it says intentionally built the persistent backdoor into devices it manufactured and sold access to criminal affiliates. Those affiliates used the backdoored devices for ad fraud, residential proxying, and additional malware deployment. The takedown of XCPE command-and-control domains also severed access to at least 10 million additional compromised devices from various manufacturers, including retail set-top boxes, karaoke machines, and telehealth hardware, not all of them Android.

The compromise is a supply-chain failure rather than a post-sale infection. Videostrong slipped the malicious code past Google's pre- and post-launch security checks by loading it after the device's first boot, and the backdoor persisted in firmware before sale. Google terminated its relationship with Videostrong, revoked its license to manufacture certified Android TV devices, and banned the company and future affiliates from producing certified Android devices or supplying software to certified vendors. Plume's contribution tied the scheme to a device-management-to-monetization framework that rented victims' internet connections through residential proxy services.

Operators of retail and IoT fleets should treat firmware provenance as the control to fix. Devices from affected models should be checked or replaced, network egress should be monitored for XCPE modules and residential-proxy traffic, and procurement should require verifiable firmware integrity and post-boot attestation. The case also shows that takedowns at the manufacturer and C2 layer can cascade: cutting affiliate infrastructure here disrupted more than a dozen related malware families at once.
