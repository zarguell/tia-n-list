Google and partners dismantled the XCPE botnet, a firmware-embedded backdoor that Google attributes with high confidence to Chinese OEM Videostrong Technology Co. Unlike typical botnets that rely on exploits or user-installed malware, XCPE was baked into the firmware of more than 1.3 million Android TV set-top boxes and other Android IoT devices, running as x.cpe (also com.oemservice) at android.uid.system privilege. Since at least late 2024 Videostrong evolved it from simple droppers into a modular three-stage design: x.cpe handles C2, com.abc.nexus acts as the loader and execution engine, and monetization modules turn devices into residential proxy exit nodes (Linkfog, AppSalt/Honeygain, NetNut, GaGaNode and others), ad-fraud engines (CBOXTV, HiddenView), or additional backdoors (Moyu, Spirit, Bird). Google cut ties with Videostrong, revoked its Android TV certification, and banned the company and affiliates from certified Android manufacturing or supplying software to certified vendors.

The argument is exposure scale and trust failure, not just malware. TrendAI telemetry counted 1,472,554 infected devices across 206 models in more than 170 countries between June 3 and October 1, 2026, with roughly 1.33 million actively monetized and about 180,000 household IP addresses offered on a typical day; Google estimates the affiliate C2 takedowns sever access to at least 10 million additional compromised devices, including karaoke machines and telehealth hardware. Because the backdoor arrives preinstalled and survives factory reset via the /oem partition, owners cannot remediate it themselves, and the traffic exits from real consumer broadband and mobile addresses that defeat datacenter blocklists and IP reputation checks. That makes this story more than another TV-box botnet: it is a supply-chain failure where a certified Android partner subverted Google's compatibility and security tests using obfuscation and post-first-boot loading, and it shows how one OEM's firmware decision cascades into residential proxy capacity used for account takeover, ad fraud, and credential stuffing.

Defenders have no patch to apply; the realistic actions are removal and containment. Consumers and enterprises should replace or reflash affected low-cost or unbranded Android TV boxes, since the implant runs as a system app signed with the manufacturer's key and reinstalls from /oem after reboot. Network teams should block the bootstrap and C2 infrastructure named in the reporting: HTTP POST endpoints under anyevonline.com (including mcstb.anyevonline.com:8000 and the stb[AndroidVersion][Type].anyevonline.com:8000 pattern) and the MQTT broker mqtt.szbboys.com, plus the upload endpoint ott.szbboys.com:8000, and hunt for the packages and artifacts x.cpe, com.oemservice, com.abc.nexus, com.android.system.ntp, and com.android.sso.auth.handler. Additional hunt targets include content-provider queries to content://com.release.debug.provider/configs, the ro.mecool.device system property being true, libseccomp_guard.so, libexploit.so, /system/priv-app/ entries with matching privapp-permissions XML, and unexpected MQTT traffic from set-top boxes. Google has tightened firmware integrity review for certified Android TV devices, but that protects future devices, not ones already sold.

What changes the assessment now is whether the affiliates rebuild: Google itself notes point-in-time disruptions do not remove implants, and Videostrong-class operators have rebuilt before, so watch for new bootstrap domains, renamed dropper packages, and fresh MQTT brokers. Also watch whether other OEMs are banned under the same process change, and whether consumer protection or retailer firmware requirements follow, since the devices remain in homes even after the C2 is gone.
