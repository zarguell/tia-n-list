Silent Push's Danglegeddon research measured how quickly dangling DNS records can be found and taken over at scale. A dangling record still points to a deleted service, for example a forgotten CNAME to a decommissioned cloud resource, and whoever reclaims that service inherits the subdomain. The team scanned 12,500 apex domains across government, banking, pharmaceutical, and automotive organizations, surfaced roughly 85,000 dangling DNS records, and used AI assistance, including Claude Opus 5 for context-enriched script generation, to narrow the list to a review set on the order of 16,000 records. Every finding was disclosed to the affected organizations.

The study's warning is that what a human analyst does slowly, AI now does at scale: the same automation that finds dangles can claim and weaponize them faster than most organizations audit DNS. Exploitable dangles yield valid TLS certificates, session cookies, and inherited trust in third-party integrations, and the researchers warn the technique could become a nation-state capability against governments, banks, and supply chains. CNAME, MX, and NS records carry different risk levels, up to full zone takeover, and mergers, layoffs, and team churn keep producing new dangles long after services are shut down.

The practical response is in the research itself: audit DNS for records pointing at deprovisioned cloud resources such as S3 buckets, Azure blobs, GCP storage, and PaaS endpoints, automate dangle detection, and require ownership verification before recreating any previously deleted resource. Watch whether the disclosed findings produce actual takeovers, and whether cloud providers add reclamation guards against this class of hijack.
