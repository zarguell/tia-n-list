Cameron John Wagenius, the 22-year-old former Army communications specialist who operated as kiberphant0m, was sentenced in Seattle on Sept. 25, 2026 to 70 months in prison and $294,978 in restitution. Court documents cited by DOJ put the conduct from April 2023 through Dec. 18, 2024, all while he was on active duty: he and co-conspirators hit at least 10 victim organizations, harvested logins with a credential-attack tool he helped develop called SSH Brute, moved stolen credentials through Telegram chats, and extorted companies privately and on BreachForums and XSS.is, attempting to extort at least $1 million. KrebsOnSecurity ties the stolen data to call and text metadata for more than 100 million AT&T customers plus Verizon push-to-talk records used mainly by government agencies and first responders. He pleaded guilty on March 5, 2025 to two counts of unlawful transfer of confidential phone records and on July 15, 2025 to conspiracy to commit wire fraud, extortion in relation to computer fraud, and aggravated identity theft. The CVE-2023-45208 tag attached to this story is not the intrusion vector: it is a D-Link command injection bug Wagenius asked an AI tool to weaponize from prison, per the government's sentencing memo.

The extortion barely paid, and that is the point. Prosecutors say Wagenius netted roughly $1,500 from selling stolen data, and the only confirmed payment was the $370,000 bitcoin ransom AT&T handed over in 2024, yet the metadata itself, covering 2022 and 2023 traffic for about 110 million people with cell-tower identifiers that approximate location, never expires. DOJ states stolen data was reused for SIM-swapping, which is how old call records convert into account takeovers at banks, exchanges, and any organization still using SMS one-time codes, so the harm keeps landing years after the sentence is served. The case outranks the day's routine CVE churn because the exposure pattern is repeatable: sensitive customer records parked in a cloud data warehouse protected by a username and password alone, a failure that AT&T's July 2024 SEC disclosure showed applied across more than 160 Snowflake customers, and the person monetizing it was a serving soldier with a clearance. The 70-month term is a fraction of the statutory maximums he faced, 20 years for the wire fraud conspiracy plus a mandatory two-year consecutive term for aggravated identity theft, because he pleaded guilty early and cooperated extensively; the government traded prison time for a map of how the data moved, and the data itself is still in circulation.
