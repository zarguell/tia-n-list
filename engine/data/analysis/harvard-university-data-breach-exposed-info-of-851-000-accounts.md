Hackers breached Harvard University's Alumni Affairs and Development systems through a phone-based phishing (vishing) attack discovered on November 18, 2025, gaining access to donor and alumni records. The exposed data includes email addresses, telephone numbers, home and business addresses, event attendance records, donation details, and biographical information tied to fundraising and alumni engagement, affecting alumni, their spouses, partners, and widows or widowers, donors, parents of current and former students, and some current students, faculty, and staff. Harvard Vice President and CIO Klara Jelinkova stated the breached systems did not store Social Security numbers, passwords, payment card information, or financial account numbers. The ShinyHunters extortion group, operating as part of the Scattered LAPSUS$ Hunters collective, claimed responsibility and asserted it took more than one million records; after Harvard refused to pay a ransom the group leaked a 1.1GB archive of documents on its dark web leak site in early February 2026. Have I Been Pwned lists 851,000 unique email addresses from the breach, and no CVE or software vulnerability is involved because the entry point was social engineering rather than an unpatched product.

The harm here is not credential stuffing, since no passwords or payment data were exposed, but targeted follow-on fraud against a wealthy and identifiable donor base of more than 400,000 alumni plus current students, faculty, and staff. Donation amounts, event attendance, and biographical notes give an attacker the context to craft highly credible impersonation and spear-phishing messages that reference real gifts and real relationships, which is far more dangerous than a raw contact list. The 1.1GB archive and ShinyHunters' claim of over one million records also mean the data is now public and permanent, so exposure cannot be undone by resetting passwords. This story matters more than a routine breach notice because it is part of a documented ShinyHunters voice-phishing campaign against university advancement systems, with the University of Pennsylvania and Princeton reporting similar donor and alumni compromises in the same window, which points to a sector-wide targeting pattern rather than a one-off intrusion. InfoStealers' analysis of the leak estimated roughly 115,000 sensitive records among the haul, underscoring that the subset with the most sensitive stewardship detail is a small but high-value fraction of the total.

Harvard removed the attacker's access on November 18, 2025, engaged third-party cybersecurity experts and law enforcement, and sent breach notifications to affected individuals on November 22, 2025, with an FAQ published on the Harvard University Information Technology site. Affected alumni, donors, students, faculty, and staff should treat any unexpected request that references Harvard giving history, event attendance, or personal biography as suspicious and verify it through known university channels rather than links or phone numbers in the message. Peer institutions with advancement or alumni systems should harden the human layer: require phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication such as FIDO2 for staff with donor-data access, verify caller identity before any single sign-on or account change, restrict bulk export capability, and segment donor databases from general identity stores. For hunting, review identity-provider and single sign-on audit logs for unexpected MFA enrollment changes, new device registrations, impossible-travel sign-ins, and unusual data exports from advancement systems, consistent with the Okta and SSO targeting TechCrunch reported the attackers described. No patch or hotfix applies because this is not a software vulnerability.

Key developments that would change the assessment include whether Harvard's investigation finds a second access path or additional affected systems beyond Alumni Affairs and Development, whether the leaked archive contains records beyond the 851,000 unique email addresses Have I Been Pwned counted, and whether concrete follow-on fraud or impersonation attempts against donors materialize. Also watch for law-enforcement action against ShinyHunters and for additional university victims in the same voice-phishing campaign, which would confirm or weaken the sector-targeting reading.
