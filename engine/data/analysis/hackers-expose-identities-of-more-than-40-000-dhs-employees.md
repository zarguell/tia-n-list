A hacktivist actor named Department of Peace released a searchable archive called Icepick through DDoSecrets containing over 41,000 records of current and former US Department of Homeland Security employees and contractors, spanning ICE, CBP, HSI, and CISA, with roughly 1,000 CISA staff included. The group claims the data was extracted from an Okta single sign-on system used by DHS, including names, work email addresses, phone numbers, job titles, office locations, and system access records, with the records last updated August 19, 2026. DHS confirmed awareness and called it a potential breach, while Okta issued a flat denial that its own systems or data were compromised; no CVE has been assigned because the intrusion vector remains disputed.

The exposure is not a abstract roster leak. It covers operational staff rather than high-level officials, but the inclusion of system-access information and the searchable online tool transforms identity disclosure into a direct enabler for targeted follow-on attacks, phishing against agency infrastructure, and social engineering of undercover ICE agents whose roles are now mapped to contact details. With more than 41,000 individuals exposed across seven component agencies, any organization that partners with DHS should treat employee contact and role data as public and assume adversaries are cross-referencing it against internal directories.

Defenders should audit access logs for the DHS Okta SSO tenant, review authentication events for anomalous queries or data exfiltration patterns, and assume employee work emails and phone numbers are now in adversary hands. Organizations with DHS partnerships should tighten network restrictions on internal phone directories and enforce additional verification for any request referencing specific job titles, office locations, or system-access details drawn from the Icepick dataset. Hunt for references to the Department of Peace, DDoSecrets, or Icepick in internal telemetry, and watch for spear-phishing campaigns that use exact job titles from the leaked records.

The assessment changes if Okta publishes a confirmed compromise indicator, if DHS releases an official incident report identifying the intrusion path, or if exploitation telemetry shows the dataset being used in targeted campaigns rather than just published for transparency. Watch for whether the Department of Peace releases additional component-agency data beyond the August 19, 2026 update, or if CISA issues new defensive guidance specifically referencing the SSO exposure.
