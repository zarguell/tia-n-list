hackers from a collective called stegan0gram physically removed a Flock Safety license plate reader from above a roadway and extracted a near-complete copy of its software and stored data. The group shared the files with 404 Media and WIRED, who published a joint investigation on September 16. The camera runs Android with roughly 20 Flock-built apps handling motion detection, image capture, object classification, and cellular uploads. The device's processor is comparable to a midrange smartphone.

The attackers found unencrypted partitions on the camera, including one labeled "media" that contained an encryption key. That key unlocked video and still-image storage, yielding about 1.6 million images covering approximately 50,200 vehicles over 21 days of recoverable logs. On a typical day the camera logged around 3,300 vehicles, peaking at 4,454. Each passing vehicle generated roughly 28 images on average, though some produced more than 100. The camera used multiple exposures to capture both the plate and the wider scene, then selected and cropped frames before uploading them to Flock over cellular.

Critically, the camera's software explicitly detects people, not just vehicles and plates. WIRED extracted the computer-vision models and ran them against test footage, confirming person detection in 11 of 27,321 short video clips, all featuring motorcyclists. The plate detector also misidentified bumper stickers, dealership frames, and an American flag patch on a saddlebag as license plates. The camera logged more than 27,000 "no space left on device" errors while attempting to save full-resolution images.

Flock stated that unauthorized camera removal is illegal and encouraged the researchers to report through its vulnerability disclosure process. The company previously acknowledged similar findings by researcher Jon Gaines in 2025 but downplayed them, saying physical access was required and footage was not stored locally. The stegan0gram collective is publishing its methodology to encourage others to replicate the reverse engineering. Previous reporting by 404 Media and WIRED showed Flock's national camera network was accessible to over 2,000 agencies in Alpharetta, Georgia alone, and that local officers performed lookups on behalf of ICE and in connection with abortion-related investigations.
