Google is testing a hidden "Additional sandbox options" setting in the Gemini desktop app for macOS that would hand the agent full computer control: reading, creating, modifying, and deleting files anywhere on the machine, driving installed apps such as Mail, Safari, and Messages, and reaching the network, each without a permission prompt per action. Interface strings captured by TestingCatalog on October 2 state that Gemini "may be permitted to take actions without asking for your permission first," depending on which options the user enables. The feature is not live and Google has not confirmed it. Sensitive categories stay gated: purchases, money transfers, account creation, accepting legal terms, and changes to personal information would still require explicit confirmation. BleepingComputer covered the finding on October 3, and the plausible engine is Gemini 4 Argon, announced September 30 and evaluated for computer use on benchmarks including Agent's Last Exam and OSWorld 2.0, though Google has linked the model to nothing.

There is no vulnerability here and no CVE to chase, which is why most desks will skip it, and that is the mistake. The security boundary of an AI agent is set less by the model than by the permissions it holds by default, and this setting moves that boundary from folders the user explicitly connected to the entire user profile, the mailbox, browser sessions, and Messages. A prompt injection that today lands inside a connected-folder sandbox would, with expanded sandbox options enabled, reach everything needed for quiet data theft or internal phishing sent from a trusted mailbox, and auto-approval is precisely the property that lets such an incident proceed without a human pause. The lead time is the reason this story matters now: the disclosure arrived before release, while Apple is reportedly still weighing restrictions on AI agent access to personal files on macOS, so policy written this week shapes the exposure instead of retrofitting after the toggle ships.

Nothing is patchable yet, so the defender work is inventory, policy, and detection groundwork. Establish where the Gemini desktop app is installed and what folders and accounts it is connected to today, because that is the current blast radius and the baseline against which enablement of the new options will show up as a delta. Decide the standing policy now, including whether expanded sandbox modes are permitted at all and whether they require documented exception, so enablement becomes a recorded config change rather than a silent user click. When the setting appears in released builds, monitor it directly: alert on new macOS privacy (TCC) grants to the Gemini app for files, accessibility, or automation of Mail and Safari, and baseline the Gemini process's outbound network behavior so an auto-approval mode surfaces as a change rather than drift. If Google documents a management key for the setting, hold it disabled via MDM until reviewed.

Two developments would change this assessment. First, Google's rollout announcement: which model powers Full Access, whether it reaches Gemini for Workspace with admin controls, and whether expanded sandbox is default-on or default-off, since a Workspace release with a central kill switch softens the exposure while a consumer default-on hardens it. Second, Apple's decision on restricting AI agent access to personal files at the OS layer, which would cap the feature's practical reach regardless of what Google ships and turn this from an endpoint exposure into a platform-policy fight.
