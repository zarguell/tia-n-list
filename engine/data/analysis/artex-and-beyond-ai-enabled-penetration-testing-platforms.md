Cisco Talos is tracking the in-the-wild use of AI-enabled penetration testing and red team frameworks that automate and chain reconnaissance, vulnerability testing, and follow-on actions, lowering the skill threshold for attackers and accelerating intrusions when misused. The frameworks combine free and open-source tools such as nmap and nuclei with internet mapping services (Censys, Shodan), CAPTCHA bypass, credential-leak management, residential proxies, and hash cracking. Hephaestus can be deployed with a single script on a VPS and controlled via Telegram; PentAGI exposes a web interface with a full attack-chain graph and over 700 currently reachable instances were found on the internet; HexStrike AI integrates with MCP-capable clients such as Claude Code and VS Code. ARTEX in particular has enabled threat actors to scale offensive operations, compressing work that previously took skilled teams hours or days into activity that can complete in under three hours, per the FBI.

The central concern is not the tools' stated purpose but their misuse: automation that chains the full attack lifecycle, exposes misconfigured instances to the internet, and compresses intrusion timelines. The 700-plus reachable PentAGI instances are a concrete indicator of how widely these platforms are being left exposed.

Defenders should treat AI-enabled attack automation as a speed and scale problem: harden exposed management interfaces, monitor for rapid reconnaissance-to-privilege-escalation chains, and assume that misconfigured red-team tooling is itself an attack surface. Detection timelines need to shrink to match the compressed intrusion windows.
