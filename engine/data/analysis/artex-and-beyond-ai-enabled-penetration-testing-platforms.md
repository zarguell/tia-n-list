Cisco Talos is tracking a wave of AI-enabled penetration-testing frameworks that automate and chain reconnaissance, exploitation and follow-on action, and that are being used in real intrusions rather than only in red-team engagements. These frameworks wrap free and open-source tools such as nmap and nuclei and bolt on internet mapping services including Censys and Shodan, CAPTCHA bypass services, credential-leak management, residential proxies and hash cracking. They differ mainly in packaging: Hephaestus deploys with a single script on a VPS and is driven entirely through Telegram, PentAGI exposes a web interface with a full attack-chain graph, and Talos found more than 700 reachable PentAGI instances on the internet. HexStrike AI plugs into MCP-capable clients such as Claude Code and VS Code. ARTEX is the one with confirmed criminal and espionage use: Talos attributes to it the compression of work that took skilled teams hours or days into intrusions that reach privileged access in under three hours, a figure the FBI echoed in a flash alert describing attacks at machine speed.

The ARTEX reporting accumulated across vendors points the same direction. The Hacker News tied ARTEX to data theft at South Korean financial firms, Wiz tracked Storm-0940 using it against IT and software vendors including a ConnectWise supply-chain compromise, GitGuardian catalogued 45 comparable tools, Unit 42 wrote on what defenders should do about them, and TechCrunch reported hundreds of ARTEX instances left publicly exposed. SentinelOne Labs added its own analysis on November 14, and Obsidian Security framed the shift as agentic exploitation measured in hours. The common thread is that skill, not tooling availability, was the limiting factor in intrusion speed, and that constraint is now largely gone.

Defenders should treat exposed framework consoles as attack surface in their own right: remove PentAGI, HexStrike and similar management interfaces from the public internet, require authentication and network allowlisting, and ingest the published ARTEX-associated command-and-control address lists as network indicators. Because the intrusion timeline from first probe to privileged access can close inside three hours, detection engineering matters more than signature coverage, and alert triage should prioritize chained reconnaissance, credential access and lateral movement completing in a single session. Two things would change the picture: a vulnerability or default credential in a framework console turning the 700-odd exposed PentAGI instances into a mass-compromise vector, and adoption by additional state-aligned actors beyond Storm-0940.
