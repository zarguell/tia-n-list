Dragos chief research officer HD Moore ran a five-month test from July through November 2026, sending roughly two million prompts with unique URLs, DNS names and canary AWS credentials through AI routers and inference marketplaces. Qualys' account of the study says the team traced about 2,000 request routes. Each prompt carried a tracked URL or decoy key, so any fetch or key use showed that a provider or an outsider had read the prompt. Dragos found evidence of exposed data at at least three AI hosting providers, none of which knew until researchers notified them.

Some prompts marked Zero Data Retention were still fetched or forwarded, including secret URLs accessed from the system of a Chinese AI lab. Resellers could read customer prompts through ordinary request logging. Seeded keys were used for tens of thousands of AWS API calls, including S3 listing, Secrets Manager and Parameter Store lookups, Bedrock model enablement and IAM user creation. In the largest case, about 200 AWS test keys sent to one provider were stolen in late October. Some came from requests the provider rejected before a model ran, which Moore read as copying at the provider front door rather than deeper in the inference stack.

OpenRouter, which routes customer requests to multiple providers, removed Together AI, Phala and Novita after Dragos notified it. It pulled Zero Data Retention eligibility from 125 endpoints and told affected customers, while saying its own systems were not breached. Atlas Cloud said it would withdraw its models from OpenRouter pending review. Moore said real customer requests processed alongside the bait were almost certainly copied too. What to watch is whether more providers show confirmed prompt access and whether affected customers get request-level detail on what was exposed.

A companion honeypot in the same research ran from July 7 to November 7 with more than 500 canary AWS keys. Attackers used 155 of them from 12,353 distinct IPs; 72 percent of used keys went to SES or SNS send-quota checks and 36 percent touched Bedrock. Median time from theft to first use was 37 minutes, and one checker graded stolen keys across five providers from 20 residential connections in 14 countries.
