Dragos led by chief research officer HD Moore sent roughly two million prompts carrying unique URLs, DNS names and canary AWS credentials through AI routers and inference marketplaces between July and November 2026, then watched for those seeds to surface. The study reports three compromised hosting providers, resellers reading customer prompts through ordinary request logging, and requests marked Zero Data Retention still being fetched or forwarded to Chinese laboratories. One provider lost about 200 test AWS keys in late October. Some keys came from requests the provider rejected before a model ran, which Moore read as evidence that requests were copied at the provider's front door rather than deeper in the inference stack. Seeded keys were used for tens of thousands of AWS API calls, including S3 listing, Secrets Manager and Parameter Store lookups, Bedrock model enablement and IAM user creation. The three providers did not detect the theft until researchers told them.

OpenRouter removed Together AI, Phala and Novita from its platform after the findings, revoked Zero Data Retention eligibility for 125 endpoints, and notified potentially affected customers. The company says its own systems were not breached, placing the leak at the inference provider level. Atlas Cloud said it would pull its models from OpenRouter voluntarily. Securityweek reported that real customer requests travelled alongside the bait prompts and were likely copied too, so the exposed material is not limited to test credentials.

A related honeypot run by Lauren Proehl from July 7 to November 7 used more than 500 canary AWS keys in a walkable git repository, fake mail server and fake AI inference endpoints. Attackers used 155 of those keys from 12,353 distinct IPs. The median time from theft to first use was 37 minutes. Seventy-two percent of used keys went to SES or SNS send-quota checks and 36 percent touched Bedrock. One checker graded stolen AI keys across five providers from 20 residential connections in 14 countries, showing organised validation of harvested credentials rather than opportunistic scraping.

For defenders, a retention flag is not a guarantee that prompt content stays private once it reaches a marketplace provider or reseller. Tokens, private URLs and customer data sent through these routes should be treated as potentially exposed. Watch for further provider removals, revisions to Zero Data Retention claims, and any evidence tying the harvested AWS keys to broader cloud intrusions.
