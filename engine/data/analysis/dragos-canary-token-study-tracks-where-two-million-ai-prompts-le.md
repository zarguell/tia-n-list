Dragos, led by chief research officer HD Moore, sent nearly two million prompts through AI routers and inference services between July and November 2026. Each prompt carried a unique tracker: URLs, DNS names or decoy AWS credentials. The study found evidence that data from at least three hosting providers was exposed, that resellers could read customer prompts through ordinary request logging, and that some requests marked Zero Data Retention were still fetched or forwarded to Chinese labs. In the largest test, roughly 200 AWS keys sent to one provider were stolen in late October, including keys from requests the provider rejected before a model ran, which Moore reads as copying at the provider's front door. Seeded keys were then checked and used for S3 listing, Secrets Manager and Parameter Store lookups, Bedrock model enablement and IAM user creation.

OpenRouter said its own systems were not breached. After Dragos alerted the company, it removed three inference providers, Together AI, Phala and Novita, revoked Zero Data Retention eligibility for 125 endpoints and notified potentially affected customers. Atlas Cloud said it would withdraw its models from the platform pending review. Dragos stressed that its bait prompts were processed alongside real customer requests, so real prompts were likely copied as well. The finding is a supply-chain problem rather than a flaw in one model: routing decisions determine which provider sees a secret.

A separate honeypot described in the same reporting, run by Lauren Proehl from July 7 to November 7 with more than 500 canary AWS keys, measured 155 keys used from 12,353 distinct IPs. Seventy-two percent of used keys went to SES or SNS send-quota checks, 36 percent touched Bedrock, and the median time from theft to first use was 37 minutes. Watch for further provider removals, confirmation of real customer data copied, and whether ZDR claims survive contract and logging audits.
