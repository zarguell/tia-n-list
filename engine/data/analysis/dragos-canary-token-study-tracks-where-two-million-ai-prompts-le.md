Dragos published a five-month study in which HD Moore sent nearly two million prompts carrying unique URLs, DNS names, and canary AWS credentials through AI routers and inference marketplaces between July and November 2026. Dragos reports that three hosting providers were compromised and did not know until researchers told them, that resellers could read customer prompts through ordinary request logging, and that some requests marked Zero Data Retention were still fetched or forwarded to Chinese labs.

The canaries showed real use of the seeded credentials. Dragos recorded tens of thousands of AWS API calls, including S3 listing, Secrets Manager and Parameter Store lookups, Bedrock model enablement, and IAM user creation. In a related honeypot run from July 7 to November 7, Lauren Proehl planted more than 500 canary AWS keys alongside a walkable git repository, fake mail server, and fake inference endpoints. Attackers used 155 of those keys from 12,353 distinct IPs; 72 percent of used keys went to SES or SNS send-quota checks and 36 percent touched Bedrock. The median time from theft to first use was 37 minutes, and one checker graded stolen AI keys across five providers from 20 residential connections in 14 countries.

OpenRouter responded by removing three inference providers, dropping 125 endpoints from Zero Data Retention eligibility, and notifying affected customers. The finding matters because prompts and credentials moved through resellers and gateways that customers may treat as private despite retention flags. Watch for further provider removals, changes to logging defaults, and whether the seeded-key activity maps to identifiable abuse of customer secrets.
