Dragos ran a five-month experiment in which researchers sent roughly two million prompts containing canary links and credentials to AI services, then tracked where those markers surfaced. The reported result is that canaries were taken by users at three inference providers, and that none of the three knew until the researchers told them. The Hacker News reports that some of the stolen requests were marked Zero Data Retention, the setting meant to prevent prompt storage, and that OpenRouter dropped the three providers after Dragos disclosed the findings. The underlying Dragos post frames the work as an AI supply chain and secret leakage test rather than a single vendor vulnerability.

The method is the substantive finding: planted credentials and links act as traceable markers inside ordinary prompts, so later reuse or retrieval can be tied back to the original submission. Corroborating coverage from the Metasploit creator describes the same two-million-prompt setup and reported theft at three providers. That gives the study more than one public source, but the provider-level claims still rest on canary telemetry, not on separate breach disclosures from each service. The Zero Data Retention detail is the sharpest operational point because customers may treat that setting as a guarantee that prompts are never stored.

Watch for the named providers confirming the exposure path and for evidence that the canary credentials were used outside the test. Until then, the defensible conclusion is that prompts can carry tracked secrets into third-party systems and that copies moved further than the submitter expected; it is not evidence of a specific CVE or of a confirmed compromise of every provider named in secondary coverage. Review which inference routes and retention settings are actually in use, and treat credentials that appear in prompts as exposed once a canary path like this shows they can be harvested from provider-side systems.
