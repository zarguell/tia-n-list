Hacking attempts against the US Federal Election Commission's electronic filing system forced the agency to take the system offline, with the outage beginning around 10 p.m. ET on Monday, October 26, 2026, and the FEC confirming on Tuesday that its electronic filing system was offline while IT staff worked to restore service. The disruption hit roughly one week before the November 3, 2026 elections and blocked campaigns, parties, and PACs from submitting the FEC's 24-hour and 48-hour spending reports, although past reports remained searchable. Reuters, which saw an email describing the activity, reported that the FEC declined further comment, and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency and the FBI did not immediately respond to requests for comment. No actor, CVE, or intrusion vector has been identified in available reporting, and the FEC has not stated whether any filer or donor data was accessed.

The practical damage is a transparency gap rather than a ballot problem: the FEC does not administer elections, so voters can still cast ballots, but the 24- and 48-hour reports are the only near-real-time view of late independent spending, and a multi-day outage in the final week means last-minute money moves without public scrutiny until after Election Day. That makes the incident more consequential than a routine outage of the same length in an off-cycle month, because the value of the data decays to near zero once results are known. The blast radius is also wider than one agency, since every committee on a filing deadline is blocked at once, and nothing in the public reporting so far assures filers that queued submissions were preserved.

Defenders cannot patch a specific flaw because no vulnerability or affected product version has been named, so the immediate actions are operational. Campaigns and PACs should retain local copies of any reports prepared for submission, monitor the FEC status page and official statements for restoration and any reopened filing windows, and treat the portal as untrusted until the agency publishes scope and indicators. Security teams should preserve access logs for the FEC-facing systems they control and watch for follow-on phishing that impersonates the FEC or filing vendors, since no indicators of compromise or log paths have been published by the agency. Filers should also confirm with the FEC whether deadlines will be extended for reports missed during the outage.

The assessment changes on a small set of facts: an FEC disclosure that filer or donor data was accessed or altered; attribution to a named actor or to the PHP flaws circulating that same week; an outage that persists through the November 3 filing deadlines; or, conversely, a clean restoration with a defined submission grace period. Any of the first three would move this from an attempted intrusion that caused an outage to an election-infrastructure incident.
