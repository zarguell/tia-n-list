The city of Boston terminated its contract with Flock Safety after discovering that the company's automated license plate reader (ALPR) system enabled "nationwide lookup" capabilities, violating the contract's requirement to limit data access to Massachusetts only. The city found that Flock's system allowed law enforcement to query plate data from across the country, not just local scans.

Flock Safety's ALPR cameras are deployed by hundreds of police departments across the US. The company markets its system as a tool for solving crimes, but privacy advocates have raised concerns about the scale of vehicle tracking data being collected and who can access it. Boston's discovery that the system exceeded its contractual scope highlights the gap between what cities think they are buying and what the technology actually enables.

This is part of a broader pattern of municipalities reconsidering surveillance technology contracts. Chesterton, Indiana recently took similar action after an officer abused the system. The Boston decision carries more weight given the city's size and political influence. Other cities running Flock systems should audit their contract terms against actual system capabilities, particularly around geographic data access restrictions.
