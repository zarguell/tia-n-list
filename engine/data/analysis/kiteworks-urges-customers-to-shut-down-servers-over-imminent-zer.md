Kiteworks urged customers worldwide to temporarily shut down their secure file-transfer and communications servers after receiving what it called credible threat intelligence from law enforcement that an attack on Kiteworks systems might be imminent over the weekend of September 26. The customer notification came from CISO Frank Balonis, who specified a coordinated six-hour window on Saturday: 4:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m. in Central Europe, and 10:00 p.m. Friday to 4:00 a.m. Saturday on the US East Coast. Administrators were told to power systems down before the window and to take servers offline even when not directly internet-accessible. Kiteworks says it has no evidence of a confirmed compromise and framed the move as preventive.

The company's support team described the action as protection against potential zero-day attacks, and BleepingComputer's September 26 report carried the same framing, but Kiteworks has not confirmed that a zero-day exists, that it was exploited, or that a CVE has been assigned. The shutdown window has now passed without any public confirmation of attack activity, and follow-on write-ups (GBHackers, cyberpress, dev.to) add no technical detail beyond the original heise reporting. That gap is the story: a vendor-directed global blackout of widely deployed MFT infrastructure serving enterprises and public-sector bodies, taken on law-enforcement advice with the technical rationale undisclosed. Germany's BKA declined to comment citing investigative reasons and BSI had not responded by late Friday, so no government side of the intelligence has been confirmed either. The heise original ran September 25 at 13:41 CEST, and coverage has since spread across BleepingComputer, GBHackers, and cyberworldops without adding technical detail.

Organizations running Kiteworks should follow the shutdown window, capture full logs and configuration backups beforehand, and treat the post-restart period as an incident-hunt window: review authentication logs, transfer records, and admin account activity for the weeks preceding the outage. The precedent echoes the MOVEit and GoAnywhere playbook, where MFT platforms became mass-exploitation targets, so verify the installed Kiteworks version against vendor advisories the moment anything is published and patch immediately if a flaw is disclosed. Watch for a CVE assignment, any Kiteworks advisory, or law-enforcement confirmation, since any of the three would convert a precautionary blackout into a specific threat requirement.
