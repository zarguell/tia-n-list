Kiteworks urged customers worldwide to temporarily shut down their secure file-transfer and communications servers after receiving what it called credible threat intelligence from law enforcement that an attack on Kiteworks systems might be imminent over the weekend of September 26. The customer notification came from CISO Frank Balonis, who specified a coordinated shutdown window on Saturday, September 26: 4:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m. in Central Europe, and 10:00 p.m. Friday to 4:00 a.m. on the US East Coast, per the heise and BleepingComputer reporting. Administrators were told to power systems down before the window and to take servers offline even when not directly internet-accessible. Kiteworks says it has no evidence of a confirmed compromise and framed the move as preventive.

Coverage of the window's length now conflicts: the initial heise and BleepingComputer reports described six hours, while later write-ups from The Hacker News and dev.to describe customers being told to go dark for nine hours, and neither the company nor law enforcement has published a technical rationale, a CVE, or confirmation that a zero-day exists or was exploited. The shutdown window has now passed without any public confirmation of attack activity, and follow-on write-ups (GBHackers, cyberpress, dev.to) add no technical detail beyond the original heise reporting. That gap is the story: a vendor-directed global blackout of widely deployed MFT infrastructure serving enterprises and public-sector bodies, taken on law-enforcement advice with the technical rationale undisclosed. Germany's BKA declined to comment citing investigative reasons and BSI had not responded by late Friday, so no government side of the intelligence has been confirmed either.

Organizations running Kiteworks should treat the post-restart period as an incident-hunt window: capture logs and configuration backups if not already done, then review authentication logs, transfer records, and admin account activity for the weeks preceding the outage. The precedent echoes the MOVEit and GoAnywhere playbook, where MFT platforms became mass-exploitation targets, so verify the installed Kiteworks version against vendor advisories the moment anything is published and patch immediately if a flaw is disclosed. Watch for a CVE assignment, any Kiteworks advisory, an explanation of the six-versus-nine-hour discrepancy, or law-enforcement confirmation, since any of the four would convert a precautionary blackout into a specific threat requirement.
