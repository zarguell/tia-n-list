Coinbase disclosed on November 17 that scammers ran a fake YouTube livestream of Tesla's flying car demonstration with an AI-generated Elon Musk deepfake promising to double any crypto sent to a displayed wallet address or QR code. More than 500,000 viewers were in the audience, and hundreds of Coinbase customers attempted transfers over about two hours; Coinbase says its AI fraud system blocked about 91 percent of those attempts, or $4.7 million. There is no CVE, version, or stolen credential here: the transfers were created and approved by logged-in customers.

The risk is the collapse of the authorization heuristic. An approval signal that normally proves customer intent here proves only that the customer was persuaded, so defenses keyed to unauthorized activity are structurally blind to this loss path, and the roughly 9 percent of attempts outside the reported 91 percent block rate is not accounted for as recovered or lost. This outranks most of today's queue because one 500,000-viewer event supplies a repeatable template: borrow a real product moment, insert a credible synthetic executive, and compress the decision into a live QR-code payment.

There is no patch version or hotfix; the control point is the withdrawal approval path. Apply a delay or step-up challenge to first-time external wallet destinations and to transfers whose destination was entered from a QR code or a live-video link, and block QR-derived wallet entry on managed browsers. Hunt for transfer approvals to newly entered destinations within the roughly two-hour window around the fake Tesla flying car stream and tie them to the giveaway context rather than to login anomalies. The events and coverage publish no wallet addresses, transaction hashes, or log paths, so do not seed detections from invented indicators.

The assessment changes if Coinbase discloses confirmed losses for the unblocked share or publishes destination addresses and transaction hashes, or if a second exchange reports the same hijacked-livestream pattern. Copycats using a different executive or platform would make a Musk-specific blocklist less useful and behavioral controls more important.
