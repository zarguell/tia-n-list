Check Point Research's year-long investigation ties three espionage clusters, SilentDelivery, OceanGate, and DarkWire, to a single supplier of China-nexus tooling tracked as CopperDragon, a digital quartermaster behind the cross-platform Sparrow framework for Windows and Linux. SilentDelivery executed highly selective watering-hole attacks across Europe and Asia targeting government, shipping, and IT and research sites, replacing legitimate pages with a fake Chrome certificate error and delivering SquadC or a SparrowDoor variant via trojanized MSI installers named CertFixer.msi. OceanGate compromised internet-facing Linux servers in Asia and pivoted to Windows, deploying AnnobinDoor, SparrowShell, trojanized SSH components, and SparrowDoor alongside custom implants including CopperAdu, CopperList, CopperLogger, and CopperLsa. DarkWire targeted telecommunications and government organizations across Latin America using CopperDoor (also documented by ESET as SparroWocky) and CopperDNS, loaded via CopperLoader through DLL sideloading.

The exposure spans several dozens of high-profile organizations across Latin America, Asia, and Europe, including telecommunications carriers, government and military bodies, research institutions, and critical infrastructure operators. Treating these intrusions as a single supply-side problem rather than three unrelated campaigns changes how defenders scope historical breaches: an entity that remediated a FamousSparrow intrusion from July 2024 or a UAT-9244 breach of South American telecoms in March 2026 remains exposed to SilentDelivery watering holes or DarkWire telecom intrusions because the loaders, backdoors, and certificates are shared across operators. The link to the Linux kernel rootkit Melofee, built on modified Reptile rootkit code and reused self-signed certificates, confirms that Linux server estates are in scope alongside Windows endpoints, and SilentDelivery's check for an active browser process before running SquadC allows samples to evade isolated automated sandboxes while succeeding on user workstations.

Defenders should hunt for shared framework artifacts across Windows and Linux hosts rather than isolated malware families. On Windows, audit registry Run keys under WindowsUpdateAssist, unexpected local administrator accounts such as admin$ created by CopperAdu, inbound firewall rules named RasiUwpApp on TCP 445, LSASS memory dump files renamed to 1.png, and staged binaries under C:\Program Files\7-Zip\Lang, C:\Windows\debug, C:\ProgramData\Oracle\Java, and C:\ProgramData\Waves. On Linux, inspect modified SSH and SSHD binaries that log credentials, AnnobinDoor execution (internally named annobinagent) and SparrowShell loading plugins from /tmp, and unauthorized Reptile rootkit artifacts. Network monitoring should alert on SilentDelivery watering-hole endpoints /image-check, /report-url, and /register-ip, CopperDNS MX record tunneling over high-numbered ports such as 6532 using TLS certificate SHA-256 ee4a8b81fe3864582efc27a77f806990e2602dcc36447ddda4b3a230a473d575, and CopperDoor command-and-control on TCP 443 wrapping raw TCP inside TLS with certificates whose common names mimic victim domains. Because CopperLsa writes harvested credentials to an operator-specified log file and CopperLogger captures keystrokes and clipboard data, domain and local credential rotation is mandatory after any suspected compromise.

Two developments would alter this assessment: the appearance of CopperDoor or SquadC outside the three tracked clusters and regions, which would signal a wider CopperDragon client base, and further independent corroboration or refutation of the Melofee and Salt Typhoon overlaps. Expansion of SilentDelivery watering-hole infrastructure beyond government, shipping, and IT and research websites would also broaden the targeted population beyond the current victimology.
