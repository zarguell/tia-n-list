Attackers compromised registry operators for .gh (Ghana), .sl (Sierra Leone), and .as (American Samoa) and changed authoritative DNS records to pass automated domain control validation. The result was valid TLS certificates issued for Google domains and other major brands -- not through a breach of the brands themselves, but through supply-chain control of the ccTLD layer. Google explicitly stated its own systems were not compromised; the vulnerability is the dependency of certificate authorities on registry-level DNS for ownership proof.

This is a trust-infrastructure breach with broad interception potential. Any domain registered under those three ccTLDs is exposed to the same registry-level redirection and certificate minting, and the attackers demonstrated it against high-value targets including Google properties. The ThreatAft report notes Chrome blocked identified certificates via CRLSets, but browser-side blocking is incomplete; domain owners under .gh, .sl, or .as must treat registry control as a direct attack surface for traffic interception and phishing at scale. The event list carries no count of affected certificates beyond the mention of "several Google domains," but the mechanism implies systemic exposure rather than a targeted single-domain incident.

Defenders should audit all domains under the three affected ccTLDs for unauthorized DNS changes and review Certificate Transparency logs for unexpected certificates. Restrictive CAA records limit which CAs can issue, and registry-level monitoring (not just registrar-level) is required because the compromise was at the ccTLD operator layer. For network hunting, look for TLS handshakes presenting certificates with issuance timestamps after 2026-10-08 and CA metadata matching the issuing authorities mentioned in the ThreatAft disclosure. There is no specific IoC list in the events beyond the registry domains themselves (.gh, .sl, .as) and the altered DNS records; defenders should treat any unexpected A or NS change in those zones as suspicious.

Watch for confirmation from additional CAs on revocation timelines and for Google or registry operators publishing a post-incident audit of the registry compromise scope. A second critical signal would be evidence of active traffic interception using these certificates -- if telemetry shows redirects through attacker-controlled infrastructure for domains under these TLDs, the assessment shifts from potential supply-chain exposure to confirmed in-the-wild abuse.
