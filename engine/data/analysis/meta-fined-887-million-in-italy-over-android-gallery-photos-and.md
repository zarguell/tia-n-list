Italy's Garante has fined Meta Platforms Ireland €805 million, about $887 million, over the Facebook for Android feature "Get suggestions from gallery when browsing Facebook", which read photos, videos and associated metadata from users' galleries without specific, informed consent. The decision was announced on 14 November 2026 and covers roughly 20 million Android users in Italy. The authority found the feature enabled by default and that gallery access rode a single permission covering several app functions with different purposes. The penalty is about 0.47 percent of the group's global turnover, and the Garante said Meta's lack of cooperation during the investigation factored into the amount. Meta has 60 days to disable the feature and request separate consent, and it has challenged Italian jurisdiction.

This is not a breach: no CVE, no intruder, no exfiltration. The consequence is jurisdictional. The Garante rejected the argument that the Irish authority should own the case, holding that the ePrivacy regime transposed into Italian law has no one-stop-shop, which means Meta Ireland cannot funnel every EU privacy matter through Dublin and is open to parallel action from each national authority. The authority also noted that a permission granted in full can give an app lasting read access to an entire photo library with no record of what was actually used, which is the design failure behind the fine rather than any single access.

There is no patch and no network telemetry to hunt, so the audit is a configuration one. On managed Android fleets that allow Facebook, inventory which devices hold a full Photos and videos permission, confirm the gallery suggestion setting is off because it shipped on by default, and review any other Facebook permission bundle that mixes unrelated functions behind one prompt. Treat the bundled gallery authorization as the finding to report upward, since the exposure is a consent flow and not a vulnerability, so log paths and indicators of compromise do not apply.

Watch the 60-day compliance deadline for the disable-and-consent change, Meta's appeal on jurisdiction, and whether other data protection authorities open their own cases on the same consent-by-default reasoning. A second proceeding elsewhere in the EU would show the fine is a template rather than a one-off, and it would confirm that consent bundled into a broad permission is now a finance-level risk rather than a product decision.
