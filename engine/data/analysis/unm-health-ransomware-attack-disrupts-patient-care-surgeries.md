The University of New Mexico Health Sciences Center disclosed a cybersecurity event on Monday, October 26, 2026 that took systems offline and disrupted patient care, academic activities, and appointments. UNM Health temporarily limited new patient admissions from 12:30 to 2:30 p.m., diverted ambulances to nearby hospitals, and suspended outpatient surgeries and appointments for the rest of the day, while more than 30 clinics across New Mexico canceled appointments. UNM stated that UNM Hospital and Sandoval Regional Medical Center remained open and that clinics would resume scheduled appointments and procedures on Tuesday, October 27, while classes at its four health sciences schools were canceled and nonessential staff were sent home. UNM did not disclose the attack type, but BleepingComputer reported that a new ransomware group calling itself Byod claimed responsibility on its data leak site, asserting compromise of 36,000 systems across UNM's main campus and hospital environments, including Active Directory forests, Group Policy infrastructure, the entire Microsoft Entra tenant, and two Oracle databases.

The serious exposure is identity, not just endpoints: if the Byod claims hold, the entire Entra tenant, Active Directory forests, and Group Policy infrastructure are in scope, which means recovery is an identity rebuild rather than a malware cleanup, and every credential, service principal, and federated trust issued in that tenant must be treated as compromised until proven otherwise. The patient-safety stakes are outsized because UNM Hospital in Albuquerque houses New Mexico's only Level I trauma center and the system records more than one million patient visits annually, so the Monday ambulance diversion window pushed the most seriously injured patients onto other facilities that lack that capability. The scope of the claimed 36,000 systems spanning both campus and hospital environments also suggests the academic and clinical estates were not segmented well enough to contain the blast radius. That combination of immediate care disruption at a sole trauma center and a claimed full-tenant identity compromise is why this matters more than a routine ransomware listing.

Defenders should treat identity-plane telemetry as the primary hunt surface: review Microsoft Entra audit logs for new global administrators, consent grants, and federated identity provider changes, and Active Directory security logs for Group Policy modifications, new domain controllers, and mass account changes, alongside endpoint telemetry for mass file renames and encryption. The KOB 4 recording of the attacker's broadcast message, which told staff to contact them on Session and claimed theft of student, employee, and patient PII and SSNs, gives a behavioral indicator but no published indicators of compromise, so hunts should key on the identity and backup-resilience controls rather than on file hashes. Other healthcare and university defenders should verify offline, immutable backups and tested restore procedures, restrict lateral movement protocols between academic and clinical networks, and confirm domain controller and identity provider coverage in EDR. UNM's own recovery steps, per its statements, include containment, engagement of state and federal law enforcement, and phased restoration.

Watch whether UNM confirms ransomware rather than a generic cybersecurity event, whether a breach notification or forensic report validates the Byod claims of 36,000 systems and full Entra tenant compromise, and whether patient data exfiltration converts the outage into a reportable HIPAA breach. Also watch for a second outage wave or a public data leak from Byod, which would confirm the exfiltration claim and reset the recovery timeline from days to weeks.
