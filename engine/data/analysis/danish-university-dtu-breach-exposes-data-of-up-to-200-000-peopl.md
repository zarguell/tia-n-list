Attackers used compromised credentials to access DTUBasen, DTU's identity and access management system, and downloaded a large data set covering nearly 40,000 active users and roughly 160,000 former users, for a total exposure ceiling of 200,000 people. The breach, confirmed by DTU on Friday (first_seen 2026-10-02), exposed Danish civil registration numbers (CPR), full names, home addresses, profile pictures, work emails, job titles, office locations, and next-of-kin relationships for active users; former users' address, profile, and next-of-kin data are automatically deleted after six months, which limits long-tail exposure for that cohort. No CVE is assigned and no specific attacker group is named, but the mechanism is credential-based IAM compromise rather than a product vulnerability, which changes the remediation profile from patching to identity hygiene and access review.

This is a high-value target story because universities hold dense identity and employment datasets that are hard to rotate; the inclusion of CPR numbers and next-of-kin telephone numbers raises identity-fraud and social-engineering risk well beyond a standard corporate email breach. The 200,000-person scale matters not because every record was exfiltrated (DTU explicitly says it cannot determine precise download scope) but because the attacker had sufficient IAM access to reach more than two decades of user data, making full impact unknown. With no Reddit signal and only two source domains (bleepingcomputer.com and dtu.dk), the story has not yet attracted broad defender discussion, which makes this desk post timely rather than redundant.

Immediate actions: audit all DTUBasen administrator and service-account credentials for rotation, enforce multi-factor authentication on the IAM layer, and review access logs for the compromised account to determine which user records were queried or downloaded. Because DTU states it cannot confirm the exact information downloaded, defenders should assume worst-case exposure for the 40,000 active user cohort including CPR and next-of-kin fields, and notify affected parties accordingly. Network-level restrictions should limit DTUBasen access to campus-managed endpoints, and hunt telemetry should focus on unusual authentication events to DTUBasen followed by large data-export patterns rather than malware indicators.

Watch for DTU's follow-up disclosure on the exact scope of downloaded records, any law-enforcement attribution naming an actor, and whether the six-month deletion policy for former users' sensitive fields actually reduced the exfiltrated dataset size. A second development to monitor is whether the compromised credentials came from a prior supply-chain breach or phishing campaign, which would shift the defense focus from DTU-specific identity controls to upstream vendor and email-security posture.
