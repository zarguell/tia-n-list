A hacker collective called stegan0gram physically removed a Flock Safety license-plate reader camera from a roadside pole and cloned the data on its internal hard drive. The group shared copies with 404 Media, WIRED, and Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets), which published a joint investigation. The camera ran a stripped-down Android system with roughly 20 Flock-built applications handling motion detection, image capture, object classification, and remote updates.

The critical finding was in storage: most of the drive was encrypted, but one partition labeled "media" was left unencrypted. Sitting inside that partition was the encryption key needed to unlock the camera's video and image archive. Stealing the physical device was therefore sufficient to access everything it had recorded. The data on the device included 1.6 million captured images, 50,200 logged vehicles, and 21 days of footage. The software also logged where people appeared in frames along with detection confidence scores, though no facial recognition was found.

Flock did not dispute the technical findings. The company pointed to its vulnerability disclosure process and noted no report had been submitted through that channel before publication. The hackers deliberately avoided contacting Flock, citing concerns about legal risk from physically removing company hardware. This incident arrives amid broader backlash against Flock's camera network: towns across the US have canceled contracts, grassroots vandalism has become common, and US lawmakers Ron Wyden and Raja Krishnamoorthi have urged the FTC to investigate Flock's failure to require multi-factor authentication on police accounts accessing its camera system.
