Unknown attackers breached two Nikkei employee email accounts in separate waves. A Google Workspace account was accessed in late July; the company discovered the intrusion in early August and reset the password. The exposed records covered 1,646 employees and business partners by name and email, but Nikkei stated the dataset excluded readers and interviewees. A second intrusion hit a Microsoft 365 account in September; on September 30th the actor used it to send roughly 9,000 phishing emails containing malicious links to internal staff and interviewees. No threat group has claimed either incident and Nikkei has not confirmed a link between them.

This is not a standard credential-stuffing story. The attacker moved from exposure of 1,646 personal records in July to active weaponization of a corporate email pipeline in September, using the same organization as both victim and delivery platform for 9,000 targeted messages. A media company that relies on interviewee trust is exposed in a different way than a generic enterprise: the phishing explicitly targeted people who had been in recent contact with employees, which turns the breach into a direct supply-chain risk for sources rather than just an internal IT incident. The absence of attribution and the unconfirmed connection between the two breaches mean defenders should treat them as a possible sustained campaign rather than isolated mistakes.

Nikkei changed both account passwords and reports no further unauthorized logins. Defenders should audit all Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 sign-in logs for late July through September, look for the malicious links sent on September 30th, and notify the 1,646 affected individuals plus the interviewee pool that received phishing. Because the company warned that new attacks may impersonate Nikkei or subsidiaries, staff should verify unexpected email via a separate channel rather than clicking embedded links. Monitor for impersonation domains mimicking the media giant.

The assessment would shift significantly if Nikkei confirms a link between the July and September incidents or if a threat actor claims responsibility. Another change would come from new telemetry showing exploitation beyond the 9,000 sent emails, or from evidence that the malicious links delivered malware rather than simple phishing pages.
