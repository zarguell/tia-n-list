On Thursday, October 2, attackers took over Microsoft's official @Microsoft account on X and turned its 13 million followers into the audience for a token scam. The account followed and reposted @clippymsftcto, an impersonation of the retired Office paperclip assistant, and swapped the profile picture for Clippy imagery. A second account, @ClippyMSFT, amplified the repost while pushing a $Clippy token that claimed to have a "liquidity pool paired directly with $MSFT." Microsoft confirmed "unauthorized access to our account on X," removed the posts, and secured the account; @clippymsftcto has been suspended and Microsoft has not disclosed how access was obtained. An apology acknowledging the unauthorized use of the Clippy brand appeared roughly 30 minutes after the scam posts and was itself deleted soon after.

This is trust arbitrage, not a breach: nothing was stolen from Microsoft, the product is whatever buyers put into the $Clippy pool during the window the hijacked account stays live. Pairing the token's liquidity to the $MSFT ticker is the load-bearing trick, because it manufactures the impression of corporate backing at zero cost, and the verified 13M-follower account supplies the distribution and the proof. The attacker does not need a mass event; a few buyers converting at peak attention covers the entire operation. That profile, no CVE, no malware, no infrastructure to sinkhole, means the incident scores low on vulnerability-driven radar while doing more immediate financial harm per minute than most exploited CVEs manage per week.

Defenders who hold branded social accounts should treat them as privileged access, not marketing property: enforce hardware-backed two-factor on the X account, audit connected applications and delegated posting tools for anything unexpected, rotate credentials, and pull the account's audit history plus the email notifications X sends for logins and security-setting changes covering the days around October 2. Microsoft has not named the entry path, so both credential theft and a compromised third-party social media tool remain live hypotheses until the company reports. Hunt outward as well: clone accounts like @ClippyMSFT and copycat $Clippy or $MSFT-paired tokens survive long after the original posts are deleted, and impersonation reports plus trademark takedowns to exchanges are the only practical containment for the token side.

Two developments would change this assessment. Microsoft disclosing the entry vector would set the blast radius for every enterprise social account: credential compromise points at X-side hygiene, a compromised third-party tool points at an entire vendor category. Evidence of real draw on the $Clippy pool, or a second victim account in the same playbook, would move this from a contained prank to a repeatable monetization template.
