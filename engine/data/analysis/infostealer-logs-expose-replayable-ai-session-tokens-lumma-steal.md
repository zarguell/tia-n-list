There is no CVE in this story because there is no bug to patch. Lumma Stealer and Vidar, working alongside StealC, RedLine, Acreed and Atomic Stealer on macOS, lift secrets that are already valid: browser session cookies, JWTs, encrypted JWEs and plaintext API keys for AI platforms, bundled as stealer logs and sold or given away on Telegram. Okta's analysis of a 7 GB dump released August 2, 2026 sizes one such batch at 5,871 infected machines across 162 countries holding 44,791 unique JWTs, of which 555 authenticated AI services, plus 2,937 JWE structures set mostly by OpenAI through NextAuth.js; 1,843 tokens were still unexpired on release day and 17.7 percent of the JWTs carried plaintext PII that never expires. A TruffleHog pass over the same dump surfaced 24 live API keys for Google Gemini, OpenAI, Groq and OpenRouter. Replay is the whole attack: a stolen session token authenticates as the victim with no password and no MFA prompt, which is why Anthropic spent late August forcibly signing users out, wiping saved payment methods and refunding unauthorized charges after Vidar and LummaC2 infections fed the theft of Claude sessions.

What makes this worse than ordinary credential theft is that one replay transfers four assets at once: a searchable archive of everything employees pasted into prompts, execution authority, standing OAuth grants into CRM and mail, and an unbounded billing line. SOCRadar's exposure data puts the victim pool at more than one million infostealer records tied to AI services across 80,000-plus corporate domains, narrowing to 482 major enterprises, 68 percent of them billion-dollar firms across 36 countries, holding 5,434 records tied to 1,500 corporate emails, with 295 of those companies appearing only in the last 90 days. ChatGPT or OpenAI sessions show up for 358 of the 482 and those firms carry roughly 90 percent of all records, which reads as a census of shadow AI rather than a verdict on any vendor; technology firms are the largest block at 144 companies, but LLM-platform exposure peaks in energy at 93 percent of affected companies. The claim the event list never quite makes: rotating the password evicts nobody, because the cookie is the session, so the remediation unit is the token, and the loss lands in cloud billing rather than in any security console, as in Unit 42's response cases approaching one million dollars in charges and the stolen METR key that burned $600,000 in credits over weeks without detection.

Start with the hunt, not the policy memo. Query stealer-log collections for corporate domains (SOCRadar's free checker covers 44 AI platforms), and treat any hit as an endpoint compromise with provider-side cleanup: revoke all sessions, rotate and reissue API keys, strip stored payment methods, reimage the machine. Kill the replay surfaces Okta calls out: IP allowlisting on AI apps defeats session replay outright, and API keys should move out of environment variables and config files into OAuth 2.0 flows with short-lived access tokens, refresh-token rotation, and scoping with usage caps, alerting on unfamiliar ASNs and odd-hour spend, the LLMjacking fingerprint. Concrete telemetry to chase: the Go-http-client/2.0 user agent with gzip(gfe) Unit 42 tied to malicious API calls, malicious login sources 117.72.74[.]48, 207.246.106[.]162, 23.236.182[.]215 and 95.214.112[.]26, transfer-station infrastructure amutes[.]com and abb1[.]life, and the fact that newer stealer builds ship regex and glob rules for Anthropic and OpenAI key formats and known AI-tool config paths, which makes those config paths themselves worth EDR coverage. Device Bound Session Credentials, present in Chrome 145 for Windows since March 2026 and rolled out with Chrome 146 in April, binds sessions to hardware, but it helps only where the provider implements it server-side, so it is a selection criterion for AI vendors today rather than a deployed control.

Two developments would change this assessment. The first is provider-side adoption of device-bound sessions: if OpenAI or Google ship DBSC-compatible authentication, the replay market collapses for those platforms while the rest of the 44 indexed platforms stay exposed, and pricing on the underground would say which. The second is the pivot: no confirmed case yet exists of a replayed AI session being used to reach enterprise data through standing OAuth grants, a stolen Zapier session scripting scheduled exfiltration from the vendor's own trusted IP space, and the SOCRadar data shows agent and automation exposure already concentrates in healthcare, financial services and technology. That first documented pivot converts this story from billing fraud into a reportable data breach, and it is the trigger to treat AI platforms as tier-one identity providers rather than ordinary SaaS.
