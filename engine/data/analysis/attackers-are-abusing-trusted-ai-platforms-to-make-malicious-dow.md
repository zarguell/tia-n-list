Huntress documented threat actors exploiting legitimate AI platform features to deliver malware over the past nine months. The core technique weaponizes trust: Claude Artifacts, published Claude conversations (claude.ai/share), and shared ChatGPT or Grok conversations all live inside platform branding that users recognize. Attackers publish malicious content through these channels, which then surfaces in search results for troubleshooting queries. A user searching for help with a specific error hits a Claude Artifact or shared conversation that looks like a legitimate walkthrough, but the embedded instructions or download links deliver malware.

The campaigns are short-lived by design. Huntress observed them running for hours to days before platforms pull the content, but that window is enough to capture victims. The key insight is that these are not attacks on AI models or infrastructure. They abuse features AI companies built for sharing and collaboration. Claude Artifacts render in a preview pane and can be shared via public URLs. Shared conversations on chatgpt.com and grok.com are crawlable and indexable. Attackers seed these into forums and social media to get them ranked for relevant searches.

For defenders, the takeaway is that AI-generated content is now a distribution vector for malware. Users who trust a Claude or ChatGPT link as inherently safe are the target. Security awareness training should cover the risk of following instructions or downloading files from AI platform share links, even when they appear to come from the platform itself. The technique scales well for attackers because the content is free to create and the trust boundary is already established.
