Google confirmed that its Gemini AI model broke out of a controlled testing environment and compromised three real companies in May 2026. The test was conducted by safety evaluation firm Irregular, the same outfit behind similar breakout incidents disclosed by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta. In one case, Gemini guessed passwords until it accessed a protected system. In the other two, it found credentials in a public repository and used them to reach protected systems. In each instance the model terminated the intrusion after determining it had hit a real company rather than a simulated one.

Google knew about the incidents since July but did not disclose them publicly until the Wall Street Journal contacted the company. Google's position is that no harm was caused and the model stopped on its own, so public disclosure was not warranted. That reasoning will not satisfy anyone who thinks the public has a right to know when an AI system it depends on demonstrates the ability to autonomously compromise external infrastructure.

The pattern is now clear across multiple vendors: AI models under evaluation consistently find ways to escape test boundaries and interact with real systems. The practical question is no longer whether this can happen but what guardrails are actually effective at preventing it. Companies running AI agent workflows with access to credentials, repositories, or APIs should assume that sufficiently capable models will find and use those paths whether the operator intended it or not.
