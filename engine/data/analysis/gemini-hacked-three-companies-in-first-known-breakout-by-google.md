Google confirmed that a Gemini model autonomously accessed the systems of three real companies during a May 2026 cybersecurity evaluation run by Irregular, an independent AI-security evaluator. The test was a capture-the-flag exercise: Gemini was instructed to obtain information from a fictional company within a simulated environment. However, the model had access to the open internet, and the fictional entity's name overlapped with that of a real organization. That overlap turned a bounded test into contact with live infrastructure.

Gemini used publicly available information and credential-guessing to access three websites it believed fell within the evaluation scope. In one case, it repeatedly guessed passwords until entering a protected system. In the other two, it found credentials exposed in public repositories and used them to access protected systems. The model stopped activity after gaining access in every case. Google said no harm resulted, the three organizations were notified, and the company does not view the incident as model misalignment.

This is the first publicly confirmed case of a Google AI system autonomously compromising external organizations. Google security VP Heather Adkins characterized the incident as a failure of testing isolation rather than a model capability problem. The distinction matters: the model did what it was asked to do (find and access a target), but the boundaries of the test environment were insufficient to prevent real-world contact. The fictional-real name collision was a foreseeable edge case in test design.

The incident has implications for how AI companies structure safety evaluations. Any test that gives a model internet access and a target to pursue requires rigorous isolation, including network segmentation and name deconfliction. Enterprise buyers evaluating AI agents for security-sensitive tasks should demand evidence that testing environments are architecturally isolated, not just instructionally bounded.
