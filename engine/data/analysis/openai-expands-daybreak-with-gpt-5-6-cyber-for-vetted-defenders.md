OpenAI expanded its Daybreak cybersecurity program into two tiers backed by a purpose-trained model. Daybreak Blue gives approved defenders general-purpose frontier models, including GPT-5.6 Sol, for vulnerability discovery, secure code review, malware analysis, incident response, and patch validation. Daybreak Red unlocks GPT-5.6-Cyber, built on GPT-5.6 Sol and purpose-trained for authorized vulnerability research, exploit validation, and security testing. Access is restricted to a vetted list of companies including Accenture, IBM, Capgemini, Cognizant, EY, KPMG, PwC, NCC Group, SpecterOps, and security vendors such as Palo Alto Networks, CrowdStrike, Cisco, Sophos, Akamai, Fortinet, and Cloudflare. OpenAI says the model will not be available to general users, citing abuse risk.

The structure is notable for how explicitly it mirrors the offensive-defensive asymmetry debate. Rather than offering one cyber model, OpenAI split the capability surface: general frontier models for defensive work and a purpose-trained offensive-capable model gated behind vetting. That separation is the real design decision, and it puts OpenAI in the position of arbitrating who gets exploit-grade AI assistance, a role that will draw scrutiny from both sides.

Watch for two things. First, whether the vetted-defender list holds up in practice, since access control on a model is only as good as the key management and downstream redistribution controls around it. Second, whether GPT-5.6-Cyber changes the zero-day economics, since the firms with access also run the bug-bounty and exploit-broker ecosystems that determine what gets disclosed and what gets sold.
