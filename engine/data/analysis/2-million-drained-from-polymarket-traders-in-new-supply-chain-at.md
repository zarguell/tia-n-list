The incident is a third-party script supply-chain compromise, not a direct breach of Polymarket's code. On October 29, 2026, malicious JavaScript served from a DebugBear real-user-monitoring address reached Polymarket's international site. The payload, obtained by Hudson Rock, scanned for a logged-in user's trading tools and portfolio, then either sold positions and moved proceeds or sent position tokens directly to an attacker-controlled address. It also read credential-related material. DebugBear confirmed an ongoing security incident affecting its RUM product and told customers to pull its snippet. Polymarket halted trading in cancel-only mode, resumed roughly an hour later, and pledged full reimbursement. Losses cited exceed $2 million, with some reports tracking roughly $2.65 million to a single attacker wallet. The US platform was reportedly unaffected.

This is more dangerous than a standard phishing campaign because the attacker did not need credentials; the malicious snippet executed inside an authenticated session and exfiltrated funds by abusing the platform's own trading interfaces. With over $2 million drained and Hudson Rock confirming the payload targeted both portfolio data and session tokens, any trader who loaded the compromised script must treat session exposure as a certainty, not a possibility. The vendor has not disclosed how long the malicious version was live or whether other DebugBear customers received it, meaning the blast radius is unknown. The reason this story demands attention over other supply-chain incidents is that a prediction market's value is tied to real-time position liquidity; draining wallets through an embedded analytics script undermines the core trading mechanism rather than just stealing static records.

Defenders should inventory all embedded third-party snippets (DebugBear RUM, analytics, chat widgets) and treat them as mutable supply-chain surfaces. Pull any DebugBear embed immediately and audit build artifacts for the commit message `chore(polymarket): remove debugbear rum script` or similar removals. Hunt for requests to DebugBear-associated addresses serving unexpected JavaScript, check logs for session reads followed by abnormal position-sales, and review any token-transfer transactions to the attacker-set wallet. Polymarket users should rotate credentials, revoke active sessions, and verify reimbursement status, since reimbursement covers losses but does not eliminate session exposure.

Watch for DebugBear's incident response disclosure: whether the compromise started before October 29, whether other customers served the malicious snippet, and whether additional attacker wallets appear. Also watch for any change in Polymarket's vendor naming or a formal loss total from the company, since it has not yet named the vendor or confirmed the full loss figure beyond the external estimates.
