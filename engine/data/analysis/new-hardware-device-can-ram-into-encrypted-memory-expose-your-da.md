Researchers from KU Leuven, ETH Zurich, Durham University, and Google demonstrated DDRop, a hardware interposer attack that breaks DDR5 memory encryption in confidential computing environments. The attack uses a custom circuit board costing under $200 that sits between the processor and memory module, corrupting DDR5 bus commands to silently drop writes to encrypted memory. The protected VM continues operating on stale data that still decrypts correctly, enabling a replay attack.

DDRop breaks the integrity of Intel TDX, Scalable SGX, and AMD SEV-SNP. The researchers demonstrated a proof-of-concept on a current Intel TDX server, achieving two attacks: injecting malicious secure page-table entries to force any protected VM into debug mode and read its private memory in plaintext, and writing to critical TDX metadata structures to forge attestation reports so a backdoored VM appears trusted. Both attacks succeed deterministically in under two minutes without crashing the machine.

This is the first active interposer attack on DDR5, succeeding at full bus speed rather than requiring the bus to be slowed to its lowest speed as with prior passive attacks. The researchers note there is no easy fix for Intel's and AMD's current scalable memory-encryption designs because the architecture deliberately trades cryptographic freshness for the ability to protect large amounts of memory. Intel and AMD both stated the attack is out of scope for their cloud computing threat model. The complete interposer design is being released as open-source hardware.
