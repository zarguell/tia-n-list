BITTER, also tracked as TA397, is a suspected South Asian cyberespionage group active since at least 2013 that targets government, energy, and engineering organizations, with FortiGuard telemetry also showing activity in Southeast Asia and South Korea. In a campaign tracked over several months, FortiGuard Labs attributes the activity to BITTER with high confidence based on lure filenames, command-and-control patterns, file metadata, and malware analysis, with the original research published on 2 November 2026. The campaign delivers a custom remote access trojan called PapRAT through three infection chains: a fileless chain in which a malicious LNK abuses the native Windows ftp.exe utility to pipe a script into cmd and create scheduled tasks; a VHDX disk image that silently runs the MSI installer Visa_Details.msi while opening a decoy spreadsheet; and a RAR archive whose URL shortcut retrieves an LNK over WebDAV to run Imp_Details.msi. The C++ build of PapRAT masquerades as bruzsvc.exe attributed to "Microsoft Corporation" with product name "Microsoft redistributables" version 2.6.14.26, beacons over plaintext HTTP every 10 seconds with its C2 host, port, and paths XOR-encoded under key 0x50, and supports eight commands. A newer Rust build waits 42 seconds, then polls over HTTPS every 5 seconds, and adds PowerShell execution, sleep, and clean exit for eleven commands total, with constants reversed and ROT-3 shifted to reveal a C2 at www.rivingtonclarks.com/banana/. In the third chain PapRAT also downloads WmRAT as vnces.exe, reaching out to netmansrvdns.com. No vulnerability or CVE is named; the campaign relies on deception and living-off-the-land techniques rather than exploitation.

The dangerous half of this campaign is the chain that deploys no malware executable at all, because signature-based and file-focused endpoint controls have nothing to alert on, yet the attacker still gains persistent command execution through scheduled tasks named MicrosoftUpdates\updates11678 and BiosUpdates\update101. A successful intrusion therefore means sustained access to government and critical-infrastructure targets that patching cannot close, since there is no patchable flaw in play, and deleting the first implant found may leave WmRAT behind. The server-side staging model, where Base64-encoded scripts fetched into err.log or talk.log are updated without pushing a new binary, gives the operator flexibility and keeps PE drops and process injection off the endpoint. Exposure is concentrated in South Asia but FortiGuard telemetry extends it to Southeast Asia and South Korea, and the victim set spans government, energy, and engineering. This story earns attention over the routine malware item because it documents a named actor's tradecraft evolution from C++ to Rust and from eight to eleven commands, ships concrete filenames, task names, and domains that defenders can hunt immediately, and shows native Windows tools being co-opted before any custom payload appears.

Defenders should hunt for scheduled tasks MicrosoftUpdates\updates11678 running every 30 minutes and BiosUpdates\update101 running every 15 minutes with conhost --headless cmd /c more ...|cmd, along with the staging files C:\users\public\music\err.log, C:\programdata\talk.log, and abc.pdf, plus ftp.exe launching shell commands via the "!" prefix, curl invoked with --ssl-no-revoke -k, and mounted VHDX images. Block or alert on the listed command-and-control domains, goodnfortunes.com, techtalkhelp.com, rivingtonclarks.com, rappellingaart.com, gomescareerplans.com, chestergreenfarming.com, and netmansrvdns.com, and inspect unexpected LNK, URL, VHDX, and MSI files such as Visa_Details.msi and Imp_Details.msi. Shane Barney of Keeper Security advises enforcing least-privilege access, restricting native scripting engines, and removing standing administrative rights, and FortiGuard recommends treating unexpected LNK, internet shortcut, disk image, and MSI files with care. Watch also for conhost running headless and WebDAV access to untrusted hosts.

The assessment would change if FortiGuard's telemetry confirms expansion beyond South Asia into Southeast Asia and South Korea at scale, if the full indicator set or a CERT advisory is published, or if later reporting links the campaign to a specific state sponsor beyond Proofpoint's June 2025 assessment that BITTER collects intelligence aligned with Indian government interests. A discovery that the campaign also exploited a named vulnerability rather than deception alone would reframe it from a detection-and-hunting problem into a patching one. Conversely, publication of the C2 panel infrastructure or the fruit-word naming convention behind the "Banana Login" panel could expose operator-side artifacts that improve attribution and blocking.
