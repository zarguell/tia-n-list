Federal prosecutors in Rhode Island charged Juan Ricardo Palacio Escobar, the 52-year-old co-founder and CTO of ransomwarehelp.com, with three counts of wire fraud in an indictment filed Nov 18, 2026. The Florida firm, operating for about six years from 2020, advertised advanced cryptographic tools, 100 percent data recovery and a success rate above 99 percent, while stating that it never paid threat actors on clients' behalf. Prosecutors and the FBI describe the opposite: in a controlled operation the bureau played both the ransomware victim and the attacker, and the firm paid about $6,003 in bitcoin for a key after quoting the victim $16,010, including a $2,750 assessment and a $13,260 recovery fee.

The material harm is not one inflated invoice. A recovery vendor that secretly buys the decryptor turns the victim's payment into funding for the same extortion operation it claimed to bypass, and the markup hides the real ransom price from the victim, insurers and any later incident record. The arithmetic scales: reporting on the indictment describes a Kansas business billed $720,000 while roughly $445,000 went to the gang in bitcoin, leaving a spread that dwarfs the sting's $6,003 payment. This is a failure in the recovery channel responders fall back on when backups fail, and an October case against MonsterCloud's owner allegedly involved more than $19 million billed and over $8 million sent to attackers, so the case reads as a pattern rather than a lone bad actor.

Defenders should stop treating a no-payment pledge as a trust signal. Require written disclosure of any third-party ransom payment, itemized invoices and proof of decryption capability before contracting, and reconcile every recovery quote with outbound bitcoin flows in the incident's payment records. For this case, the hunt is concrete: search email and chat with ransomwarehelp.com, procurement and invoice records carrying the $2,750 assessment or $13,260 quote, and bitcoin transactions near the $6,003 payment in the Rhode Island and Kansas matters. Report the intrusion to the FBI, keep offline backups, and preserve those records for the docket.

The assessment changes in two ways. If the docket adds counts, corporate victim names, restitution figures or charges against other RansomwareHelp staff, the Kansas billing stops looking exceptional and the exposure estimate moves from one firm to a wider customer base. If evidence shows the company did use legitimate decryption tooling in some cases, or the case ends in a narrow plea, the allegation shifts from a blanket false capability claim to a limited misrepresentation about payments.
