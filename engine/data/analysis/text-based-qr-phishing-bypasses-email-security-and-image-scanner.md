Kaspersky researchers highlighted a growing evasion technique in QR-code phishing: attackers are building QR codes directly in emails using HTML table cells or Unicode text characters instead of embedding them as image attachments. The email client renders the black-and-white grid as a fully scannable QR code, but because no image object exists in the message, Secure Email Gateways that rely on image inspection, OCR, and QR decoding fail to detect the malicious URL.

ESET's H1 2026 Threat Report places QRCode/Phishing among the top email threat detections at 4.1% of the top 10, alongside HTML/Phishing.Agent, malicious Office documents, and JavaScript downloaders. The technique is effective because the scanning and browsing happens on a personal mobile device, outside corporate security controls and endpoint monitoring. Typical lures impersonate IT departments, HR, cloud providers, delivery services, or banks, claiming the recipient must scan to view a secure document or verify their identity.

This is a practical gap in current SEG architecture. Text-rendered QR codes bypass image-focused scanners entirely, and URL-scanning engines cannot see the encoded destination until the code is physically scanned. Organizations should consider adding QR-code decoding to their email security pipeline and training users to treat any unsolicited QR code in email as suspicious regardless of how it appears technically.
