Thousands of ASOS customers received a push notification through the retailer's own mobile app around 5:00 a.m. ET on Tuesday, titled "ASOS HACKED" and addressed to the company's data protection officer and IT staff. The message claimed the sender had "fully compromised the Snowflake instance" and threatened to leak the data unless engaged. ASOS shares fell almost 12 percent on the London Stock Exchange after the reports, which the BBC, the Guardian, and others covered through the day.

ASOS has confirmed that third-party platforms used to communicate with customers were accessed without authorization and says basic personal information, including names and contact details, may have been exposed. It has not confirmed the Snowflake compromise claim and has not said how many customers are affected, but believes payment-card data and account passwords were not impacted. The company is showing an in-app notice telling customers to ignore the rogue alert and not click its link. Context from coverage: ASOS's marketing stack uses Simon AI, a personalization platform that runs on Snowflake, alongside Braze, to trigger customer communications including push notifications. Reporting links the messages to a group calling itself Xuanye, and UK outlets note National Cyber Security Centre attention to the case.

The delivery channel is what elevates this incident. Abusing the app's own notification infrastructure turns an extortion claim into a demonstration of access, visible to customers without any leak site posting. The same tactic could be run against any retailer that wires customer engagement platforms into push channels with weak tenant isolation.

Watch for ASOS or the NCSC confirming or refuting the Snowflake claim, any customer count and data-type disclosure, and copycat uses of push notifications as extortion proof. The distinction between compromised notification plumbing and compromised data stores will decide how serious this actually is.
