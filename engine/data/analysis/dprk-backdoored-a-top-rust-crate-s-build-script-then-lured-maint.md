A DPRK-linked social engineering campaign targets Rust crate maintainers through video call-based attacks. The Rust project's crates.io team and security response working group issued a warning about attackers who contact developers under the guise of job offers or contract opportunities. During video calls, victims are tricked into installing software claiming to fix a missing audio codec, or executing malicious code pasted to their clipboard. Attackers create shell companies with convincing LinkedIn pages to establish credibility.

The campaign connects to two prior incidents: a June attack targeting prominent Rust developers and the August compromise of the arrayref crate. In the August incident, North Korean threat actors compromised the arrayref developer's account and published several malicious crates. The arrayref incident surfaced on August 20 and was attributed to DPRK operators. The Rust project noted it cannot confirm whether all incidents originate from the same campaign, but observed that North Korea consistently uses this attack style across both Rust and non-Rust ecosystems.

ThreadLinqs analysis identified 9 detections and 23 indicators of compromise associated with the build script backdoor technique. The attackers backdoor a popular crate's build script, then use the video call social engineering to trick maintainers into executing the poisoned code. This supply chain approach targets the trust model of package registries, where maintainer credentials can be leveraged to push malicious updates to downstream users.
