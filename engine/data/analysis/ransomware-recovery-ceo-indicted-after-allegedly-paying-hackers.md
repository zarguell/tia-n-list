Two federal cases in this story describe ransomware recovery firms that promised decryption tools they did not have. Zohar Pinhasi, owner of MonsterCloud, was accused by the Justice Department of charging hundreds of clients more than $19 million while paying over $8 million to attackers during a five-year period ending in 2023. Prosecutors said his claimed proprietary recovery tools did not exist and that client fees funded ransom payments without customers being told. He faced two wire fraud counts and one conspiracy count. Jon DiMaggio of Arkem Cyber told CyberScoop that victims were going straight to a shady source after being hacked.

A federal grand jury also indicted Juan Ricardo Palacio Escobar, 52, a Colombian national and co-founder of a Florida data recovery company. 404 Media and Brian Krebs had previously identified his operation as Ransomwarehelp.com, whose site is now under maintenance. Prosecutors said he claimed decryption capability, negotiated with ransomware gangs in secret, paid ransoms and kept the difference. Assistant US attorney Charles Calenda said the defendant preyed on victims by falsely marketing a solution he did not possess. Escobar faces three wire fraud counts carrying a maximum of 60 years in prison. A 26 November repost of the securityonline.info report kept this second indictment in view.

Both cases point at the same gap in incident response: victims who cannot decrypt their own data turn to intermediaries with little oversight. The earlier sentencing of three former ransomware negotiators for deceiving clients and conspiring with affiliates shows the pattern is not limited to one company. Watch for trial dates, restitution orders, and any DOJ guidance on vetting recovery vendors.
