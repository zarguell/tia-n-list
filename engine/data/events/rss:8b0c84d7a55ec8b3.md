In 2024, an employee at engineering firm Arup was tricked into transferring $25 million after attending a fake video conference featuring deepfake versions of their colleagues. A year later, Marks & Spencer (M&S) suffered a major cyberattack after attackers used social engineering to gain access through a third-party helpdesk. More recently, attackers have impersonated IT support staff to convince employees to hand over remote access to their devices. While the tactics differ, these incidents raise an important question for IT and security teams: how can organizations be confident that someone requesting access or authorizing a sensitive action is who they claim to be? We examine these attacks and explore how stronger identity verification and more secure workflows can help organizations defend against similar threats. A finance employee at engineering firm Arup's Hong Kong office received an email in January 2024 that appeared to come from the company's UK-based CFO, requesting a series of transactions. Initially suspicious, the employee was convinced the request was genuine after joining a video conference featuring what appeared to be the CFO and several colleagues. However, the meeting was a deepfake. According to Hong Kong police , the attackers had used publicly available video and audio to create a convincing pre-recorded conference.
