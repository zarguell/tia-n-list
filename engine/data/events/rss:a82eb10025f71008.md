Finding a real security flaw and getting paid for it is an appealing goal when you are learning cyber security. Bug bounties give you a chance to test your skills on real software and help its developers make it safer. Headlines about programs closing or pausing under a flood of AI reports can make that route feel less certain. Earlier this year, curl, the widely used data transfer tool and library, ended its paid bounty program after similar problems . As a beginner, these decisions raise a practical question about where to put your effort. A useful starting point is understanding what went wrong, then building the skills to produce findings that someone else can verify. A bug bounty program rewards eligible security findings under a defined set of rules. Developers must still review each submission, reproduce the issue and assess its impact. A convincing report about an imaginary vulnerability can consume time that a small security team needs for genuine problems. When curl ended its program on January 31, 2026, maintainer Daniel Stenberg explained how the quality of submissions had deteriorated. He confirmed vulnerabilities fell from above 15% in previous years to below 5% starting in 2025. Across its lifetime, the program had helped uncover 87 confirmed vulnerabilities and paid researchers more than $100,000. Those figures describe curl’s experience specifically.
