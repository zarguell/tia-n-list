As federal agencies revise their strategies for implementing post-quantum encryption by President Donald Trump’s new 2030 deadline , agency leaders and federal contractors are mulling over how to adapt the new protections to older, legacy technology and address gaps in visibility over federal encryption protocols. Dan Pomeroy, deputy associate administrator at the Office of Technology Policy at the General Services Administration, said his agency focused first on the smart Personal Identity Verification (PIV) cards that grant employees access to GSA facilities, computers and the authority to sign official documents. Pomeroy said agency technologists ran new post-quantum encryption algorithms directly onto the card chip, issued new credentials, and built the surrounding policies, certifications and hardware security modules to support the transition. Post-quantum cryptography tends to require larger keys and signatures, along with a fast, reliable network connection to process larger amounts of data. That could be a problem for assets like satellites, which have weaker connections. But in many cases, agencies can switch to newer algorithms using the same systems, data and devices already in use throughout the government. “That work demonstrated that quantum-resistant credentials are not theoretical, they are achievable with the cards we have today, the technology we have today,” said Pomeroy Tuesday at FedTalks presented by FedScoop.
