In August, the Federal Bureau of Investigation announced that it detected and disrupted another Chinese state-sponsored cyberattack targeting U.S. critical infrastructure — the latest in a series that includes the 2025 PlugX malware incident, the 2024 Flax Typhoon hack and the 2023 Volt Typhoon operation. The severity and frequency of these attacks should raise alarms because they show a pattern of America’s adversaries looking for any possible weakness in our sensitive systems to exploit, and in many cases cooperating together to do so. These attacks are often aimed at the federal government’s own networks, and they are all too often successful. The incoming House and Senate cyber leaders will need to assess and address this vulnerability fast. The math isn’t on the government’s side. Cyber adversaries only have to be right once, whereas defenders must be right across every system, program and vendor. Unfortunately, the very way that the federal government contracts and manages critical systems makes the job of its cyber defenders much more difficult. Federal systems are often cobbled together over decades, often with unpredictable budgets, shifting missions and procurement rules that favor incremental fixes over true modernization. The outdated technology is often easy to identify.
