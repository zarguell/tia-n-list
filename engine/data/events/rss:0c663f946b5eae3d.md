Earlier this month, attackers switched refrigeration systems in supermarkets across nine European countries into defrost mode . Stores were forced to throw out spoiled stock, and as is now being reported, some blood banks in France were hit as well. Around the same time, U.S. agencies including the FBI and CISA warned that internet-exposed refrigeration and temperature controllers were being actively targeted. The attackers didn’t get in by breaking encryption or cracking passwords. They found admin interfaces exposed to the internet that never should have been public facing, along with known vulnerabilities and, in some cases, default credentials. In other words, unnecessary attack surfaces, unpatched systems, and factory default passwords. They didn’t need a quantum cryptography breakthrough. They had an open door. That’s worth keeping in mind as the post-quantum cryptography (PQC) conversation picks up speed. New algorithms have been standardized , governments are setting migration timelines , and vendors are urging organizations to start migrating. Quantum computing’s potential to break widely used encryption deserves serious preparation. But nearly all of that guidance is targeted at IT. In operational technology (OT), the PQC timeline looks very different, and the risks hurting organizations today are frankly much lower-hanging fruit.
