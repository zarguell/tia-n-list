In his LABScon 26 talk, Mauro Vignati, Advisor on Digital Technologies of Warfare at the International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), argues that guardrails cannot address the deeper problem with frontier AI. Advanced cognitive capabilities are now distributed through the same on-demand, as-a-service model that has shaped industry, and later cybercrime, for seventy years. That model was designed for industrial efficiency, not for technology this powerful. Vignati traces the model back to Toyota’s post-war just-in-time production system, built on the principle that customers get only what they need and pay only for what they get. The IT needed to coordinate that system helped give rise to application service providers and eventually to software-as-a-service. Cybercriminals later adopted the same logic for crimeware and ransomware, and hacktivists for DDoS campaigns. In each case the people taking part needed no technical skill of their own. Model-as-a-service differs from its predecessors because the capability is dual-use at the source, supplied by private technology firms largely independent of who the end users are or what they intend. Vignati explains why criminals who tried to build their own AI systems have largely turned to wrapping commercial ones instead, leaving the legitimate economy to carry the cost of training and infrastructure.
