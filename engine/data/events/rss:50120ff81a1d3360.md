The cybersecurity industry is currently captivated by the emergence of autonomous, agentic offensive frameworks. With recent reporting from Google , CrowdStrike , Microsoft , and the FBI have all been highlighting the operational use of tools like ARTEX, Cairn, CyberStrikeAI, and PentAGI, among others. The prevailing narrative assumes every cyber threat actor is racing to automate intrusions from end to end. From my vantage point of tracking ransomware campaigns daily, however, a striking contradiction emerges. While lower-tier crews and emerging actors are experimenting with agentic tooling, top-tier Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) affiliates, like Akira, Qilin, or Rhysida, are conspicuously absent from this trend. Understanding why requires looking past the hype of autonomous exploitation and examining the actual operational, economic, and architectural realities of running ransomware attacks. This article was also inspired by me recently pondering to the Twitter/X hivemind about why we are not seeing all of the top ransomware gangs using ARTEX and other AI pentesting tools. Experimenters vs. Established Affiliates Newer outfits and smaller extortion groups are visibly testing the waters. Researchers at CtrlAltIntel have seen WhiteArmor, for instance, leveraging ARTEX, though with notoriously poor operational security, operating the framework from the exact same VPS infrastructure tied to their original clearnet domain.
