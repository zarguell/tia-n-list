How Russians use "Made in USA" AI for cyberattacks, espionage, and disinformation

Kremlin-backed actors have used Anthropic's Claude AI system to program autonomous lethal drones, enhance their cyber and influence operations, and evade detection, the American company announced. Anthropic's report is the latest warning from companies, think tanks, and other experts that Russia, China, Iran, and other countries are using American-made tools to attack targets worldwide.

Between December 2025 and August 2026, Russian state-sponsored cyber actors integrated commercial American AI models -- specifically Anthropic's Haiku, Sonnet, and Opus (the three performance tiers of the Claude language model family) -- directly into their offensive toolset, executing global multi-stage cyber campaigns at unprecedented speed with minimal human intervention.

The main threat identified is GTG-20006, an internal designation for a group whose tactics, techniques, procedures, targets, and Russian-language operations directly link to Midnight Blizzard (also known as APT29 or Cozy Bear) -- the Russian state intelligence unit responsible for the SolarWinds supply chain breach. Operational indicators showed key infrastructure was managed by a Russian-speaking actor using the pseudonym "JackPoterz."

Instead of relying on large teams of specialized human engineers, JackPoterz and a small group of coordinators maintained complex multi-victim global campaigns acting as high-level managers. The hackers completely changed how cyberattacks work. Instead of using AI only for questions or text generation, they linked Claude into a network of digital agents working autonomously end-to-end.

Humans no longer do routine work. The hackers' role reduced to coordinators who select targets, provide general instructions to the robots, and analyze stolen data at the end. Everything else -- victim reconnaissance, lure site creation, phishing email distribution, and data extraction -- is handled entirely by automated programs.

The biggest change is in malware development. Previously, cyber espionage was a cat-and-mouse game: hackers wrote a virus, security firms detected and blocked it, forcing attackers to start over. Russians changed this by having AI test viruses against commercial antivirus software itself. If the AI sees security software detects the virus, it rewrites and modifies the code on the spot, repeatedly, until it becomes completely invisible. Once it passes scans, the AI automatically uploads the code to the internet and infects victims. Once inside networks, the software bots read thousands of emails, documents, and intercepted conversations, extract only truly valuable information, and send it directly to human coordinators.

A global theater of operations

Anthropic identified over 20 high-value target organizations targeted by GTG-20006's AI-accelerated operations across five major regions:

- Ukraine: The largest concentration of attacks hit Ukrainian government ministries, military personnel, diplomatic staff, and defense companies. Operatives scanned remote access systems in over two dozen Ukrainian state organizations.

- Europe: Targets included European defense industry companies, foreign ministries, diplomatic missions, and think tanks.

- United States: Operations targeted think tanks, foreign policy organizations, and individual experts connected directly to American foreign policy.

- Asia and Middle East: The group compromised a Southeast Asian government maritime agency monitoring maritime transport and vessel tracking, along with Middle East targets.

- North Africa: Operators compromised a North African government technology authority by stealing administrative credentials for state VPN equipment.

According to Anthropic's report, the Russian agents' campaign heavily focused on Ukraine's military drone supply chains. Actors mass-exported entire email inboxes from drone component manufacturers and targeted military drone manufacturers.

In one case, the group stole a complete proprietary software development kit for a drone computer vision system. Operatives then spent several days using AI models to reverse-engineer the drone's vision firmware, extracting product architecture, hardware bill of materials, supplier dependencies, and details about an unreleased product. AI control and vision firmware for military drones represented a maximum-priority espionage target for battlefield advantage.

Anthropic also stated that regarding drones, Russians used Claude to develop an AI model small enough to run on single-board development boards but powerful enough to allow a drone to "select targets (including a 'person' target class) and issue detonation commands without human intervention."

The group also used Claude to create software enabling autonomous drones to communicate with each other to improve target identification. Although the Russian group had not yet deployed the model in the field, they "were using real hardware-in-the-loop tests within their sessions," according to the report cited by Defense One.

Other incidents

To reach high-value targets without launching direct attacks, Russian operators compromised at least three Horeca software providers offering hotel guest Wi-Fi services. Using a technique named CaptiveCrunch -- which Microsoft Threat Intelligence independently documented in July 2026 -- actors took control of Domain Name System (DNS) settings, automatically redirecting hotel guests' web traffic to attacker-controlled servers.

When high-ranking guests, including Ukrainian diplomats traveling abroad, connected to hotel Wi-Fi, their device identifiers and IP addresses were recorded. They were then shown "ClickFix" social engineering lures designed to install malware on Windows, Android, and iOS devices. Stolen hotel guest data was cross-referenced with device telemetry to isolate priority individuals for subsequent surveillance.

Simultaneously, the group targeted communication and physical security networks through two main techniques:

- Automated WhatsApp account takeovers: Using headless browsers and the open-source automation library WPPConnect, hackers hijacked targets' WhatsApp accounts by connecting as "secondary devices." The AI configuration disabled read receipts to hide the intrusion while mass-exporting conversation history in Ukrainian and Russian, successfully compromising at least two former senior Ukrainian officials.

- Live surveillance video stream hijacking: Hackers exploited authorization vulnerabilities in camera streaming platform APIs. By enumerating user databases and harvesting access tokens, the group obtained direct access to live video feeds from security cameras in targeted regions.

Experts also noted that the deployed campaigns bear the hallmarks of Russian influence operations, such as fake news sites and journalist profiles spreading lies about NATO activity in Baltic countries. Anthropic stated that American-made AI tools helped optimize the process, increasing disinformation volume and creating much more credible fake reporters for local audiences.

Russia-linked groups "automated much of their operations, from development, infrastructure acquisition, phishing, persistence through command and control, to data exfiltration," the report states. The group even used AI to forge documents from the Gendarmerie and Ministry of Defense of the Central African Republic.

Another group, GTG-84005, used AI to steal census data and public information allowing agents to tailor messaging campaigns for specific audiences in Malaysia, where the Turkish-origin group "laundered Russian and Chinese state media content, presenting it as independent journalism."

Strategic objectives

Primary objectives behind GTG-20006's campaign were state strategic espionage, military intelligence acquisition, and asymmetric capability development.

By targeting military drone manufacturers and software developers, Russian intelligence services sought direct technological advantage in battlefield automated capabilities, particularly in computer vision and AI-guided firmware for unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs).

Simultaneously, compromising diplomatic communications, government remote access portals, foreign policy think tanks, and live surveillance cameras provided a real-time tactical picture of policy decisions, troop movements, and Western military support supply chains.

Anthropic's Threat Intelligence team cut off GTG-20006's platform access in August 2026, subsequently updating model protection mechanisms and sharing threat data with authorities and industry partners.

Defense One noted that Anthropic's recent report came just two weeks after OpenAI revealed Russian actors used ChatGPT to create a fake "expert community" called the "International Burke Institute" to promote Kremlin messaging to international audiences.

This campaign activity extended across multiple online platforms, targeting users in Europe, the United States, and other strategic regions. The AI model was primarily used to accelerate content production, improve foreign-language text quality, and simulate opinion diversity of real users, facilitating rapid spread of Moscow-favorable narratives.

Although Anthropic and OpenAI managed to block the schemes they discovered, such operations will likely continue as Russia uses AI to "scale industrial production of Kremlin-aligned content," according to an August report by the Institute for the Study of War (ISW).

Russia also exploits young people's curiosity about AI to increase the number of people spreading Russian disinformation, offering young people worldwide "internships and training in AI-based media" through university donations and related programs.

Thus, the Kremlin extends its cognitive warfare strategy by building a global network of journalists and content creators trained to use AI to promote Russian narratives. Through state media training programs like SputnikPro and RT Academy, Moscow recruits young professionals from Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the post-Soviet space. Courses increasingly emphasize using AI tools to accelerate news production, generate multimedia content, and manage social media campaigns, allowing students to multiply disinformation volume at minimal cost and increased speed.

The long-term goal of this initiative is creating a global influence resource operating independently of official Russian state channels, making disinformation resilient to potential Western blocks or sanctions. In regions like Africa and Latin America, these media cadres are prepared to undermine trust in democratic institutions and shift perceptions of the Ukraine war, while in the former Soviet space and occupied Ukrainian territories, programs aim to ensure lasting informational control and indoctrinate new generations of bloggers and journalists.

According to industry experts, Anthropic's report marks a definitive shift in cybersecurity: sophisticated persistent operations no longer require a room full of specialized hackers. Armed with commercial American AI models, a small team of Russian operators can orchestrate complex global campaigns at speeds traditional security signatures were never designed to stop.

Beyond Russian agents, Anthropic detected groups from China and Yemen attempting to use Claude to design conventional weapons, efforts the company said it thwarted.

The report also detailed how foreign groups used Claude in elaborate data theft and disinformation campaigns. The document mentions its tools were used to search for target information, find vulnerabilities in their online accounts, create malware designed to bypass security systems and steal data, cover tracks, and spread disinformation.

"We saw actor groups using Claude to build social media profile networks and entire news sites," the report states.

Anthropic also announced it blocked scientists using Claude models in ways that could support biological weapons development.
