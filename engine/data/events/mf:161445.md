On August 25, 1991, a 21-year-old University of Helsinki student posted a modest note to the Usenet group comp.os.minix . “I’m doing a (free) operating system (just a hobby, won’t be big and professional like gnu) for 386(486) AT clones,” Linus Torvalds wrote. The project had been brewing since April, he explained, and was beginning to take shape. Torvalds believed he was creating something enjoyable for himself not the foundation of digital infrastructure used by billions of people. Thirty-five years later, Linux runs beneath much of the modern world: the internet, public clouds, Android devices, high-performance computing clusters, enterprise data centers, embedded systems, and national-scale critical infrastructure. Linux Turns 35 as Open-Source Kernel Powers Torvalds initially considered naming the project “Freax,” a blend of free, freak, and Unix. But when a colleague uploaded the first public source tree, the directory was named Linux. The name stuck. The first public release followed in September 1991. From roughly 10,000 lines of code, the Linux kernel has expanded to more than 40 million lines in the source tree. Its development model also evolved far beyond a student project, now involving hundreds of maintainers and thousands of contributors across hardware vendors, cloud providers, universities, security teams, and open-source communities.
