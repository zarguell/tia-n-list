Cybercriminals serve malware from hacked websites: details of the Psychedelic Stealer campaign

Arctic Wolf Labs has published an analysis of a campaign distributing an infostealer tracked as Psychedelic Stealer. Instead of spoofing popular Ukrainian business and medical sites, the operators looked for vulnerabilities in legitimate, trusted websites and took them over, then injected a hidden iframe into the page code that imitates the Cloudflare CAPTCHA verification mechanism. The fake prompt copies the real thing closely, including a randomly generated Ray ID and a visitor identifier in the footer.

When a visitor clicks the "I am human" checkbox, the page walks them through a ClickFix sequence: a Win+R Run dialog, a Ctrl+V paste of a command that was silently placed on the clipboard when the fake CAPTCHA appeared, and Enter, which triggers the download of an MSI package that installs an executable named psychedeliclove.exe. That executable is the infostealer.

Psychedelic Stealer steals passwords saved in Chrome, Opera, Edge, Brave, Vivaldi, and Yandex, but not Firefox, along with session tokens and data from cryptocurrency wallets including Exodus, Atomic Wallet, Electrum, Bitcoin Core, and Litecoin Core. Beyond one-time theft it installs browser components, sets up a service that messages its command and control server, can kill browser processes, unpacks extension archives directly into browser profiles, and creates a scheduled task named psychedelicloveUtils for persistence. It also profiles the host, including antivirus presence and installed browsers, and polls the C2 server for further tasks.

Researchers found a management panel named "РУБЛЁВКА TDS", a reference to the exclusive Moscow suburb Rublyovka, which the operators use to centrally manage the Windows Installer commands delivered from the hacked sites and to track the funnel: page opens, CAPTCHA clicks, and presses of the Done button. The domain uasputnik[.]com was registered on September 9, updated three and a half hours later, and its URLs were active September 12 and 13, so the whole operation from domain registration to observed malware distribution took under a week. Although the fake pages display Ukrainian text, the HTML contains Russian comments and declares lang="ru", suggesting Russian-speaking actors. The technique is notable because the attacked addresses were fully legitimate and familiar to victims, defeating the usual advice of verifying the URL before entering data or downloading anything.
