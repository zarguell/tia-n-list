A website asks you to click “Allow” to start a quiz or read an interesting story. A little later, your device starts warning you about viruses, missed payments, or legal trouble. While every message may look like a new security problem, the cause could be a permission you granted earlier. For scammers, browser push notifications turn a passing visit into something much more valuable: access to your attention after you have left their website. Two investigations—including our own research into deceptive quiz sites and HUMAN Security’s investigation of an operation called Pushpaganda —show how that permission can become a tool for repeated advertising, scareware, and fraud. Ordinarily, a deceptive website has a limited opportunity to influence you. Once you leave, its sales pitch disappears. A simple browser push notification permission changes that relationship. A website you’ve authorized can send browser notifications even when you’re not visiting it. Depending on the platform and browser configuration, those messages can appear even when the browser is not visibly open. Just what scammers need That gives scammers several advantages: Repeat opportunities to get a click. Instead of relying on one visit, they can send more messages to bring you back to their sites. A more convincing place to display a lie.
