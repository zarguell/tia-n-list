This essay originally appeared as part of a round table in The Free Press entitled “Is America’s Infrastructure Under Attack?” Microsoft, the FAA, banks, United Airlines, the NSA—it’s been a really bad week in cyberspace. But, amazing as it might seem, these are not related. As far as we know, they’re not the result of cyberattacks. They’re not even related to AI. (Well, that’s mostly true. The NSA will probably never tell us what went wrong.) It’s really just a series of unfortunate coincidences. Our computerized infrastructure is vulnerable and fragile. Much of it is old, and has been incrementally patched and upgraded. A surprising amount of it is maintained by hobbyists, without any recompense. And more and more of it is centralized: the same systems affecting many different aspects of our lives and society. In many cases, it just barely works. And when it fails, it tends to fail badly. There are a lot of technological reasons why this is true, but they are all downstream of the economic ones. The technologies we rely on are built and maintained within our economic system—and that’s where the core problem lies. Security, safety, resilience, redundancy, recoverability—these are all essential parts of reliable technology, but they are all costs. More importantly, they are inefficient: costs that feel wasteful until something bad happens.
