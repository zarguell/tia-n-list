CVE: The story of how two engineers got tired of chaos and created an identifier that linked all the world's vulnerabilities

One vulnerability, a dozen names, 321 records, and a booth where competitors agreed to "get along." The history of the most well-known identifier in cybersecurity, based on primary sources from 1999.

In the late 1990s, one NFS vulnerability appeared under different names across three scanner databases: "NFS file guessing check" in CyberCop, "nfs-guess" in X-Force (ISS), and "SunOS NFS Jumbo and fsirand patches" in CERT advisories. The PHF CGI vulnerability was known under roughly a dozen different names simultaneously. Correlating N independent databases required N(N-1)/2 integrations.

On January 8, 1999, two MITRE engineers, David E. Mann and Steven M. Christey, published "Towards a Common Enumeration of Vulnerabilities," proposing a minimal model: a unique name and text description sufficient to distinguish records. No taxonomy, no risk scoring, just agreement that this record and that record describe the same problem. The name format was deliberately boring: CVE-YYYY-NNNN.

The program launched publicly on September 29, 1999 with 321 records and a 19-organization Editorial Board including competitors like IBM, Cisco, ISS, SANS, and even the Ballistic Missile Defense Organization. By September 5, 2026, the CVE list contained 380,563 published vulnerabilities, with 537 CNA organizations across 43 countries.

The article traces the evolution from the initial 1999 document through the 2014 format expansion (when 4-digit sequence numbers proved insufficient), the 2016 CNA scaling strategy, the April 2025 funding scare when MITRE's contract was set to expire, and the creation of the CVE Foundation. The author notes that while CVE remains the primary bridge identifier, it did not eliminate duplication: PYSEC, RUSTSEC, GHSA, Russia's BDU FSTEC, and others maintain their own databases, most of which cross-reference CVE.
