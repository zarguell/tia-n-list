The password spreadsheet your admins use may look like a complete inventory, but it only reflects some of the credentials employees have reported using. More specifically, that spreadsheet doesn’t show passwords that have been changed, created, or shared since the document was last updated, until someone brings it up to date. That’s assuming someone ever does. Meanwhile, work passwords can end up in all kinds of places. Someone saves one in their browser. A teammate sends a shared login in chat to move forward with a project. Another employee decides to put a few passwords in a personal vault because getting access to the company one takes too long. Unless someone reports these actions, the spreadsheet won’t capture them. And so, admins may think they have a handle on company credentials when, in fact, their spreadsheet tells only part of the story. The predominant factor is convenience. When the approved process is slow or unclear, employees use what’s at hand: a browser’s built-in password storage, a shared Google document, a Slack message, or one of the sticky notes on their desk. They may know that that’s against the rules, but in the moment, their priority is getting work done. That’s why a process that relies on employees to manually report every new password, update, and shared credential is nearly impossible to sustain.
