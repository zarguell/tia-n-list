An attacker with a working credential may find the system already open to them. That is the uncomfortable reality behind the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency’s (CISA) new strategy , “CISA 2030: Defend, Harden, Prevail.” The strategy prioritizes operational technology security, software supply chain integrity and continuity of essential services. It also acknowledges that strong defenses cannot guarantee that intrusions will stop. Federal agencies face these dependencies in building controls, research facilities and other operational systems. A compromised contractor credential or unverified update can turn an infrastructure problem into a mission disruption. How much infrastructure still trusts a device, software update or machine identity simply because it was approved once? Meeting CISA’s goals requires examining those approvals, including those no one remembers granting. Yesterday’s approval becomes today’s exposure A controller can stay in service for decades while its firmware, credentials, suppliers and connections change around it. A maintenance contractor leaves. A remote connection outlives its purpose. The hardware remains in place. The original reason for trusting it may no longer hold. An inventory is a starting point. Knowing a controller exists says little about whether its software is authentic, who can change its configuration or which systems depend on its credentials.
