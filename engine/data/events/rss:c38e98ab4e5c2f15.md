Artificial intelligence models now find software vulnerabilities faster than organizations can fix them. In HackerOne’s platform data, the valid report backlog is up 131% over the last two years, and critical and high-severity findings are rising while remediation falls further behind. Finding flaws is no longer the hard part. The harder question will decide the balance between offense and defense in the age of superintelligence: When someone outside an organization finds a flaw in a system Americans depend on, is anyone on the other end listening? The Task Force on Super Intelligence is about to give that question an official answer, whether it means to or not. Its charter directs it to commission a report on the impacts of superintelligence on “the offense-defense balance in cybersecurity.” It also requires the task force to assess the preparedness of the government and the private sector across healthcare, communications, banking, energy, water, transportation and emergency services. Both assessments will shape federal policy for years. If they leave out third-party testing and disclosure, they will start from an incomplete picture. Recognizing third-party evaluation and disclosure Any honest assessment of the offense-defense balance must ask how quickly a defender learns about its own weaknesses.
