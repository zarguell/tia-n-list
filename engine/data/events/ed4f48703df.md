The Supreme Court ruled 6-3 that law enforcement collection of phone location data through **geofence warrants** constitutes a Fourth Amendment search, extending privacy protections to the digital age. The decision in *Chatrie v. United States* is described as a "major win" for digital privacy with "seismic" implications for surveillance doctrine.

Justice Elena Kagan, writing for the majority: "A new technology should not transform what individuals had reasonably thought they could withhold from the Government. An individual has a reasonable expectation of privacy in records about his cell phone's location." Justice Gorsuch wrote separately, arguing location history data qualifies as "personal property" under the Fourth Amendment's language.

While the ruling did not determine whether the specific warrant in Chatrie's bank robbery case was proper, the holding that geofence data collection triggers constitutional protections will constrain future police surveillance programs and limit "reverse search" warrants for other sensitive datasets held by tech companies.
