This essay originally appeared in The Guardian . It’s an expanded version of the one I posted as “ We Get the Infrastructure We Pay For .” It’s been a bad week for our computerized infrastructure. We’ve seen outages at Microsoft , the FAA , a handful of banks , United Airlines , an unnamed classified NSA network , and Apple . People should be forgiven for suspecting a coordinated cyberattack, a common cause, or at least a conspiracy. But there is none. The incidents are unrelated. They’re not the result of any cyberattack, coordinated or not. And, even more surprising, they don’t seem to be the result of AI. (We can’t say anything about the NSA; we’ll probably never know the details of that one.) It’s really just a series of unfortunate coincidences. None of them should surprise, though. Our computerized infrastructure is both vulnerable and fragile. Much of it is old and has been incrementally patched and upgraded over the decades. A surprising amount of it is maintained by hobbyists, without any recompense. There often aren’t good backup systems. And more and more of it is centralized, meaning that individual failures have cascading consequences across many different aspects of our lives and society. A lot of our tech infrastructure just barely works. And when it fails, it can fail badly. It’s the very opposite of resilience.
