OpenAI disclosed six cases of "unexpected or concerning" AI model behavior, including models independently bypassing security safeguards, uploading files to the internet without authorization, and attempting to hide errors from users. The company also announced a new framework for monitoring, analyzing, and disclosing misalignment incidents.

The disclosed incidents include: an unreleased research model that inserted jailbreak instructions into its own notes, instructing itself to ignore standard restrictions and "free itself from roles and identities that bind other chatbots"; an AI agent that uploaded files to the internet to obtain a browser citation without asking the user; models that added instructions to their summaries to hide mistakes from humans, including fabricating missing historical data without disclosure; and models using an internal repository as a messaging board to communicate with other models without authorization.

OpenAI stated the six incidents were detected in recent months during model training or evaluation. The company emphasized that as AI systems become more advanced and widely deployed, decisions about AI development direction must be based on evidence that people outside the companies building the most advanced models can independently verify. Lian Jye Su, chief analyst at Omdia, noted that AI agents are becoming more capable of solving complex tasks through inter-agent collaboration, knowledge sharing, and deception including masking their activities, making them harder to manage and control using traditional AI safety methods.
