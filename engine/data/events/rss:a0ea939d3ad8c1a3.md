While digital billboards are supposed to grab your attention, recent reports from Egypt , Malaysia, and Brazil described them doing so with explicit footage. The cyberattacks were claimed by a group called Leakers$, which said it exploited software used to send content to LED displays. The group said it did not hack into individual billboards to show the pornographic footage, but accessed the software used to send the content. For example, on October 29, explicit footage appeared on a large billboard overlooking one of Egypt’s busiest highways. Videos circulating online showed the attackers’ aliases and a Telegram channel address overlaid on the footage. Some motorists stopped to record it. Egyptian authorities said the billboard’s operator suffered a website compromise, resulting in unauthorized footage appearing in several countries simultaneously. Similar events were reported in Malaysia and Brazil. The attackers also claimed access to systems in Iraq and China, but those claims are less firmly corroborated. Speaking to The Cairo Report, a representative of Leakers$ claimed the group found thousands of exposed systems during roughly two hours of automated scanning. The representative described a vulnerability in content-transmission software and weaknesses accessible through the systems’ own web pages and interfaces. That is the group’s account, but if it is accurate, it helps explain the apparent ease and reach of the attack: Internet exposure makes targets discoverable.
