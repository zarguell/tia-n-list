Your family will one day want your photos. They may not be able to get them. For a considerable portion of our shared history, whenever people have died, they have left behind physical remains. There are the photo albums, and jewelry, and clothes, even cars. And as surviving loved ones were forced to puzzle out who keeps what, much of the work—at the very least—was tangible. Death today is different. It’s digital. Photo “albums” are increasingly stored on smartphones and handwritten letters have become a series of texts (never mind the loss of cursive), while social media posts, streaming playlists, and shared online documents have developed a sometimes-equal feeling of sentimentality. But digital remains can be thorny to preserve. For starters, they’re often managed by companies, not by people. Further, there’s a whole lot more digital remains than most people can organize. In fact, according to Jed Brubaker, founder of the Digital Legacy Clinic at the University of Colorado Boulder, the average American internet user has 190 online accounts to their name.
