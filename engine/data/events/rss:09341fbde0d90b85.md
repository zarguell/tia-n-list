Scammers are finding different ways to use personal details exposed in data breaches to lure people into conversations. Last week , we showed how a Facebook Marketplace listing used the recipient’s name to make a text asking “Is this still available?” more convincing. The one we got this week was a lot simpler. No suspicious link. No overdue payment. No warning that your account is about to be closed. No fake images. Just your name, followed by a question mark. It’s a remarkably small opening for what could become a much bigger problem. A message like this invites an almost automatic response: “Yes?” Or perhaps, “Who’s this?” That’s because a scammer needs to get you to engage before they can persuade you to hand over money or personal information . Sometimes, the first thing they ask you to give them is simply a reply. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has warned about similarly brief, apparently harmless messages that scammers use to start conversations. The text in our example gives the recipient very little information, but leaves an obvious question unanswered: Who wants to know? Using the correct name makes that question more compelling. This is not just a generic “Hello” that could have gone to anyone. It looks as though the sender is trying to reach you specifically. It’s tempting to think, “They have my name and number, so this must be someone I know.” But having those details is not evidence they know you.
