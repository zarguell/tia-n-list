Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN under fire from an APT group. Cybercriminals use the CVE-2026-20127 flaw to take over devices.

Security researchers from Cisco Talos published a detailed analysis of an APT group tracked as UAT-11473. The attackers actively used the security flaw CVE-2026-20127 in Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN controllers to distribute KeyDrift malware.

TLDR: Cisco Talos researchers published an analysis of the new APT group tracked as UAT-11473. Cybercriminals exploit the critical CVE-2026-20127 vulnerability, rated 10.0 on the CVSS v3.0 scale, to take over Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN controllers. On the compromised devices they install their own in-house tool, KeyDrift malware, which they use to communicate with a C2 server, maintain persistence and exfiltrate data, including the keys that encrypt IPSec tunnels. There is no effective workaround for the flaw. The only remedy is installing the latest patches. Although the vulnerability was fixed in February 2026, not every administrator applied the updates in time, and it took the attackers only a short window to exploit the flaw and break into the targeted environments.

CVE-2026-20127 is a critical vulnerability rated 10.0 in CVSS v3.0. The flaw lies in faulty handling of the authentication mechanism for peering connections. By sending a crafted request, an attacker could bypass the authentication process and log in to a highly privileged account, such as v daemon. That grants access to the NETCONF interface, which in turn allows manipulation of the network configuration of the entire SD-WAN architecture. The attack is fully remote and requires no privileges on the system. The affected products were Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Controller (formerly SD-WAN vSmart), Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Manager (formerly SD-WAN vManage) and Cisco Catalyst SD-WAN Validator (formerly SD-WAN vBond). There is no effective way around the problem other than installing the official patches. Until the infrastructure can be updated, the vendor recommends temporary measures to limit the potential impact of an attack, sourced from sec.cloudapps.cisco.com and the Firewall Ports for Cisco SD-WAN Deployations documentation.

After gaining access to the targeted infrastructure, the criminals installed KeyDrift. It is advanced malware that runs inside SD-WAN system processes and is mainly tasked with data exfiltration. It consists of two modules, PolyFlame and LittleFilch. The attackers went to great lengths to hide their activity. For that reason, loading the individual KeyDrift components was not done through simple background installation scripts. Instead, the loader scanned active processes for vdaemon and temporarily stored their PIDs in the file /tmp/system-vdaemon. It then launched its own rsshd component, imitating the name of the SSH service, in order to inject the malicious library libdssl[.]so, containing the PolyFlame module, directly into memory. To avoid detection, the loader used Linux system calls directly (ptrace, fork, wait4, mmap, mprotect). It also checked the TracerPid value in /proc/%d/status. If it detected an analysis environment such as a debugger or a sandbox, it halted execution.
